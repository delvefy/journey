The Guttering Shrine

East of the antechamber the light changes its mind. You come into a low chapel of a room — or a workshop for something that is not quite worship — and the torch in its bracket burns amber, but the flame leans, always, toward the same corner, as though some slow breath you cannot feel were drawing it. Your shadow falls a half-beat late behind you, and settles wrong.

On the lintel of the door ahead someone has drawn a spiral in candle-soot, winding inward, and pressed a fingertip to its centre so the black smudged. A charm, perhaps, or only a habit dressed as one. You find your own hand rising to touch it before you have decided to, and you make yourself let it fall.

The bell is here again — not in the air but behind your eyes, a single sounded note that goes on counting an hour with no number to it. It was at your back in the room before; here it is all around you, patient, as if it had been waiting only for you to come a little closer.

Behind you, to the west, the plain rushlight of the antechamber still burns, honest and dull. Ahead to the east the air thickens and seems to listen, and you feel almost certain that if you spoke a name there — even one you do not have — something would answer. To the north the bent light steadies into something clearer, caught and cool. To the south the spell goes to root and green, older and wilder, less spoken than grown. You were going somewhere, once. The charm on the door does not say where.
