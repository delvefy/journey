The Ochre Hollow

The clay gives way to a wider dark, and the dark is not empty of pictures. You come in low, on your hands for a moment, and rise into a hollow whose walls have been touched — smeared, daubed, pressed — with ochre and soot and the red of old iron. Beasts run across the stone in the failing light: long-backed, horned, fleeting, drawn by someone who had no word for what they were but needed badly to keep them.

Among them, at the height of a reaching arm, is a hand — a print in red, fingers spread, blown round with pigment so the stone holds the ghost of a palm. You raise your own without deciding to, and it fits. Above it a spiral has been wound in ochre, the coil once more, the only mark here that is neither beast nor hand. The painted eyes catch what little light there is and seem to follow, and the dark behind the wall leans in to listen, as though the pictures were a question the stone had been holding for you.

No drip, no glass, no throat — only, when you go still, a soft knocking deep in the rock, slow and double, the heartbeat from the room above heard now through stone, counting you in.

To the north the wood thickens and the painted beasts grow eyes that blink, wonder waking out of the wall. To the west the pictures fail and the clay only listens, the unstruck flint and the colder count. To the south the images thin to the bare body and the dark before any hand thought to mark it. To the east the beasts step half out of the stone, the wild taking shape and breath. You were going somewhere, and someone drew the way and left no word for it.
