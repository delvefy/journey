The Green Chancel

Here the earth wakes up. The loam of the room before turns from dark to living: moss furs the walls in green light, pale flowers open and close as though dreaming, and a tree whose root you cannot find has pushed a single branch through the stone overhead, in full leaf, though there is no sky to leaf toward. The air is warm and breathing still, but it is awake now, attentive, and it bends toward you the way the torchflame bent in the shrine — only here nothing is lit, nothing is made; the bending is the thing itself.

A young fern stands uncurling from the floor, its tip wound in a tight green spiral, and as you watch it moves — too slow to see, and yet between two blinks it is further open. You put out a hand and it leans into your palm like a creature. The magic here is not spoken, not drawn in soot; it is grown, older and wilder than any charm, and it knows you — or knows something you used to be.

A note sounds: not iron, not glass, but a throat, a single low animal call that keeps the time the bell once kept.

West, the loam darkens back toward root and sleep. North, the green thins into the leaning, half-spoken charm-light you came down from. To the south the wood deepens into older wonder, things with eyes. To the east the wakefulness sharpens to a presence, as if the room beyond were not empty of someone. You were going somewhere. The fern, uncurling, seems to point the way and then to forget it.
