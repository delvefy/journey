The Everlamp

You climb, and the light stops being caught and begins to be kept. The room is small and clean-walled, and at its centre a lamp burns with no oil you can find and no flame that gutters — a steady, sourceless white, brighter and cooler than any rushlight, the made cousin of the glass-caught glow you carried up from below. It does not lean or flicker. It simply is, and goes on being, as though someone solved the trick of light and walked away.

Before the lamp hangs a single disc of worked glass, and the light passing through it throws a spiral onto the wall — the coil again, but cast in brightness now, turning, slow, one patient revolution that you somehow know to be an hour. It is the water-clock's climbing scale set free of its water, time told in light. You watch the bright spiral turn a quarter, and feel, faintly and unaccountably, watched back.

The held glass-note is still here, thinner now, riding above the lamp like a wire drawn taut — the bell pulled to a single sustained line that the turning light seems to measure out.

Below, to the south, the glass that merely caught the light, work of the patient hand. To the west the same hour kept in falling water, plainer and colder. To the north the light grows fiercer and surer, lamps in their hundreds, a made day with no night in it. To the east the turning glass begins to mean something, the spiral spinning as though it had somewhere to be. You were going somewhere, by some light. The lamp burns on and asks you nothing, yet.
