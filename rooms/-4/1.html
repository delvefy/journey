The Iron Mile

North of the marker the road got iron, and the iron got the Waste. The weathered waymark below has been recast here in metal: an iron post stands in the open grey, and beyond it another, and another, a line of them marching off level into the dimness — mileposts, or the stumps of a fence, or the last of a rail laid across ground that no longer goes anywhere. Each is numbered, a figure raised in the casting to count the distance, and each counts it faithfully, and the distance means nothing; the tally runs out into the grey toward no place at all.

The iron is doing here what the bench of tools a step east only began. The posts are scaled orange, flaking at every edge, shedding a brown grit the wind takes off level with the ground — the made cold blowing away as ore. On the nearest post a maker's mark is cast: the spiral, raised proud once, eaten hollow now, the coil rusted through from the centre out until it is more wound than mark. Below it a legend, a name or a make, scaled over past reading; you thumb it and the rust comes away like pollen, and the letters do not.

Strike the post and it gives one flat dead note, the bell remade as a tool's complaint and worn even past that; and then, faint, the small tick of cold iron settling lower into itself, surrendering a warmth it cannot recall having, counting nothing toward no one.

To the north the posts rise into rail and engine and a cold even hum, the works of the disenchanted age driven out across the Waste. To the east the line gathers back to a bench of cold hand-tools, the iron still worked, the trade not yet wholly blown away. To the south the iron softens back to a stone waymark on a faint road, hand-set and weathered, a way that no longer goes. To the west the posts thin out and the last of them blows to ore-grit, the open grey with nothing made left standing. You were going somewhere, by some count of miles. The posts count them still, off into the grey, every number true and not one of them the way.
