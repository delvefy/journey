The Unhaunted

West of the shrine there is no shrine. The walls do not even fall here; they were never raised. The cut stone that carried a voice gives way to plain open country — a grey ordinary day over grey ordinary ground, a cart-rut, a fence, the long flat distance the keeping finally fell into. Nothing is ruined. That is the strangest part of it. There is nothing here that was ever lost, because there is nothing here that remembers having had it.

You look, out of habit, for the coil — graven, scored, scratched out, anything — and you do find it, but it has come down in the world. It is a whorl in the grain of the fence-rail under your hand, the curl of a dead leaf, the turn the rut makes around a stone: a shape that happens, and means nothing, and is read by no one as a mark. No hand cut it to hold something. No hand scored it out in doubt. It was never asked to mean, and so it never had to stop meaning. The spiral is safe here only because here it is nothing at all.

Above the shrine a ropeless bell at least kept the shape of its ringing, a silence cut to the size of a sound. Here there is not even that. A wind moves across the flat country and a gate knocks somewhere far off, plain sounds that were never a bell and make no claim to be one, keeping no hour, answering no stroke. You wait for the small leaning-close, the ear in the quiet that the stone refused you a step east. Here there is no quiet to lean in, only distance, and the distance does not know it was ever listened to.

To the east the country firms back toward the shrine, stone that still remembers being knelt in, a charm still faintly doubted. To the north the plain ground squares into a made and certain place, iron and engine, a world too busy to have ever believed. To the south the open country thins toward bare hearth and older dark, the ordinary day giving out into the deep. To the west the distance only lengthens, the forgotten country going on past where anyone could come back, the place where names die and nothing recalls they were ever names. You were going somewhere once, under a name, perhaps to pray at the end of it. The flat country takes the name off you as easily as the wind takes the gate-knock, carries neither anywhere, and forgets them both.
