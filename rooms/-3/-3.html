Bush

Lower than a tree and thicker than the grass, the bush hunches in the hedgerow, a tangle of woody stems and small green leaves. No single trunk - it springs up in a dozen shoots at once, dense enough to hide a nesting bird or a whole rabbit. Some bushes give berries, some give thorns, and all of them give shelter. A plain, natural thing, rooted and round, it marks the edge of the garden and the start of the wild.

North

I am soft green velvet, cool and damp,
on shaded stones I make my camp;
no flower, no stem, I spread out slow,
where it is dark and dampness flows.

East

I was a tree, now felled and round,
I lie in lengths upon the ground;
you sit on me, you split me too,
and burn me when the fire's due.

South

I am a nut in a little cap,
I drop from oaks into your lap;
squirrels hoard me, bury me deep -
plant me, and a great oak you'll reap.

West

I stretch out from the sturdy trunk,
I hold the leaves and twigs and bunk
of nesting birds up in the air -
a wooden arm the trees all wear.
