The Antechamber

You wake on cold flagstones with no name to call your own. The room is square and close, walled in grey stone gone slick with damp, and a single rushlight gutters in an iron bracket. By its uneven flame you count four doors — one set dead in the middle of each wall, every one of them shut.

You remember nothing. Not how you came to be here, not your own face, not the shape of the word that ought to be your name. Only a faint, insistent certainty that you were going somewhere, and that the going is not yet finished.

There are signs to read, if you will read them. Beneath the north door slips a thread of air that tastes faintly of cold metal, and with it the thinnest hairline of light — too straight, too even, lit by no flame you know. From under the east door comes amber torchlight that seems to bend where it ought to fall straight, and a bell, though you hear it less in your ear than somewhere behind your eyes, counting an hour that has no number. The south door is warm to the touch, almost as though it breathes; the smell that seeps beneath it is wet earth and green rot, older than any made thing. The west door is cold and silent, and carries a dry breath of ash; gouged into it at eye height is a single mark — a spiral, cut deep and then half scratched out again, by some earlier hand. Yours, perhaps. You cannot say.

On the flagstone where your head had lain, the same spiral has been chalked, half smudged by your waking. A reminder. A warning. A direction. Choose a door. The journey only goes forward — even when it circles.
