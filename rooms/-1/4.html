The Dead Letter

North of the stamping the cold word leaves the page for good. What the date-press only filed below, this room flings: a long cold exchange of brass and wire, registers clicking over of themselves, reels feeding tape out faster than the press ever stamped, the sending quickened past paper onto wire and onto light — and not one line of it meant for a reader. The room is lit by the same sourceless white as the filed hour, and into that white the messages pour out and pour out, sent, logged, filed, and never once read.

The sense has been stamped clean out of it. You lift a length of the tape from where it heaps and uncoils on the floor — the spiral again, a loose bright coil printed the whole of its length with one entry: a name, perhaps yours, set perfectly and meaning nothing, run off and sent and come back undeliverable, a thousand true copies in the dead-letter heap. No one here to take it; no one anywhere the wire reaches. The exchange needs no reader. It was built to send, and it sends, and the sending is the whole of it.

A bell rings somewhere down the registers — the small clerkish ting of a printer calling an operator who never sat down, the bell filed along with the rest, ringing the hour onto a roll instead of telling it to anyone, even and empty as the perfected clock.

To the north the exchange sheds even its tape, the word flung out as pure cold light and number for no reader at all, the made day of the dead pole. To the east the wire warms by a hair toward an honest sending, a word that still half-means, a name that might yet reach. To the south the reels slow back to the date-press and its racks, the cold word returned to paper and believed by no one. To the west the exchange runs down, the wires slacken, the records crack and yellow toward the long grey silence, the Waste that signal comes to. You were going somewhere, and word was sent. It uncurls down the tape on the floor, sent and sent and returned, and not one perfect copy of it is ever read.
