The Cinder Hollow

The breathing stops. You cross into a place where the warm green lung of the earth has gone out entirely, and what is left is ash — deep, grey, cold ash, drifted into the corners of a room with no light and no warmth and no sound but the faint hiss of cinder settling under your own weight. It is the dead end of two directions at once: the magic scratched out of the world to the north, the life burned out of it to the east, meeting here in a hollow that holds neither.

Something pale lies half-buried in the drift — bone, you think, a curve of it — and beside it, scratched into the ash by a finger long gone, the spiral, almost erased now, just the ghost of a turning. You crouch. The ash is cold to the wrist. You could draw the spiral whole again with a fingertip. You find that you do not have the heart for it, here, and the not-having frightens you more than the dark does.

No bell. No drip. No throat. Time does not trouble to be counted in a place that is already over.

To the east the ash warms by a single degree toward the sleeping loam. To the north it pales toward the scoured grey stone and its half-unmade mark. To the south the cold deepens, older than fire, down toward the body and the dark. Westward there is only more ash, and the long after. You were going somewhere, once. Here it is hard to believe anyone ever went anywhere at all.
