The Loam Below

You go down, though the floor barely slopes. The fitted stone gives way to packed earth, and the walls turn soft and root-threaded, weeping a little at the seams. The air is warm here, and damp, and it moves — in and out, slow, so that the whole low room seems to breathe around you. This was the warmth that pressed against the door above; you have walked into the lung of it.

There is no flame. The light comes greenish and sourceless, the way it does under leaves, and your eyes take a long time to trust it. Underfoot, half-sunk in the loam, a shell lies coiled in a tight spiral, ridged and patient, older than any tool, older than the hand that might have set it there. You crouch and almost lift it, and something in the gesture feels rehearsed — remembered from before the room you woke in.

A slow drip somewhere keeps a wet and unhurried count. Not iron, not the bell behind the eyes; only water finding water, the oldest clock there is.

Above you, to the north, the door still holds the dry rushlit warmth of the antechamber, the made world you came from. To the south the earth deepens and the dark grows older, down toward something from before fire. To the east the green takes on a brightness, a wakefulness, as though the loam itself might answer if you asked it. To the west the smell sours to ash and cold, and the breathing stops. You were going somewhere. The shell remembers only how to spiral inward.
