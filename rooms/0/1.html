The Wheelwright's Cell

You step through into a colder air, and the warmth of the flagstones falls away behind you. This room is made, where the last was only walled: the grey stone here is fitted and squared, and a long bench runs beneath the far wall, its surface scarred by years of patient work you cannot remember doing. Iron tools hang in order on their pegs — gouges, a drawknife, a hammer worn to the shape of a hand. The air tastes faintly of metal, of cold filings, and you understand now what it was that seeped beneath the door behind you.

Against the wall stands a great wheel, taller than you and half-built, its spokes set into the hub like the rays of something and its rim not yet closed. A curl of pale shaving has fallen from it to the floor and lies there coiled tight as a spiral, and the sight of it tugs at you — as though you had cut that curl yourself, in a life you set down somewhere and cannot find.

Somewhere a slow drip falls into standing water, counting. Not the bell from the room before; a plainer time, measured out in droplets.

The door at your back glows still with the rushlight you left. To the north, a hairline of light leaks in too straight and too pale to be any flame, and the cold comes off it. To the east, something glints where it should not, a wink of caught colour in the grey. To the west, the metal taste sharpens, exact and joyless. You were going somewhere. The wheel is not yet finished. Neither, it seems, are you.
