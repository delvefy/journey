The Flint Hollow

You go down again, and the slow breath of the loam thins out behind you. The walls are no longer root and weeping soil but raw clay and colder stone, and the green light that lit the room above dwindles to the barest stain, the colour of water seen through ice. The damp warmth eases off a degree, then another. This is older ground, and it does not breathe.

Set into the clay at the height of your knee waits a nodule of dark flint, glassy along one edge where something has struck it, or where something might. Your thumb finds that edge and rests, and the gesture is worn smooth with use you cannot remember — a hand that knew, in a life with no name, what the stone was for. Beside it lies a curl of yellowed bone, and pressed into the soft clay a spiral no wider than a thumbprint: not chalked, not gouged, but coiled there by a finger. You did not make it. You cannot swear you did not.

The patient drip of water is gone. In the close dark there is only your own pulse, loud against the clay, keeping a count older than any drop — the first clock, the one you were already counting when you woke.

To the north the door breathes back the green and the wet, the made world rising toward rushlight and stone. To the east the clay seems to lean in and listen, warm where it should be cold, as though the dark might answer a question you have not learned to ask. To the south the cold thickens toward a place that has never held a flame — only the body, and the long dark before it. To the west the air goes dry and ashen, a silence where even the spiral would not keep its shape. The flint waits under your thumb. You were going somewhere, and the going is not finished.
