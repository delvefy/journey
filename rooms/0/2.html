The Clepsydra

The drip you heard below has become a discipline. This room is taller and cooler, and the light that fills it is the pale even light that leaked under the door — you see its source at last, and it is no flame: a tall slot in the wall, grey daylight or something built to pass for it, falling perfectly straight onto the instrument that stands at the room's centre.

It is a vessel of clear-glazed clay and copper, water sliding from an upper bowl to a lower through a throat no wider than a reed. A float rides the rising water; a copper pointer climbs a scale etched up the wall — a spiral scale, you see now, its marks winding upward turn on turn, gathering the hours into a coil. The slow water-clock has been taught to keep a number. Where the antechamber's bell only tolled, this one measures.

You watch the pointer climb a single fine mark, and the small click it makes is the bell again, tamed and divided and made exact. The cold comes off the straight light in a thin and steady breath.

To the south, the warm wood and iron of the workroom, where the counting was only a careless drip. To the north the made world deepens — the steady light promises lamps that never gutter, presses, engines, the printed word. To the east the measuring takes on a stranger cast, time half-enchanted. To the west it goes sterile and pure, mechanism with the soul filed out of it. You were going somewhere, by some hour. The spiral counts, and does not say which.
