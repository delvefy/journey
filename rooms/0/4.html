The Wire

North of the press the word leaves the page. The honest thump of the screw has thinned to a faster, finer clatter: a long bench of brass keys, each beneath its own small hammer, tapping the letters not into lead now but into a wire that runs out through the wall and away to somewhere you cannot follow. There is no paper here worth the name — only a thin ribbon of tape unspooling from a drum, pricked with dots and dashes, the whole alphabet boiled down to a stutter of marks the hand can no longer read at the speed it makes them. The word has been taught to travel faster than the eye that meant it.

The tape gathers on the floor in a loose bright coil, the spiral come round again, and you pick up its end and try to read. Down the pricked length, between the weather and the prices and the hours, a name clatters past — yours, perhaps, or one you keep almost having — set true and sent and already gone down the wire before you can lift a hand to answer it. A thousand true copies, none of them stopping for you, all of them on their way to no one you will ever meet.

The clock's old tick has gone electric: a sounder taps in a brass cup on the bench, a clean quick ping where the bell used to toll, counting nothing now, only sending — the hour flung out on the wire instead of struck for anyone in the room.

To the north the wire gives way to light, the word shed of its last body and flung onto a made day of glass and number, the cold bright pole. To the east the sending starts to mean and to arrive, a word that does the thing it says, a name that might reach the one it is meant for. To the south the wire firms back into the press and the patient lead, the word slowed to a weight you can hold. To the west the sending goes cold and clerkish, the tape filed and never read, signal with the sense stamped out of it. You were going somewhere, and word was sent ahead. It ticks past you now in dots, your name, sent and sent and never once to you.
