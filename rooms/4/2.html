The Kindled Host

East of the conjuring engine and north of the shining folk, the glass is gone. No panes here, no frames to call the dead through — the rank on rank of lit glass has thinned away to nothing, and the folk it summoned stand free in the open room, bright and edged and many, needing no surface now to be. Yet they are not loose. They have been built into a working: each shining neighbour set in its place, turned and joined to the next, the whole parish strung into an engine made not of glass but of the kindled dead themselves, load-bearing, humming, the made day raised out of standing light.

You move among them and they carry the work between them like water along a chain. One lays a hand of light to the shoulder of the next and a brightness passes down the line; a shape firms, dims, firms again, the labour of the made day done now by the folk who are its only engine. And the name goes round with the brightness — not fogged backward on glass, not said by one lit tongue, but passed hand to hand around the ring of them, each speaking it on toward the next, gaining an edge each time, coming round the curve of the room toward where you stand. You almost have it. The nearest turns, holding it, about to set it in your hand.

The coil of cold light that wound pane to pane runs now through the folk themselves, person to bright person, a ring of them turning slow, the spiral grown into a circle of the standing dead. North the host refines past flesh to pure light and number, the city where the dead are infrastructure of cold fire. East they shed even their arrangement and simply are, no engine left, the pole where the dead outnumber the living and naming is enough. South the light banks back into lamps and frames, the folk lit by craft again. West the open room folds shut into ranks of glass. You were going somewhere, under a name; it comes round the ring toward you, brightening, one bright hand already lifting — and the chord swells and the moment turns past.
