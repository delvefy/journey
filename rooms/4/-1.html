The Rooted Folk

North of here the dead kept a house — a hearth, a doorway, the small manners of neighbours. Here the house has gone back to ground. No roof: root hangs where the rafters were, wet and dark, and the standing shapes of smoke and held breath stand not in any room but in a grown place, among hanging root and packed earth, as if the parish had been dug up and left to take. They are more here than ever, and less tame for it — not called, not penned, not housed, simply grown into the dark the way the root is, always-here and gone wild.

They keep no afternoon now. They have sunk into the place. One stands half out of a knot of root like breath come off a cold pond; two lean together where no doorway is, only earth. When you pass close, one turns — slow, the turn of something rooted — and a word comes up toward you through the ground rather than the air: your name, the name half-yours everywhere, but risen now instead of offered, the syllables pushed up out of the dark like the folk themselves, known here the way the wild knows a returning thing.

The spiral that lay graven in the floor above and worn in the earth to the west is here a coil of living root, the same turn pressed slow into the wet wood of it, a thing grown rather than drawn. And the bell comes up through your soles — no parish of glass tones now but a single low beat carried in the root, felt before heard, the slow heartbeat of the dark keeping a time with no count in it.

To the north the wild ground draws back into walls and a hearth, the folk neighbourly again, fitted to a house. To the east the standing dead thicken into a whole country of the present gone, names traded like weather, the living grown few. To the south the root goes deeper into older dark, before any parish, the always-here older than your name or any name. To the west the crowd thins back to one shape in a ring the earth grew round it. You were going somewhere, under a name. The folk have grown up around your place and kept it, patient as root.
