The Old Tongue

South of the rooted folk and east of the speaking herd, the dark learns to talk. The root that hung wet over the parish goes down here past any house ever kept, into an older dark, the always-here older than your name or than any name — and yet it is not silent, not the mute deep. It is warm, packed close, breathing on every side, and it speaks. Not one horned mouth now but the whole of it: root and hide and the long-dead and the breath of living beasts, no longer told one from another, all grown into a single dark that has every word and lets them out slow on its one long breath.

You stand in the middle of the saying. It comes from no direction because it comes from all of them, from the earth under your soles and the warm flanks pressed unseen against you and the dead leaning where no doorway is. And in the flood of it, said plainly at last, is your name — but the wild does not offer it to you. It says it the way it says everything, one word among the all-words, no more yours than the root's or the beast's, a name from before there was a single mouth to keep it or a single one to own it. You almost have it. It is already gone by in the telling, and the next word, and the next.

The sound curls as it comes, wound in a slow coil through the close dark, the spiral you have met as shell and horn and root grown now into the very turn of the speaking, breath winding out and round. North the talk gathers back into root and a half-kept house, the folk grown tame toward a hearth. East the wild keeps every word and turns them on you, naming you into its own, the pole where the dark is more real than the one who hears it. South the warm thins and the words sink back below any mouth, the first dark that has not learned to speak. West the all-saying narrows to one horned one and a herd, your name almost-said by a single throat. You were going somewhere, under a name; the dark has it, and says it, and keeps none of it for you.
