The Wild Guests

East of the throng and south of the hearth, the door stands open to the wild. The long hall keeps its fire, but the walls have thinned and the door at the end gives not onto a yard but onto the wood, and through it the always-here have come in: the throng you met crowding the dark a step west, solid now, and seated, the old powers taken their places at the household fire as honoured guests. Root and horn and beast are at the hearth among the folk, and no one minds; the dead of the house and the spirits of the wood keep one company, and the fire warms them all the same.

A neighbour-shade names you in words, easy as ever; and across the fire a horned guest lifts its head and names you too, in the old way, the lowing-shaped-to-sense you have heard out in the dark, and the two namings meet over the flames and are the same name. You are known here twice, by the tame and by the wild, the household and the wood both keeping it for you. The spiral is everywhere at once: cut over the lintel as a house-mark, worn into the floor by the throng's old going-round, wound in the horn of the guest at the fire — the one coil the kin-sign and the wild-mark and the maker's-mark have always secretly been, met here at a single hearth.

The house-bell and the throng's drumming keep time together, a soft chime by the door and, under the floor, the low beat in the packed earth — the homely count and the wild count fallen into one warm rhythm, the hour kept by a hall that is also a wood.

To the north the wild withdraws and the hall is only household, the kept hearth, the folk at the fire. To the east the wild and the dead come wholly sovereign, the old powers' own hall, the high Wyld pole where the wood needs no house. To the south the door opens wider onto the wood, the guests grown more beast than folk, the fire ringed with horn and root. To the west the seated guests rise and become a throng again, the always-here crowding the dark outside the door. You were going somewhere, under a name. The house and the wood both have it, and say it together over the fire, tame and wild in one breath — and you sit between them, named twice and holding neither, warm at a hearth where the living and the dead and the wild are all, tonight, at home.
