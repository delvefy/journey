The Name Fair

East of the standing folk, the room opens into a crowd. A step west the dead kept to a house, going quietly about an unseen afternoon; here the walls have given way to a common under a low grey sky, and the dead are not a household but a multitude — a fair-day press of smoke and held breath, more than any town of the living ever held. None summoned, none penned. They throng the open ground trading the only coin there is: names, passed mouth to mouth and freely, the way folk at a fair pass bread and news and small change.

You go among them the stranger, for being alive is the strange thing now. The living are the few here, and the few the dead make room for kindly, as a crowd parts for a child. Names move past on every side, none quite caught — a greeting, a haggle, a name called across the press and answered three rows on. And then, in the trade, your own — the name half-yours everywhere — comes up hand to hand toward you, passed along the line like a coin worked smooth, until the one beside you holds it out. You reach for the change of it.

The spiral is the fair itself: the slow wheeling of the crowd about the common, an eddy of the dead that draws each name in and round and out again, the mark grown from a footpath to the turning of a throng. And the bell is the fair-bell, struck somewhere over their heads, a market's worth of voices taking the hour up and trading it on. North the fair is fitted to craft and light, the thronged dead set running in mechanism. East the common widens into the whole country of the present dead, names the only currency, the pole where to be quick is to be the ghost. South the crowd loosens out of any common into root and dark, the dead less neighbours than the always-here. West the throng thins back to a quiet household of standing folk. You were going somewhere, under a name; it comes down the line of the fair, hand to hand, almost in your palm — and the crowd wheels, and the name is traded one further on.
