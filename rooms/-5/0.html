The Far Country

West of the unhaunted country the distance simply lengthens. A step east there were still the furnishings of a place — a cart-rut, a fence-rail, a gate that knocked far off, the props of an ordinary grey day. Here even those thin out. The rut runs on and is not followed; the fence ends in a post and does not start again; the road, if it is a road, goes grey and straight toward a horizon that comes no nearer for the walking. This is the forgotten country gone long: not ruined, never ruined, only gone on so far past anyone's coming back that the land has quietly stopped expecting to be crossed, and the names of things have begun to fall off them like dust off a hand.

You go to draw the coil and your hand lifts and then forgets the way. The spiral that came down in the world a step east — a whorl in a fence-rail, the turn of a rut — finds here nothing even that idle to settle in: only the flat, and the going. And the strange ease of it is the worst thing in the room. You do not grieve the mark. You cannot quite hold, this far out, why it mattered — the forgetting is gentle, ordinary, like mist taking a field, and you feel your own name loosening in it the way the fence loosened, the way the road did, with no pain at all and no one to mind.

There is no bell, and out here not even a gate to knock in its place; only the long sound a wind makes crossing a country with nothing to cross, a sound that keeps no hour and was never asked to. North the flat squares up toward the made and certain, iron and engine, a world too busy to know it forgot. East the distance firms back to the unhaunted country, charms freshly let go, the shrine remembered a mile on. South the ordinary day thins toward bare hearth and older dark, the country giving out into the deep. West the road goes on toward the dead-magic pole, past all return, where nothing answers and nothing recalls it ever did. You were going somewhere once, under a name, and meant to arrive. The far country takes the name off you as gently as the distance takes the road, carries neither anywhere, and does not notice that you have begun, a little, to be no one at all.
