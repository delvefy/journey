Log

A whole tree, felled and cut to length, lies across the forest floor. The log is the heaviest plain thing in the wood - too thick to be a branch, too solid to snap like a twig. Sit on it and feel the cold damp of the bark, the soft give where the moss and the beetles have moved in. Left long enough it sinks back into the earth that grew it. For now it is a bench, a bridge, a quiet brown weight among the trees.

North

I am the smallest piece of tree,
a little stick that snaps with ease;
birds weave me into nests up high,
I crack beneath your foot - that's I.

East

I have no legs, no eyes, no bones,
I tunnel through the dirt and stones;
the early bird hunts me at dawn,
I'm pink and long across the lawn.

South

When the rain has soaked the ground,
soft and brown I can be found;
boots get stuck and pigs delight,
I squelch and squish with all my might.

West

I am a clump of leafy stems,
too low to be a tree, my friends;
I grow in hedges, round and dense,
with berries, twigs, and thorns for fence.
