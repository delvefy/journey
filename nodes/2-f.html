A Man's Pockets

The road will keep no secrets. The reeds will keep yours, if you are quick.

It is not a decision so much as a recognition. You have nothing. The man has things. The road has riders coming up it at a slow searching pace, and the world is moving and you must move with it.

"Sister," he says, when he sees the change in your face. He says it without surprise, the way a man on a sinking boat says water: simply, naming the thing. His good hand lets go of your wrist. It is not forgiveness. It is only that he has finished asking.

You work fast. Your hands know how to do this, which is the second thing today that has lifted small cold hairs along your arms. You cut the thong of the charm — there is a small knife folded into the lining of your own coat that you had not registered until your fingers reached for it — and the silver fish comes free into your palm with a wetness that you do not pause to examine. You go through what is left of his pockets, which is little; the men who came before you were thorough. You find a single small clay phial, stoppered with wax, no larger than the joint of your thumb. You pocket it without thinking why.

The boots. He is wearing fine boots, fitted, less worn than yours. You consider them for one breath. You do not take them. They are below the body in the mud, and the time it would take to pull them off would not be paid back by the wearing of them. Your own are still good. You leave him his boots, and you do not let yourself say to yourself that this is mercy, because it is not. It is arithmetic.

The coat, the same. To strip the body of its outer layer would mean to roll it, and to roll it would mean to leave clear marks in the mud, and the riders will be at the verge in heartbeats. You leave the coat. You take what is in the coat, which is everything you have learned a coat carries — a small whetstone, a folded scrap of vellum with three lines of writing you cannot read in the half-light, a copper ring with no stone in it, and three more coins. You add them to your purse. The purse, which had hung empty-ish on your hip, settles heavier into the cup of your hip-bone, as if it had been waiting all morning to be useful.

You look at him one last time. He does not look back. His eyes are open but they are pointed at the grey paling sky, and the small almost-smile on his mouth is not for you.

You go up the bank.

At the top, you do not look back. You step onto the road and you keep your face turned forward and you walk — not too fast; never too fast on a road; a fast walker invites questions; a fast-walking woman on a dawn road, double — east, toward Sadaiziche, away from the bend that is now beginning to clatter with the unmistakable approach of three horses. You re-braid your hair as you walk, fingers working without thought, tucking the long damp rope of it down inside your collar where it will not catch a passing eye.

The silver burns cold in your pocket. The little phial knocks against it with each step. The vellum scrap is folded against your hip and you can feel it there, the way you can feel a blade pressed too close. Whatever was written on it, the dying man thought it worth carrying through the long beating that brought him to the ditch.

Ahead, the road opens. To either side, the country: vineyards stripped for winter on the flats, oak scrub climbing the slopes, the smudge of a shepherd's fire to the south, the shrine of Vabum the Cup-Bearer to the north — its little stone pillar growing closer with each step. Somewhere behind you, three horses round a bend and meet a dying man in a ditch and a question they will not stop asking.

Three breaths, before the road forks you.
