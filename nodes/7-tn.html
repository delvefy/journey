The Folded Paper at the Walnut Inn

A country inn at the turning of the road by the lone walnut, evening, six men at the long table and a seventh at a small round table by the chimney, and a folded paper at the seventh man's elbow with a corner that has lifted a little in the small draught off the door.

You had crossed the river on the ferry. You had walked the small clay path between the reeds up to the road on the far bank. You had come to the turning at the lone walnut by the small bell of the late afternoon, and the lone walnut was an actual walnut, an old gnarled one with a small whitewashed shrine of the Cup-Bearer set into the base of the trunk, and a country woman with a basket of dry figs at the shrine's foot had pointed you with her chin at the road south and at the small inn perhaps a quarter-bell along it. The inn was the kind of inn the country had set there because the country had been crossing the river at this ferry for as long as anyone at the river minded. There was a long pine table at the centre of the main room and a small round table by the chimney and a kitchen open at the back wall. The bread was on the table. The cheese was beside it. The wine was the small thin red the country had been pressing on these slopes since the empire was first an empire.

You had taken a seat at the long pine table at the far end from the chimney. You had eaten a piece of bread and a piece of cheese. You had drunk half the cup. You had read the room without lifting your face: six men at the long table — a farrier, two carters, an old man with a small bone-handled awl, a boy of perhaps fifteen, a soldier of the imperial pattern in plain clothes, the dust-stains at the knees a soldier's wife had not yet washed — and the seventh at the small round table by the chimney.

The seventh was perhaps forty. He had a brown wool coat, the cut not the country's. He had a small leather satchel at his feet and the heel of a long-sleeved riding shirt at his wrist. He had no instrument on the table, no cup of his own, no plate; he had a single folded paper at his right elbow and an inkwell beside it the inn-keeper would not, by the inn-keeper's habit, have set out on a Thursday evening. The inkwell was the seventh man's. The paper at the elbow was a paper he had folded twice. The corner of the upper fold had lifted, by some small breath of the door each time the door opened, a fraction of a finger.

You did not, in any small motion, draw the room's eye. You ate the bread. You did not, by any short turning of the eye, read the paper directly. The body, by the careful sideways attention the body had taught itself all week, read the paper by the small shape of the fold and by the colour of the inkwell's stopper. The fold was the careful triple of the order's posting-clerks. The stopper was a small piece of red glass the size of a finger-bone. The inkwell, by its red glass stopper, was an inkwell the order kept at perhaps a dozen wayside inns along the southern circuit for its travelling clerks. The paper at the elbow was a description-sheet. The man at the round table was waiting on the door.

He had not, at any point in the bell since you had come into the room, looked at the long table. He was looking at the door.

The door opened, in the slow careful way the inn-keeper's daughter opened it for someone come from outside. A small grey cat came in. The door closed. The cat went under the long table and came up at the seventh man's table by the chimney and laid itself against his calf in the small familiar way of a cat the seventh man had been keeping at this inn for the time he had been on the circuit. The seventh man bent. He set a finger between the cat's ears. He turned, with the bending, the folded paper a half-turn against his elbow.

You read the upper line at the back of the page. You did not need to lift your head. The body had read it before your mind quite had time to. The upper line at the back of the page was the small careful hand of a Khaboran circuit-clerk and the line read: man, perhaps forty, sun-darkened, Persian boots of the order's pattern; the right hand carries the Khaboran fold at the lining at the third button; the gait at the right knee a small habit from a boyhood at the seminary's south stair.

The inn-keeper's daughter brought the inn-keeper's daughter's other small particular kindness — a brown earthenware bowl of stewed lentils — to the long pine table at the far end where you sat. She set the bowl in front of you. The bell of the evening rang in the small chapel at the south end of the village. The cat at the man's calf settled into a slow purr.
