The Ferry at the Reed-Bank

A small flat-bottomed ferry on a slow brown river at the bell of mid-morning, an old ferryman at the stern who does not ask names, and a second passenger on the bench opposite yours who has been watching the back of your right hand since the moment you stepped onto the boards.

The road had brought you down to a river you did not, by any chart your week had given you, have a name for. The river was wide and brown and slow. The crossing was a flat-bottomed ferry on a hand-over-hand rope strung from a peeled poplar on each bank. There was a small thatched lean-to on the near bank where the ferryman kept a clay pot for the half-copper a man paid to be taken across. The ferryman was old, perhaps seventy, with a face the colour of wet oak and a long-handled pole at his shoulder and the small economical attention of a man who had stood on the same boards two-thirds of a working life. He had not asked your name at the lean-to. He had not asked the second passenger's. He had taken the half-copper from each of you, put it in the pot, and gestured at the boat with the small turning of the pole-end.

The second passenger was a man perhaps fifty. He had a broad heavy coat of a sort country merchants wore on the long road, the coat the colour of dry dust, and at his shoulder a piece of folded oilcloth that had a stack of leather under it. He had stepped onto the boards behind you. He had taken the bench opposite. He had laid the oilcloth across his lap. He had spread his hands on his knees in the careful idle gesture of a man waiting out a crossing, and the eyes under the heavy brow had not, since the boards had begun to lift and settle on the small wash of the slow water, lifted from the back of your right hand.

You did not look at him. The body did. The body had read him in the small breath at the lean-to and had read him again when he had taken the bench and had read him a third time now in the small slow turning of the ferry against the rope. He was carrying — by the way the coat sat — no sword and no long knife, but the inside of the coat at the second button had the small particular pull a folded cudgel made when a cudgel was laid in the lining and the lining did not, by the maker's habit, quite hide it. He was carrying — by the small pause at the wrist when he had taken the half-copper from his purse — a Persian copper, not the river-country's, and the Persian copper was the kind a man got at the post-office at Sadaiziche in change for an imperial silver. He was carrying — by the colour of the dust on his boot at the inner heel — the road from the west, not the south. He had been at Sadaiziche the day before yesterday. He had crossed an imperial road the morning of yesterday. He had been on this small ferry crossing perhaps three quarter-bells.

The ferry took the river slowly. The far bank was perhaps twelve poles away. The current was the small slow current of a river the country had been crossing at this point in this manner since the first bridge of the imperial road had been a bridge somewhere else. The ferryman at the stern, by his small particular care, was a man whose ferry did not, when it crossed, like to be the place a man on his bench was watching the back of another man's hand from. The ferryman had not said so. He had not said anything.

The man on the bench opposite spoke. He spoke quietly. He spoke to a piece of the boat's planking by his right boot, in the small careful voice of a man who did not want the ferryman at the stern to hear the speaking.

"Friend." He did not lift his eye. "The hand at your right at the third button is, by the lining, a Khaboran fold. There were eight folds taught the year I came up at Khaboran from Behbahan, and the master's wife had a way of moving the haft a quarter-turn at the third button in the seventh fold that nobody at the seminary but the master's own boys learned. You have, friend, the seventh." He breathed. "I am Hassan. I was at Khaboran a year and was let go for a thing the master would not, before letting me go, write in the order's drawer. I have not been near a man with the seventh fold in his coat in eleven winters. I am not on this ferry by accident. I am at the ferry by my own walking and by the very small inquiry I have spent the week's silver on at four wayside inns. I have not, today, decided what to be. Tell me, by the time the ferry reaches the bank, whether you are at Khaboran's hand still or whether the order has, in the four years I have heard rumour of, let you go also."

He spread his hands on his knees. He did not look up. The ferryman at the stern leaned his weight on the pole. The far bank was perhaps eight poles.
