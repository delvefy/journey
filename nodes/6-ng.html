The Boy at the Verge

A wagon-road in country off the chart at an hour the bells did not yet reach, a boy of nine or ten lying on his side in the long dry grass at the verge, and a small grey mare standing reins-down ten paces further on with her flank against a low thorn.

You had walked out of the way-house, or the bishop's outer room, or the matron's kitchen at Vahdat in the grey hour. The road south was the road and the road did not, by the light, much care which door you had come out of. The name in your coat was a name a single witness had given you back the night before and the morning had not, in its waking, tried to take it from you again. The body had eaten and slept and walked. The body was, for the first morning in seven, walking without the small careful waiting at the back of the head for the next stranger to read your face.

You came round a long bend at perhaps two farsang from the way-house. The country here was small fields of stubble and a long line of poplar going down to a stream you could hear but not yet see. The road bent again. In the bend, at the verge on the left, in the long dry grass under the lowest of the poplars, was a boy. He was lying on his right side. He had one knee drawn up. He had a small woven sack across his shoulder that had spilt a half-fist of millet in a brown line into the dust by his head. His left hand was a fist at his collarbone. The hand was not relaxed and was not, by the colour of the knuckle, the hand of a boy asleep.

You stopped. You did not, at once, go down to him. You read the road first. The mare ten paces further on was not a country mare. She was a small grey of the army's pattern, saddled, the bridle dropped over a thorn-branch the way a man drops a bridle who has, in a hurry, meant to come back. There was no man on the road behind her. The line of poplars went on a long way. The boy at the verge was breathing — by the very small lift of the shoulder under the sack — and was not, by the breathing, breathing as a boy breathes who is only winded.

You crossed to him. You went down on your knee. He had been struck once at the back of the head, by some object harder than a hand and softer than a stone; the blood at the hair had stopped but had not, by its colour, stopped long. He was perhaps nine. He was thin. His shirt under the sack was a brown homespun the country's poor children wore and the sack itself was the kind of small grain-sack a boy carried home from a country mill in payment for an hour's sweeping. He did not, when your hand went to his cheek, open his eyes. The cheek was cold against the morning.

You looked up the road. You looked down the road. You looked at the mare. The mare was watching you with the careful sideways attention of a horse who had been ridden hard and was not, by the dropping of the bridle, expecting to be ridden hard again at once. The saddle's near pannier was open at the strap. The far pannier was buckled. The country at the far side of the stream was rising, by a slow shoulder, into a small fold of oak. There was no smoke at the fold and no smoke at the stream.

You knew, by the body's own slow accounting, three things at once. The boy at the verge would not, by the bell of the second hour, be a boy if he stayed on the verge. The man who had ridden the mare hard was somewhere not yet visible at the line of poplars. The mare would carry a body the half-farsang to the next country house and would carry it faster than your own legs would. The body of the boy in your arms would weigh, by the body's careful knowing of such weights, perhaps three stone and a half.

You looked, once, at the long line of poplars in the direction the mare's man had come from. Nothing moved among them. The morning was the kind of morning a country morning was when the country had not, by anyone's word, yet been told what the morning had on its road.
