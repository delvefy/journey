The Search

Three horses, three men, three searches a road can bear. You stand in the middle of them all.

You give them nothing. You woke in the road an hour ago, you say — and it is not, in any meaningful sense, a lie — and you remember little. You saw no one. You heard nothing but the wind in the reeds. You are walking to Sadaiziche to find a temple-physician for a headache that will not leave you, which is again not, strictly, untrue.

The leader does not interrupt. He listens with the slow patience of a man who has heard many flavours of evasion and is professionally interested in which one you will pick. When you are done, he is quiet for a long count. Then he turns his head a fraction toward the youngest rider, who has finished his slow walk down the bank and is now standing in the reeds with his boots in the mud, looking at the body.

For a moment you do not understand the youngest rider's stillness. Then you do.

He is not looking at the body the way a man looks at a thing he has found. He is looking at the body the way a man looks at a thing he has lost. The angle of his head; the small slackness around his mouth; the way one gloved hand has come up to his collar and is touching, very briefly, the badge of his rank as if for steadiness. He is younger than the others, and the body in the reeds is the first body he has been required to find. The others — the leader, the silent third — have not yet looked down the bank at all.

"Captain," the youngest says, without turning. His voice is the voice of a man trying very hard to be a man. "There is a — a person. Here."

The leader does not move. He is still looking at you. "Living," he says — flat — to the youngest, but watching your face.

"I — I think so. I think he is. He is — he is —"

The wounded man, somewhere under the mud, makes the smallest of sounds. A breath through teeth. The youngest crouches, then half-rises, then crouches again, the way a man crouches when he does not know whether to lift or to call.

The leader's slow eyes have not moved from yours. "And you," he says, to you, "heard nothing."

"Nothing," you say.

"And saw nothing."

"Nothing."

The third rider, who has not yet spoken since the bend, says — without dismounting, almost to himself — "He is wearing fine boots for a ditch."

The youngest looks up. "There is a — a charm. A fish. A —" He cannot finish. He has been told what to look for. He has been told, certainly, what to do when he finds one. The training and the doing have come apart in his hands.

The leader, at last, dismounts. He steps once toward the bank — not toward you, not yet — and the morning around the four of you, where two minutes ago there was wind in the reeds, has become the kind of silence that a road only makes when it has decided to remember a moment.

You have, perhaps, half a breath before the leader is down the bank with the youngest, and the wounded man's eyes meet his.
