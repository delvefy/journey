The Stripped Rows

A road forgets its travellers in an hour. A vineyard forgets its workers in a winter.

You step off the road's pale crown at a place where the verge falls away into the first vineyard. You do it without breaking stride, the way you would step off the edge of a boat — one foot still on the road, the other already gone — and then you are down the soft shoulder of cleared earth and walking between the bare-pruned rows.

It is, very suddenly, quieter. The wind that ran along the road runs over your head now, in the canopy that is not there yet because winter has eaten the leaves, and at the ground level the air is still. Each step crackles a little — the dry leaf-litter under the vines — and you choose your steps for the patches of dark earth where the litter has been raked clear by some thrifty winter hand. The rows are perfectly regular. The vines are old; the trunks are gnarled the way old men's hands are gnarled, knuckled into themselves, the bark grey. Someone has, in the last week, gone along these rows with a knife and reduced each vine to a single short bowed wand. You walk through their work.

The hooves come up the road. You hear them very clearly through the still air, above the level of your head, the way you hear voices through a wall. Three horses, walking. They pause at the verge — at the milestone, presumably, at the smear of blood — and then they pass. They do not turn south into the vineyard. Why would they. There is no reason for a traveller to step off the empire's road in the dawn cold, and the wardens have only one morning to give to one heretic, and the road runs east to where heretics make towns.

You count their breaths, not yours, until they are out of hearing. Then you stop, between two old grey vines, and you take stock.

The silver fish is in your pocket. The phial with its wax stopper, no larger than the joint of your thumb, is also in your pocket. The whetstone weighs nothing. The copper ring without its stone is in the seam of your trousers. The three small coins have joined your original four in the purse on your hip. And the folded scrap of vellum — three lines of writing you could not read in the half-light at the bottom of the ditch — is between your fingers now, in the proper colour of dawn, in a place where no one will come for the next hour.

You can, if you wish, sit on the cold earth between these two old vines and look at it.

You can, if you wish, climb higher — up out of the vineyard, into the stripped olive-yards above, and up further into the oak scrub where, somewhere on the southern slope, a column of pale smoke is rising off a shepherd's fire that you have been climbing toward without admitting it.

You can, if you wish, simply walk on east through the country. The vineyards run in long strips toward Sadaiziche and beyond, and between them are stony strips where no one goes in winter, and the country eats footprints the way a river eats lamp-oil. A patient walker who avoided the road and avoided the towns and avoided the shepherds could be in the foothills above the imperial heartland in three days, and no clerk would ever have written the figure down.

The morning is grey-yellow now. The sun has begun to lift behind the eastern ridge. Far below, on the road, a starling crosses the verge — small, oil-black, ordinary — and lands at the smear of blood and inspects it with the unhurried interest of a bird that has lived through many such mornings on the imperial road and expects to live through many more.
