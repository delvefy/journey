The Page in the Magistrate's Hand

A bright bishop's table at the second hour of an afternoon, a circuit-magistrate reading what the local pen has written, and a name read aloud from a register the empire kept in a drawer when you were younger than your boots.

You had been sat in the small front room of the post for nearly a bell. The post-priest's clerk had given you a cup of pale tea and a small flat barley-loaf and had taken your account on a single sheet of vellum and read it back to you in the small careful voice of a man who liked to read a thing back. You had signed at the bottom in your own hand. The clerk had taken the sheet to the back room and the back room had been quiet a long beat and then there had been a low murmur of two voices through the door — the post-priest's voice you had heard before, and another voice you had not — and now the door was opening and the post-priest came out first, his hand at the small of the back of the man behind him, the way a younger priest comes out behind an older one.

The older one was perhaps sixty. He had a clean white beard cropped square at the chin in the eastern fashion, a long grey wool robe with the small gold cross of a magistrate-father at the collar, and the long thin hands of a man who had done his trade with paper. There was a tied bundle of files under his arm. He came into the front room without ceremony and the clerk stood and the post-priest stood and you stood with them because the body, again, knew the standing before the mind had given the standing a name.

"Sit, friends." The magistrate's voice was pleasant. "Sit. I am on the road from Khaboran and the afternoon will not, by the bell, wait." He set his bundle of files on the long table by the window. He set a folded pair of reading-spectacles on top of the bundle. He looked, briefly, at the cup of pale tea by your hand, and at the small flat barley-loaf, and at the sheet of vellum on the top of the bundle, which the clerk had taken to the back room a quarter-bell past. He sat. He opened the spectacles. He read the top of the sheet without yet reading the body.

His face did not change. He set the spectacles down. He looked at you. He looked at the wrist where your sleeve had ridden, a small accident, when you had reached for the cup. He looked at your boots. He looked, last, at your eyes, and the look he gave you was the look of a man who has read a face before in a different light and is, by careful habit, finding the prior reading again now.

"Friend," he said. He paused, as if granting the word. Then, without unkindness: "Khorshid."

He let the name settle. He did not, at once, follow it. The clerk did not understand the name as a name. The post-priest, who had until that moment been at the door listening with the small attentive patience of a host, took a half-step into the room and stopped, his face the small ordinary white of a man who has just understood that his afternoon is now a different afternoon.

"I am Magistrate-Father Hossein," the older one said, more for the post-priest's record than for yours. "I was, twenty years past, the seminary-prefect at Khaboran. Khorshid Daiyani came to me at thirteen and left me at nineteen and went into the field for the order of the question. The leaving was not finished cleanly. The order has had your name in a paybook ever since, in a column that does not close." He folded his spectacles and laid them on the sheet. "Tell me, friend, what is the post-priest's pen has written about you that has brought your face into my afternoon." His own face did not move. "Or — if it is easier — tell me what you have, in the last week, remembered."

You did not speak at once. The body knew the standing; the body, you understood at the slow centre of the chest, knew the man as well. Khorshid Daiyani. The name fit you the way the Persian boots had fit you all this week. The wrist scar under the cuff was the small white tally of a manacle that had been taken once and let go. The seminary-prefect had been the man who had let it go.

The room held still. The post-priest closed the door behind himself with a small careful click. The clerk, by his small pale face at the table, did not yet know whether to be the witness to a writ or the clerk to a homecoming. The afternoon outside the window was the same afternoon it had been a bell ago.
