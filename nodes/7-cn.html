The Stream-Bend at the Washing Stones

A small stream a day's walk west of the dyer's town, three flat stones at the bend where the country women came in the morning to do their washing, and a woman of perhaps fifty kneeling at the middle stone wringing a brown shawl who has not yet, in the four breaths since you came up the path, lifted her face.

You had come out by the rooflight in the dyer's mother's daughter's coat — or by the rooflight in your own, or by no rooflight at all because the willow at the rooflight had given way to a hand a stranger had pulled in — and had gone over two roofs and down a drainpipe at the back of a shed and out by an alley into a country lane that had taken you, by the small particular forgetfulness of country lanes, away from the dyer's town faster than a man on the lane would suspect. You had walked. The night had been a long night. The morning had been a grey morning of small low cloud that had not, by the second farsang, decided whether to rain. You had walked off the road at the bell of the third hour to sleep in a hollow under a thorn-hedge and had woken at the bell of the noon. You had walked another farsang. The stream-bend was at the foot of a low slope. The three flat stones at the bend were the washing-stones the country women used. Two of the stones were empty. The middle one was not.

The woman at the middle stone was perhaps fifty. She had a coarse brown apron over a long grey dress and a scarf of the same grey. She had a small wooden bat for the beating of cloth at the side of the stone and a low woven basket of unwashed linen at her hip. She was wringing, when you came up the path through the willows, a brown shawl that had once been some other colour. Her hands at the wringing were the steady careful hands of a woman who had done this number of shawls at this stone for, by her own forearm, perhaps thirty years.

You did not, at the path's last bend, see her. The stream's wash through the willows had hidden the small sound of the wringing and the willows had hidden the small sound of the stones. She had seen you, at the last bend, perhaps four breaths before you saw her. She did not, in any of the seeing, lift her face. She wrung the shawl. She straightened it. She laid it on the dry stone at her left. She took the next from the basket. She wrung.

"Friend," she said, to the linen, in the small dry voice country women used when they were minded to speak to strangers without lifting the face. "The path goes on at the far bank by the small footbridge of cut willow. The footbridge holds. The next house is a half-farsang on at the small holding of my cousin's husband's nephew. The nephew is not at home. The nephew's wife is the woman with the hare-lip; she will give you a piece of bread and not ask a name." She wrung. She laid. She took. "I have heard, friend, by the talk of two horsemen who came down by the same path at the bell of the noon while I was at this stone, that there is a description out by the river-road and the cart-track and the small wayside inn at the lone walnut, of a stranger of the order who came up out of a dyer's loft at the small town at the back of the slope yesterday at the bell of the midnight by a willow at a rooflight, and who is being sought by Bardas's man at Sadaiziche and the post-priest at Bagh-e-Sang and the question-priest at the south-gate at Sadaiziche all three." She wrung. "Two horsemen are an evening on a road. Three offices on a description is a season. I had not, friend, much expected by my own ear to hear the description on a path at the foot of my own washing-stone." She straightened the shawl. She laid it on the dry stone. "The path takes the small footbridge. The footbridge holds. The nephew's wife is the woman with the hare-lip. The description has at it a particular knee."

She took the next from the basket. She did not, by any small lift of the face, look at you.

The stream went on. The cloud, by some accident of the bell of the noon, decided not to rain.
