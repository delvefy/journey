The Smoke at the Bend

A small yard at the foot of a track, three riders on the next ridge, and a morning that is, by every door it has opened, no longer asking the question quietly.

The horse was a good horse. Mehrdad had kept it the way a tradesman keeps a tool he depends on; the bit sat clean, the saddle's leather was oiled, the stride was the steady reaching stride of an animal that had been ridden long mornings and trusted the rider's knees. You did not, by the small accidents of your body, ride badly. You had not known until you swung up. The hands had known. The knees had known. The road east of the crossroads had taken you at a clean canter under a sun beginning to slope and a sky going pale at the top and the small leather case strapped behind the cantle had bumped at your back with the unbothered weight of a master's purse and instruments now passing, by the morning's mathematics, into another man's keeping.

You smelled the smoke from a quarter farsang. It was not the smoke of an evening's hearth. The wood was wrong — green wood, wet thatch, the particular cinnamon-sourness of an animal-stall taken by fire. You did not slow. You came round the next bend of the track at the same canter and the farmstead lay below you in its small yard: a single low house, a stall against the lee wall, a wagon with its tongue down, the stall and the lee end of the house both smoking out into the afternoon air in long thin grey columns that bent eastward under the wind.

You drew up at the upper end of the yard. The yard had been worked over in the last quarter-bell. There was a man in the dirt by the wagon-tongue who would not be getting up. There was a woman by the threshold of the house on her side, alive — the small high catch of her breath audible across the yard — with the dark wet across the front of her under-dress and one hand still in the dirt as if she had been crawling. A child was sitting against the doorpost of the smoking stall, knees drawn up, eyes open, not crying. The hen-coop was open and empty. The wagon's small chest was open and emptier still. The work was perhaps not yet a quarter-bell old.

You looked up at the ridge east. Three riders were going down its far side at a walk that did not bother to be a canter. They had not bothered to look back. They were perhaps four hundred paces ahead. The middle of the three had a long bundle across his saddle the colour of the woman's apron. The horses were good Persian horses, not the small Persian horses of the network; province-bandit horses, taken from a posting-house some season since, with the empire's iron at the shoe. The leader of the three wore no shoulder-cord, or wore one with the cord cut off at the seam. The wind brought a piece of his laughing back across the yard between the smoke columns, and the laughing was not, by its colour, a man's first kill of the season.

The horse under you blew once and tossed its head at the smoke. The leather case bumped at your back. The small folded knife in the lining of your coat had been, an hour past, the cold practised thing in your hand at four paces, and the hand remembered. The woman in the doorway made a small wet sound that was not yet a word. The child against the doorpost did not turn its head.

The road forked at the yard. The lower track went on east up to the ridge after the riders. The upper track turned away into the country south through a fold of oak. The smoke went east on the wind.
