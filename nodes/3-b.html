3-b · Vine and Oak

Vine and Oak

Off the empire's road. The land is patient, and the riders ride a road that no longer leads to you.

You go down the lee bank fast, the man's good arm hooked across your shoulders, your own boots punching through the dry vine-leaves where the irrigation runs lowest. The cut of the dawn comes through the stripped rows in long pale strips. You move along the irrigation in those strips, where the dark earth swallows your prints, until the road and the milestone are out of sight behind the vineyard's first shoulder.

The hooves come up the road. You hear them very clearly through the cold air, the way you hear a bell across a valley — and then a pause, where the leader has reined in to look at the verge, at the blood, at the bootprints down the bank — and then the slow walk of three horses past the milestone and on. You count their breaths, not yours. You count them all the way until the hooves are smaller than the wind.

The man sags against you. "Bless," he whispers, and it is not a benediction; it is the small disbelieving word a man uses when something he had stopped expecting happens anyway.

You keep moving. You climb. The vineyards give way to a stony strip where the soil thins, and then to the oak scrub — squat, grey-leaved trees, no taller than a man, growing in tight knots out of the limestone. The slope is not steep, but each step costs more than the last. He is heavier with every farsang of altitude. Once you have to set him down against a stone and breathe for a count of forty before you can lift him again.

When you do, his eyes are clearer. The cold air, perhaps, or the simple shock of still being alive. "There is a shepherd," he says, in a voice not much above the wind. "On the south face. Above the second vineyard. Asha keeps a hut. He has — he has —" His head lolls. "He has fed us before."

Us. The word lodges in you, the way a thorn lodges in a sleeve. You do not ask. There will be time, or there will not be time, and asking now is greed of the wrong kind.

You sit him in the lee of a flat-faced stone, where the morning sun, when it finally crests the eastern ridge, will warm him first. You crouch in front of him. You take his good hand. The Mardesran charm — that silver fish swallowing its own tail — has come out of your shirt during the climb and lies now against your collarbone, in plain view. He notices it. His eyes move from the charm to your face, and a small slow smile begins at one corner of his mouth and does not finish.

"You knew," he says. "Or you guessed. Or you — recognized." He coughs and the coughing pinks his beard. "Brother. Sister. Whichever. Whichever you are. I will not ask again."

You do not know how to answer this. You do not know if the knowing is in you, or only the wearing of the symbol that has — for these last moments — covered the breastbone of a man not yet dead.

The wind moves through the oak scrub. Somewhere to the south, a column of pale smoke rises off the slope. The shepherd's fire, presumably; the same one you saw from the road in the dark, the one you have been climbing toward without admitting it. Below you, the imperial road is a thin pale thread, and the bend where the riders were is empty now. To the east, the country folds away into hills you have no map for. To the north — only the long open scrub, no people, no fire, no road.

The man's eyes close. They open. They close again. He is sliding. Whatever you do, you have an hour at the most before he is past asking.
