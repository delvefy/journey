Asha Down the Ridge

A small dry herder's shelter at a fold of the country east of the dyer's town, the upper slope marked at the third bend by a single white-thorn in winter-fruit, a clay pot of barley-and-fat at a slow fire under a goat-hide roof, and a tall white-haired woman in a man's coat at the slope's last step who has, by the small particular long walking of her own boots since the bell of the dawn, come down from a fire of her own to a fire that is not at any map the empire keeps.

You had come out by the rooflight or by the willow at the back or by no roof at all. You had walked off the cart-track at the third milestone and gone over the low ridge above the willows by a sheep-path the country woman at the washing-stones had named at the small lift of her chin. The country east of the dyer's town was the country off the empire's map; the imperial road kept to the river's south bank, the cart-track kept to the willows, the donkey-paths kept to the contour of the low hills, and the country between had been folding into itself for two days at the small unhurried pace of the body's own walking. The herder's shelter at the fold was the kind of shelter the country had been keeping at this fold for as long as a herder's shelter had been a useful thing.

The shelter was a goat-hide pegged out at the foot of a low limestone outcrop. There was a slow fire under it. There was a clay pot of barley-and-fat at the fire. There was a small flat stone laid across two larger ones to serve as a bench. There was a thin grey cat at the inner wall. There was no other person at the shelter when you came up by the sheep-path; the herder, the body read at the second look, had been up to a higher fold this morning with the flock and was — by the angle of the small notched staff propped at the goat-hide — not yet expected.

You sat on the flat stone. You took the pot off the fire by the small wooden handle the herder kept at the side. You ate with the small wooden spoon that lay at the pot's lip. You did not, by any small particular hurry, eat fast. The cat at the inner wall watched you and did not move.

You heard the second pair of boots at the slope's last step at the small particular bell — perhaps two breaths after the body had heard them at the wind's small turning above the ridge. The boots were not the herder's. The herder's small notched staff was at the goat-hide and the boots at the slope did not, by the sound, have a staff with them. You did not, by any small motion, set down the spoon. You did not, by any small motion, turn at the slope.

"Khoda's road, brother." The voice was a country woman's voice in the small carefully roughened pitch a woman in a man's coat used for ten years in country off the map. "Sit, brother. The herder is up at the second fold and is not down at the shelter before the bell of the noon. The shelter holds for two on a Thursday. I have come down from the second vineyard's ridge by the bell of the dawn at the small particular request of the morning's runner."

You turned. She was at the slope's last step. She was tall. The hair under the dark blue scarf was white at both temples and grey at the crown. The coat was a man's coat the country had worked on for many winters. The boots were the boots the country wove the felt for and the empire did not, by the bishop's licence, sell at any market in the south. There was no star at her temple. There did not, by any small particular of her face, need to be one.

"I am Asha," she said. She did not, by any small motion, sit. She stood at the slope's last step at the small unhurried pace of a woman who had, by her own walking these years, no immediate hurry to be at any small flat stone. "The morning's runner at my fire was the wash-woman's nephew at the small wash-stones west of the dyer's town. He came up at the bell of the third with a description and a particular knee. The fire's morning has — by the second bell since — sent four runners north along the ridge, two south toward the spur, and one west to the salt-line. The country has put its ears to its own ground in the last half-day, brother, for the man who came up out of a rooflight at a willow in the bell of the midnight. The country is, this morning, listening at four ridges and three roads. I came down because the man at the shelter is, by my own small honest reckoning at the bell of the noon, the man my mother's mother's circle is owed an afternoon's reading on."

She did not, by her face, lean. The thin grey cat at the inner wall stretched. The fire at the pot held.
