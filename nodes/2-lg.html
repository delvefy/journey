Kinsman

A lie told on the empire's road, with both hands open and a clean tongue.

You went up the bank with him slung across your shoulder. The mud took your boot-prints and gave them back; you went sideways, a crab's climb, and at the top of the bank you laid him flat in the lee of the milestone — the one that read SADAIZICHE 2 ½ FARSANG in tired weather-worn lettering — and you tugged your own coat over him to flatten his shape against the stone. The silver charm at his throat you lifted on its thong and slipped down inside your shirt's collar, where the cold of it against your collarbone was shocking, the way a wedding ring is shocking on a stranger's hand. Then you straightened, you set your hands open at your sides, and you turned to face the bend.

The first rider came round it. He saw you at once — a single dark figure on the empty road, exactly where blood had been spilled on the verge — and his hand went to the rein in the small particular way of a man for whom the next gesture is the sword. Two more rose behind him: hard leather coats, the road-warden's badge on the chest, the white shoulder-cord of church writ. Hunters. Three of them at a walk, in no hurry now, because the verge had already begun to answer them.

You spoke before the leader did. The lie was in your mouth and you let it out the way a stonemason lets a chisel-stroke go — once, cleanly, with no second blow. "Forgive me, sir. My kinsman. He fell from his cart at the turning of the night. Word came to me in Rabad before the lamps were out. I have walked from there in the dark to find him."

The leader did not answer at once. His horse blew steam between you. He looked at the milestone, at the shape under your coat, at the smear of blood on the verge, and then at you — and his slow eyes settled there longer than was comfortable. He asked your kinsman's name. The name came to you the way the word in your mouth at waking had come, from no place you could find. Daiyan, you said. Daiyan of the small vineyard above Sadaiziche.

The wounded man, with what was either grace or the simple economy of a body running short of blood, made a small sound at exactly that moment — half-cough, half-moan, the sound a man might make if he had spent the night in a ditch with broken ribs from a fall. It was convincing because it was the truth, only with the wrong cause attached.

The leader looked at you a final time. The look was not friendly. The look was not unfriendly. The look was what a man does when he files a thing for later. "Then walk your kinsman to Sadaiziche, friend," he said, "and walk him quickly. The road is uncertain this morning."

He clucked his tongue. The horses began to move. And then — when he had already half-turned — he reined in, and looked back, and the air went out of you. "We will be in Sadaiziche by the noon bell," he said, "at the warden-house by the south gate. Bring your Daiyan there before the bell, friend. So that we may write the road's record properly."

It was not a request. It was the lie being held, gently, between his fingers, where you both could see it.

He rode on. The hooves dwindled eastward. In the cooling silence on the road you turned to the man at your feet, and the man at your feet opened his eyes, and his look was the look of a creature who had been delivered a length of life he had stopped counting on and did not yet know what to do with.

"They will have a clerk," he whispered, in the cracked half-voice of someone who has spent his strength on stillness. "By the bell. And the clerk will ask. The clerk will write down my mother's name and the price of last year's grapes. The clerk will know Daiyan from no Daiyan in Sadaiziche."

He coughed. The cough came pink to his beard. He smiled, very faintly, with the kind of mouth a person makes when they are no longer afraid of much and are interested, again, in what comes next. The lie had given him his hour. What the hour was for had not yet been agreed.

The sun cleared the eastern ridge in a thin yellow line. The road ran east toward a town with a warden-house in it and a clerk who knew his vineyards. Behind you, the shrine of Vabum the Cup-Bearer waited at its turning. Off to the south, the vineyards stretched into oak scrub and a column of pale smoke that you had been climbing toward, in your mind, without admitting it. Three roads now. One bell. An hour.
