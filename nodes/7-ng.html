The Mare's Man at the Yard

A country house at the mid-afternoon of the same day, a small inner yard with a brown ox at the trough and a brick oven warming under a piece of tin, and a horseman at the gate whose grey mare is the grey mare the verge gave you.

The country house was the matron's niece's at the mill-stream, or the smallholder's at the bend where the road turned at the lone walnut, or a way-house's back yard the woman at the well had said you could lay the boy down at without questions. The boy was on a folded blanket in the small inner room behind the kitchen. He was breathing more evenly than he had been at the verge. The woman of the house had washed the blood out of the hair at the back of his head and had laid a paste of figs and rue at the bruise and had said, by her own count of such bruises in country boys, that the boy would wake by the bell of the evening if he was the kind of boy who woke from such bruises at all. She had given you a small earthenware cup of barley-broth and pushed you to a stool by the kitchen door. The kitchen door looked out into the inner yard. The inner yard had a brown ox at the trough and a brick oven warming under a piece of tin and a small grey hen with three half-grown chicks at the foot of the oven.

You had been at the stool perhaps three quarter-bells when the hooves came up the lane at the front.

One horse. A fast walk, not a trot, of the kind a tired horse and a tired rider made when neither of them quite had a fourth gear left in them. The horse stopped at the front of the house. There was a long pause; the rider was, by the small particular sound of the leather, getting down. He came round the side of the house to the inner yard on his own feet because the front door was the kind of door country houses did not, by their own habit, open quickly at a stranger's hand.

He stopped at the gate. The mare was three paces behind him at the rein. The mare was the same grey of the army's pattern, saddled, the off-side pannier still open at the strap. The man at the gate was perhaps thirty. He had a dust-blue coat with the silver olive of the imperial border force at the shoulder; the coat was dirtier than it had been when its tailor cut it; one cuff had a thin dark line of dried blood at the inside of the wrist that the cuff was, by the way he was holding the cuff, not yet ready to put a name to. He had no sword at the hip. He had a small light cavalry sabre in a leather case at the saddle behind. He had been weeping in the four bells since the verge and was not weeping now.

He saw you on the stool at the kitchen door. He saw the woman of the house at the oven. He saw, by the small turning of his head at the open kitchen door, the corner of the folded blanket in the inner room. He set his hand against the gate-post. He breathed in twice before he spoke.

"Friend." His voice was very low. "The mare at the rein behind me is the mare I left on the road at the verge under the poplars at the third bend. The boy in the room behind you is the boy I left in the long grass at the verge. The boy at the verge is my brother. I am Manuchehr of the second tower at Sayyaf. I left the boy at the verge because I had — by the small particular bad luck of a third man in a thicket at the third bend — no third gear left in my legs, and I had to go on the legs the mare did have, and I had to go on those legs in a direction that was not the direction the boy was on. I have come back the long way round the mill-stream by a road the third man's friends are not, by my own walking these three bells, on. The third man is in the thicket. He is no longer the third man's friends' third man." He breathed in once more. "I had not — by any reading I could make at the bell of the noon — expected the boy to be at any kitchen tonight. I have, friend, no claim on the boy by any small accounting at the gate. I owe you the boy. I owe the woman of the house the room. I owe the kitchen door whatever the kitchen door's people require of the owing. Speak first, friend. Tell me what I owe."

He did not, in any of the speaking, let go of the gate-post. The mare behind him made a small noise about the ox at the trough. The woman of the house, at the oven, had not yet turned. She was waiting on you.
