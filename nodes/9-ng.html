The Cooper at the Inner Door

The fourth house's inner kitchen at Behesht the morning after the doorstep, a low table under the window onto the back yard with bread and dried mulberries and a brown clay jug of buttermilk, the cooper's wife at the bread-oven kneading the second loaf, Banu Mahin at the table picking the small stones out of a small heap of country rice, and a man of perhaps thirty-five at the inner door from the workshop's yard in a coat the country had been working on for as long as a country had been working a working man's coat.

The night had been the night the doorstep had set the body into the small wool pallet at the inner room's far end, or had not — had set the body, instead, by some small accident of the long careful walking, at the small store-room cot under the back stair, or at the workshop bench under the cooper's lambskin at the lane's head where the cooper had laid a folded coat for the boy of eight to sleep at the kiln-fire's afternoon. Either of the bells had carried the body to first light. The body had washed at the small brown jar at the back yard's tap. The body had eaten a small piece of yesterday's bread at the workshop's bench. The body had walked through the back yard's small gate to the inner kitchen of the cooper's mother's house at the bell of the second of the morning.

The kitchen was the kitchen the boy of eight had read at the kiln-fire's afternoon. The low table under the window onto the back yard was a long table of pine the cooper's father had set in the year of the second snow. The bread-oven at the inner wall was the bread-oven Banu Mahin's mother had built with her brother in the autumn of the first chestnut harvest after the second snow's winter. The cooper's wife at the oven was perhaps thirty. She had her hair under a small dark scarf and her hands at the second loaf and the small careful angle at her shoulder a country woman's body kept in the third hour of the morning's baking. She had been told, by the cooper's mother at the bell of the first, that the morning's first cup of buttermilk at the table was for the man at the wool pallet's far end. She had set a clay cup at the table's near corner without lifting her face from the loaf.

Banu Mahin was at the table. She had a small heap of country rice on a square of brown sacking at her left hand and a small earthen-ware bowl at her right. She was picking the small stones out at the small careful pace a country woman picked the small stones out of a small heap of rice on the first Thursday after a Thursday at sixty years. She did not, by her face, lift to the bell of the gate or to the body's coming through the small back gate or to the body's setting itself at the bench opposite. She lifted, instead, the small stone at her thumb's nail and laid it at the rim of the earthen-ware bowl. She lifted the next.

"The cooper has gone," she said, without lifting her face, "to the workshop at the lane's head at the bell of the first this morning, and the cooper has come back at the bell of the second by the back gate. The cooper has come back, child, because the country woman with the basket of figs at the shrine on the cart-track yesterday afternoon walked the cart-track at the bell of the dusk to the cooper's wife's mother's sister's house at the next valley and the cooper's wife's mother's sister's husband walked it at the bell of the dawn to the cooper. The cooper is at the inner door. The cooper has not, by his own honest accounting at his bench, been minded to come into the kitchen until the man at the bench has minded to lift his face."

She set the small stone at the bowl. She did not look up.

The man at the inner door was at the inner door. He was perhaps thirty-five. He had a country coat of dark brown wool over a small white shirt the cooper's wife had been washing on a Thursday for ten years. The hands at his sides were a cooper's hands — broad at the palm, the small careful scars at the knuckles a man got at his bench at the second year. The hair under the cap was the same dark hair the body had read at the boy of eight's third winter at the kiln-fire when the cooper's wife's mother's sister's son had been the boy of eleven at the back of the yard. He was Bahram. He had not, at the long pine table at any wayside inn at any small careful afternoon at the small kitchen door, been at this doorpost. The body had not, in twenty years, been at this kitchen.

"Khorshid." The voice was a working man's voice. "I have walked, brother, the small careful four farsang from my own forge since the bell of the dawn at the cooper's wife's mother's sister's husband's word. I have not, in the walking, lifted the conversation off the road. The morning's bell at the lane is the morning's bell. The cooper's mother is at the rice. The cooper's wife is at the loaf. The brother at the bench is — by the cooper's mother's word at the doorstep last evening — the brother. I have come up by the back gate. I have not, by any small motion, set the conversation at the workshop. I have come up to ask the brother, at no greater volume than the kitchen has at the bell of the second, what the morning's bell is for."

He stood at the doorpost. The cooper's wife at the loaf did not, in any small motion, turn. The cooper's mother at the rice lifted the next small stone.

The back yard's chickens at the small breath of wind made a small careful sound at the wall.
