The Doorstep at Behesht

A small house at the top of the village's single lane, a low wall of mud-brick under a yellowing vine, a basket of clean laundry on the doorstep with a folded brown towel on top, and a woman of perhaps sixty at the basket whose face is the small particular face of a woman who has stopped halfway through folding when the lane below put a fourth pair of boots into the bend at the foot of the wall.

The road had taken you east by the slow careful country pace the bell of the evening had allowed. You had come up by the cart-track at the mill-stream — or by the imperial road from the warden-post Manuchehr's mare had taken you a stage of — or by the small donkey-path the country woman at the lone walnut had said the cart-track met at the second milestone east — and the country had narrowed at the foot of a low ridge of olives into the small single line a man's road narrowed to when the man was, by the bell of the seventh, going to a village he had not been at in eleven years. Behesht came up at the lane's bend under the old chestnut at the head of the village. The chestnut was the chestnut you had eaten the green chestnuts from at eight. The single lane went up the slope between two rows of low houses. The fourth on the right was your mother's sister's.

You had not, in any walking, said the village's name aloud all afternoon. The body had said it for you, at the small careful turning of the right knee at the second milestone where the country woman with the basket of figs had been at the shrine, and the body had been saying it under the bell of the evening ever since. You had walked the last quarter-bell at no faster a pace than the bell allowed. You had come up to the lane's foot at the small unhurried walk of a stranger who had not, by any particular need, anywhere to be at the bell of sundown.

The fourth house's doorstep had a basket of clean laundry on it. The woman at the basket was perhaps sixty. She had the small careful long bones of the Mardesran side of your mother's house. The hair under the dark blue scarf was a steady iron grey; the scarf was the country pattern your mother's mother had set the loom for the year of the second snow. The hands at the folding were the hands the body had read at every washing-line in your eighth year. The woman was Banu Mahin — your mother's elder sister, the cooper Bahram's mother, the half-aunt who had taken you on her lap at four and let you fall asleep against her shoulder for an afternoon at the kiln-fire the year your mother had stopped getting out of the bed.

She lifted her face at the bend's fourth step. She did not, by any small motion, stand. The folded brown towel at the top of the basket she did not, by any small motion, set aside. She looked at you the way a woman who has folded a small grey shirt onto a basket every Thursday for sixty years looked at a shirt that was, by the small grey light at the lane, not quite the colour it had been the week before.

"Khorshid." Her voice was very dry. "The cooper's mother's nephew comes up the lane at the bell of the seventh, eleven years on, in the boots the order makes for a field-man and the small habit at the right knee a south-stair switch made of a boy of fourteen. The cooper is at the workshop. The cooper's wife is at the bread-oven at the back. The cooper's mother is at the doorstep folding the cooper's wife's washing. The cooper's mother does not, by her own honest accounting at sixty years, much care what the boots are. The boy at the bend is the boy. Come up, child."

She did not, in the saying, lift her hand. She bent again to the towel. She folded it the small careful three-fold a country woman folded a husband's clean towel at sixty years. She laid it on the basket. She picked up the next from the un-folded pile. She did not look up again.

The lane was very quiet. There was the smell of bread from the back yard and the smell of the kiln from the cooper's at the lane's head, and the small far sound of a child calling a goat down off the ridge above the village. The chestnut at the head of the lane shifted in a breath of wind. Banu Mahin folded a small white square of linen the way she had folded a small white square of linen every Thursday since before your mother had stopped getting out of the bed.

You stood at the foot of the bend. You did not, in any small motion, set the body to going up the lane and you did not, in any small motion, set it to going back down.
