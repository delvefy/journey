3-g · Mercy

Mercy

A word for what you do now, if you need one. A word, anyway.

You stop on the road. You stand there for a count of three with the silver burning in your pocket and the road open in front of you and the bend behind you growing louder, and then you turn around.

You do not pretend, even to yourself, that this is mercy. You hear the word in your head as you go back down the bank, and you say to yourself, clearly: no. You are not going down for him. You are going down for you. The man can describe a figure on a road. The man can describe a height, a shoulder, a voice. The man can — and a wounded man with the wardens crouched over him will — give them anything they ask, because at the bottom of the ditch a man's loyalty is the cheapest thing left in his purse.

So you go.

The bank is easier the second time; your bootprints already in it; your weight already known to its mud. You pick the stone up on the way — a flat one, river-rounded, the size of a small loaf, the size that fits the hand without thinking. The reeds part. He is where you left him. His eyes are closed. His chest is moving, very small, the way a sleeping bird's chest moves.

He opens his eyes.

It is the worst thing the morning has done to you so far. He opens his eyes, and they find yours, and there is a flicker of confusion in them — you came back, you came back for me — and then the flicker resolves into something else, and the something else is not anger. It is recognition. He sees the stone in your hand and the small still calculation on your face and he understands, with the same speed a fisherman understands the shape of his own death by drowning, what is going to happen.

He does not speak. He closes his eyes again. He turns his head very slightly into the mud — to give you a clean angle, you realise, because some long-trained part of him will not let him die without sparing you the awkwardness of an unclean stroke.

"Bismi Khoda," he whispers. In the god's name. Not the church's god. The road's.

You do the thing. You do not think about it as you do it. You will, later, but later is for later. The stone comes down once and it is enough. The cold practised thing in your hands does the work, the way the cold practised thing has been doing your work all morning, and afterwards you sit on the wet bank with your hand wet on the stone and you breathe through your mouth so the smell does not find your nose.

You wipe the stone in the reeds. You set it back where you found it. You go through his coat one more time — for what, you do not know, but the work is the same and the work is steadier than your hands without it — and find only what you found before: nothing. The men who came before you were thorough. You were thorough. The empire's wardens, when they get here, will find a beaten man without a charm and without a pouch and without a coin, killed by a stranger for what little he had on him, and the empire will tell itself an old, simple, useful story about country-road robbers.

Above you, the road. The hooves still have not rounded the bend. You hear them, closer now, three sets at a walk, but the walk is the searching walk and not yet the finding walk. You have, perhaps, fifty heartbeats.

You go up the bank. At the top, you stand on the imperial road in the colour of dawn, with the silver of a heretic in your pocket and the cooling blood of a heretic on your sleeve, and the world is the same world it was three minutes ago except that you are not, quite, the same figure walking through it.

East. The road runs east. The shrine of Vabum stands at the next turning, fifty paces back. The vineyards stretch south. The hooves are very close.
