The Cart at the Salt-Line

A dust-coloured ox-cart drawn up at the long white smear of the western salt-line, two men sitting on the cart's tail with their boots in the salt, and a small canvas-wrapped bundle in the back of the cart with the shape of a man under the canvas and a hand at the bundle's far end that the canvas has not, by the heat of the morning, quite stayed pulled over.

The river-road had taken you west by the bell of the noon and by the small particular industry of legs that had walked the previous afternoon's bell faster than the legs had wanted to. The country had thinned to the long flat brown the country went to on the approach to the western salt-line. The salt-line was a thing the country had been losing its colour into for as long as anyone at the line minded. A low ridge of small dust-coloured hills had run along your right shoulder for the past half-bell. The road, by some accident of the country's traffic, was empty of carts in both directions; the only traffic ahead was a single small ox-cart drawn up at the side of the road at the place the road met the long white smear of the salt.

The cart was the small two-wheeled kind country men used for short hauls between the country and the salt-line's loading-stages. The ox at the yoke was an old brown one with a small flat brass bell at the throat. The two men sitting on the cart's tail were the country's: country boots, country trousers, country shirts that the salt-line's wind had been bleaching for some number of mornings now. The taller of the two had a small leather wineskin at his knee. The shorter had a small piece of dried mutton in his hand. They were not, in any small particular of their sitting, men minded to be at the salt-line's edge in a hurry.

The bundle in the back of the cart was perhaps six feet long, wrapped in a piece of dusty grey canvas, tied at three places along the length with brown rope. The bundle's far end was the end the men were not sitting at. The canvas at the far end had — by the heat of the morning, or by the rope at the third tie not having been pulled quite as tight as it might have been — slipped a half-finger back from where it had been tied. Under the slipped canvas was a hand. The hand was the hand of a man perhaps forty, the small middle finger broken at the second joint at some old healing, a small green-bronze ring at the third finger that the country did not make rings of. You had seen that ring two evenings past on the counter at Karkand. The ring was at the fence's man's third finger when his hands had been flat at the counter at the small lamp's edge.

You did not, in any small motion, change your pace. The body had read the cart at perhaps a hundred and twenty paces and had not, in the reading, suggested the legs do anything different than the legs were doing. The two men on the cart's tail had not yet seen you. The road past the cart was the only road; the salt-line beyond was a long flat white you could not, by any small detour at this hour, walk around without losing the half-day. You had perhaps a quarter-bell at the present pace before you came up to them.

The shorter of the two — by the bell of his looking up the road — saw you at perhaps eighty paces. He did not, in any small motion, lift his hand. He nudged the taller's knee with his own. The taller looked up. He took a slow drink from the wineskin. He set the wineskin between his feet. He looked at the second man on the road behind you — a man you had not seen at any small turning of the head, a man the body had been registering at the soft of the shoulder-blades for the past two farsangs, walking at the same pace as your own at the small careful distance the body had been telling you was a coincidence and had not, in the telling, quite been believed. The second man on the road behind you was perhaps eighty paces also. He was, by the shape, narrow at the shoulder and tall. He had a black coat. The black coat at the lining at the third button had the small particular pull.

The taller man on the cart smiled the small slow smile of a country man who had been waiting at a piece of road for a piece of road's traffic to come up to him. He did not stand. He spread his hands a fraction in his lap, in the small open gesture a country man made to a stranger he was prepared to be very patient with. Then he said, in a voice that the salt-line's wind carried easily the eighty paces to your ears:

"Friend. The cart is at the road. The road is at the salt. The salt is the empire's. You will, by the bell of the noon, be on the road or on the cart. Bardas's man at Sadaiziche had a description by the river-road and the cart-track and the salt-line all three by the bell of the morning. The man on the road behind you has, by the half-bell, decided which of the three the description's man would come up by. He is the very small remainder of the deciding. We are the larger. Speak first."
