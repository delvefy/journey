The Pin on the Writ

A small brass pin, a folded paper with a name on it, and a culvert under the imperial road where the named man has been bleeding into the dust since first light.

The afternoon at the south-gate post had been the kind of afternoon you could put a hand on without disturbing — wardens in and out at the door, a clerk's pen the only steady noise, the post-priest writing his bell-of-noon record in the small back room with the door open onto the yard. You had answered the questions you were asked. You had given the answers a man gives at a desk that owns him for the hour. The leader with the slow eyes had not, in the end, sent for you again; his corporal had taken your account, the post-priest's hand had signed the closing line, and the small brass token they handed you for the eating-house up the street had a soup and a hunk of bread written into the back of it. You had eaten the soup. You had drunk the cup of thin red the eating-house gave with the soup. You were on your way back to the gate by the alley behind the dye-yard, in no hurry, when the corporal — Davud, by his belt-buckle — caught you at the elbow.

"Friend. The leader would have a word."

You walked back. The leader with the slow eyes was at his table now — a heavy plank trestle in the larger of the post's two rooms, a map of the country east of Sadaiziche pinned to the wall behind him with three black tacks at three known places. He did not stand. He gestured to the bench across from him. He looked at you a long beat without speaking, as if reading something on your face he had not yet had time to read at the morning's desk. Then he reached into the leather pouch at his hip and laid two things on the table between you.

One was a folded writ — quarter-folded, the Church's small seal on the outside, the name on the inside but you could read the first letter at the fold's edge: a low Arabic Hamza, the rest concealed. The other was a small brass pin, the shape of an open hand, the empire's stamp on its palm.

"There is a writ," the leader said, "from the post-priest, taken at the bell of noon. The named man is hiding in a culvert under the road at the third milestone east — a stone arch a man could lie in. He went under it before first light. He has been there since. He is, by the brother's account, bleeding. He has not come out and he will not come out, by the bell's end, on his own feet. Two of my men are at the third milestone; the third is at the river. I am one rider short. The writ requires three pinned hands at the culvert." He set his finger on the brass pin. "Davud has spoken for you. You walked here on your own boots this morning by your own account, and the post-priest's pen is satisfied. I will give you the pin until sundown. You will walk with Davud and Hamid to the third milestone. You will assist with the taking. You will not draw a weapon unless I have, by Davud's mouth, said you may. At sundown you will return the pin to this table and the writ will be back in this pouch. There is a copper at the end of it." His eyes were on yours. "Or you may say no, friend. The pin is not the kind of pin a man must take."

You took the pin. You did not, you registered after, decide to take it; the body decided. The brass was warm from his pouch. It weighed, in the palm, more than the morning's paper.

The third milestone was a half-bell at a walk. Davud and Hamid waited by the gate. The culvert under the imperial road was a stone arch the height of a stooping man, the shape of a half-egg, with a little water running out of one side from the leather pipe that fed the wagon-stop's trough. You smelled the man before you saw him. The smell was the smell of yesterday's verge, and it was older than yesterday's verge had been by an hour.

The named man inside was a thin man in a dyer's apron with a knife-cut along the meat of his thigh that he had bound badly. His name, by the writ Davud unfolded on a knee, was Hamza of the river-quay — wanted on a count of murder, his own brother, three days past in the dye-yard. The wound had been put on him by the brother before he had used the knife back. He had crawled here in the dark. He looked at the three of you over a small bright fear that you understood, in your own body, in a way Davud and Hamid did not.

The writ said: bring him in alive if he can be brought.
