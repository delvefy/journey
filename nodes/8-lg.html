The Matron at the South Stair

A small upstairs parlour at the order's seminary house in Khaboran, the south-stair window onto the courtyard's lemon-tree, a low chair by the window with a very old woman in it, and three signed-back writs already laid on the side-table in the small careful arrangement of a hand that has waited many afternoons to lay them there.

You had come up the imperial road by Hossein's grey or by the post's grey or by no grey at all, by the order's red-cord packet at the inside of the coat or by the warden-post's sealed letter folded into the same pocket or by neither, and the road had narrowed by the second milestone east of Khaboran into the small single line a man's road narrowed to when the man was, by the bell of the noon, going to a door he had not crossed in twenty years. The town came up at the slope's foot. The seminary house sat at the south end of the town behind a low wall the lemon-trees of the courtyard reached up over. The gate was open. The brother at the gate did not, by his face, ask a name. He said, in the small even voice the order's gate-keepers used, that the matron was at the parlour at the head of the south stair and that the matron had a pot of tea on at the bell of the third.

The south stair was a stair the body knew. The body did not, by any small turning of the head at the second landing, need to be told which door at the third was the parlour. The door was open. The matron was at her chair by the window. She was perhaps eighty. The hair was the white the boy of thirteen had not yet had the years to see at the temple under the dark blue scarf; the scarf today was a small careful pearl-grey of the order's outer house. The face had thinned along the bone. The hands at her lap were the same hands. The hands were folded in the same careful fold the boy of thirteen had been told, on his first morning at the south stair, was the fold the order's women used when they were minded to ask a boy a question he had not yet had the years to answer.

"Khorshid," she said. She did not stand. She did not, by any small motion, ask you in. She had asked you in by the lemon-tree at the gate and by the brother at the door and by the south stair's second landing where the third step's small flaw was the same flaw the body had read at thirteen. "Sit, child. The tea is at the side-board. The writs are at the table."

The chair opposite hers was a small low chair the order's parlours had for the woman who came in for the afternoon's reading. The side-board was the small low side-board the order kept under the south window with a brown earthenware pot and two cups and a plate of dried apricots. The writs at the table were the three writs Hossein had laid out at the wayside post at the bell of sundown — the covering sheet at the head of them turned now to face her chair and the red cord folded a half-turn at the spine — or, if the red cord had not been the hand the morning came up by, the single sealed letter from the Khaboran circuit-clerk laid open at its second leaf. The table was the same long oak table the matron's mother had laid her hand at when the order's seventh house at Khaboran was the order's first.

"Sit," she said again. She watched you cross the room. She watched the foot at the third step from the door — the body's small habit at the right knee, the habit she had set into the boy's gait at fourteen with a thin willow-switch and a small particular patience — and her face did not move. "The column has been open four years. The column has been open at half-pay because the order's habit is to read at half-pay the man who has not, by his own choosing, written the column closed in his own hand. I have read at the column's head, these last four years, the sentence presumed living, location unknown. The sentence is now, by the morning's reading, the column. The column is now, by the morning's reading, you."

She did not, by her face, lean. The lemon-tree at the courtyard had a single yellow fruit at the upper bough. The pot at the side-board steamed.

"Three things, child, the order has a hand on. The order has the writs at the table — the three under the red cord, or the one under the wax. The order has the column at the paybook, which has, by Father Karim's careful hand, gone into Khaboran's south drawer the morning the dawn-cart came down with the green linen. And the order has, in the matron's small particular keeping, the day you came up the south stair with the boy from Sayedabad's hand at your shoulder, and the morning you sat for the first reading at this table with the lemon-tree's then-third bough at the window. I have those three things. You have, this afternoon, only the choice of which of the three the matron's small careful arrangement on the table is for. Speak first, child. The bell of the fourth is the bell I take the pot off the side-board."

The body sat. The chair held the body in the same shape it had held at fourteen. The matron's hands at her lap did not move.
