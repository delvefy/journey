The Pedlar's Donkey

A grey midday, a small fire by a wayside shrine, and an old pedlar who does not, by trade, take notice.

The morning had become an afternoon. You did not, when you looked back at it, quite know how. The walking had been the kind of walking that does its own thinking — boots one in front of the other, the country opening and folding around you, the sun climbing through a thin pale sky toward a noon it never seemed to quite reach. You had drifted off the empire's road; or you had stayed on it and let the road drift; the difference did not, in any honest accounting of the morning, matter. The Cup-Bearer's roadside shrine had come up under your right hand at some turning you had not registered, and a small fire was burning at its foot. A man sat beside the fire. A small bony donkey was hobbled in the lee of the shrine, methodically working its jaw on nothing.

The man at the fire looked up at you the way men look up at the colour of the sky — to take note, and to take the colour, and to file neither against any particular morning. He was old. He had a grey beard worn long and parted in the middle by what may have been habit or what may have been the wind on a hundred roads. He wore a coat that had been many coats. A small leather case the shape of a long-necked instrument leaned at his shoulder against the shrine's stone. He nodded at you. He pointed with his chin at a place by the fire that was not occupied. He did not speak.

You sat. The fire was small but it had been built clever, with the smoke going up the lee of the shrine's stone so that the wind, when it found the smoke, scattered it before it could be seen at any distance. The pedlar — for he was certainly a pedlar, his pack was on the ground at his feet, half-open, showing the dull edges of dyed wool and the rim of a cheap tin mirror — had a small clay pot on the fire and a wooden cup at his hand. He filled the cup without ceremony and held it out to you across the flame.

"Goat-bone broth," he said. "It is not for selling. The road is long and the bones cost nothing."

You took the cup. You drank. The broth was hot and salt and tasted of an animal you had not eaten in living memory. The old man watched the fire and did not watch you. He had the patience of someone who had sat by many fires and outlived most of the men he had shared them with, and who did not, by trade, take particular notice of the men currently sharing one.

"You came up the spur," he said, in a tone that asked no question. "The boots are not eastern boots. The eastern boots come up the road. Coming up the spur is the way of a traveller who has had a morning. I am Razin. Razin the cup, when there is a cup. Razin the pedlar, otherwise. From here to the coast and back, twice a year, fifteen years."

He let the silence be a silence for a long beat. He did not, when it grew long, fill it.

"I do not ask the road its business," he said at last, almost as an apology for his own quiet. "The road brings men, the road carries them. I sell what they will buy of me, I trade for what I cannot sell, and I sleep where the fire-wood lets me. If you have come for news, friend, I have news. If you have come for cord or for wax or for a small mirror I have those. If you have come for the company of a man who will not ask your name and not remember your face by morning, you have come to a man's fire who has been that man some thousand nights, and it is no work to be him this one." He smiled briefly into the fire. The smile was almost not there. "Tell me what to be for you, traveller. I have been most things."

The donkey shifted its weight. A small wind came down the spur and turned in the lee of the shrine. The shrine was old — a thumb-pillar of stone marked with the slow concentric ring that meant Vabum's cup — and the pillar's top had been worn shallow by the rains of many years, and rain that had collected in the shallow was full of dead pine-needles and a single dark feather. You looked into the shallow for a long beat. You did not, when you looked away, know what you had been looking for.

You looked at Razin. Razin did not look at you. He waited. He had nowhere, in the unhurried economy of his afternoon, that he needed to be.
