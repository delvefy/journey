3-d · The Third Milestone

The Third Milestone

A lie sent west, light enough to ride after. A truth left east, heavy enough to stay.

You tell them with the kind of useful, half-frightened detail that a real witness gives. A man on a brown horse, leaning bad in the saddle, his coat dark across the front. He had passed you at the third milestone behind you. You had thought him drunk. You had stepped off the road to let him by, and he had grunted something at you that you took for a curse, and then he had ridden hard west — too hard, you had thought, for a man so badly seated; a man riding like that does not ride for long.

The leader of the wardens listens without expression. He glances once at the road behind you — at the long unbroken pale strip of it, where there are no fresh hoofprints going west — and you say it before he can ask: "I have been on the road perhaps a quarter hour. The wind has been at the dust." You gesture at the air, at the thin dry skein of grit moving along the verge in the dawn breeze. It is true. It would, in fact, lift hoofprints in that time. You have the small clean horror of a person who finds that the world is conspiring to make their lie plausible.

The leader's slow eyes move from the road to your face and back. He is not, you understand suddenly, looking for the truth. He is looking for the cost of the lie. He is calculating how badly it will hurt his morning if he trusts you and is wrong.

Behind him, the youngest rider has reached the verge. He stoops, picks up something with his gloved fingers, looks at it, and lets it drop. Then he climbs back up to his horse without speaking. You do not let yourself look at what he dropped. You do not let yourself look at the reeds.

The leader makes his decision. He clucks his tongue and turns his horse's head west. "If you have wasted our morning, friend," he says — not loud, almost an aside — "we will come and find you in Sadaiziche by the noon bell, and you will sit with us at the warden-house and explain how you wasted it."

"Yes, sir," you say.

The third rider gives you a small thin nod. The youngest does not look at you at all. They turn their horses and they ride — at the controlled trot of three men who do not yet know if they are being mocked — back toward Rabad and the third milestone where a brown horse is not.

You stand on the empty road. You count again, the way you counted before — their breaths and not yours — until the hooves are smaller than the wind. Then you crouch at the edge of the bank, and you call down softly into the reeds.

"They are gone."

The reeds move. The man rises out of them slowly, mud-faced, a creature half-born of the ditch, and his eyes — which had been pointed at the sky in case the sky was the last thing they would point at — find yours with a new attention. He looks at the road behind you, where the hoofprints go west. He looks at the road in front of you, where the hoofprints have not yet been laid east. He understands.

"Khoda," he says quietly — the formal old word for the god of the road, older than the church's word for it — "you sent them west."

"Get up," you say. "They will work it out within a farsang. Maybe two."

His good hand goes to your shoulder for the climb up the bank. The cold of the mud comes off him into your sleeve. At the top, the road stretches east, pale and waiting, two and a half farsang to Sadaiziche along its known length. He sways once and steadies himself against your hip.

"East?" he asks, and there is in the word the bare beginning of what could become a plan, if either of you had time for a plan.
