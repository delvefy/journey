The Brick Floor

A one-room warden-post a half-farsang east of the gate, a brazier set in its corner, and a man already on the table whose questioning is, by the leader's mouth, an errand requiring a second pair of hands.

The affidavit had paid in stamped copper from the Church's own purse and the leader with the slow eyes had taken its measure as a small but reliable wage and decided, by the slope of his eye when he set the coppers in your palm, that the wage might be a beginning. He had not, that morning, said so. He had, by midday, said so. The corporal Davud had been sent up the eating-house street to find you over your soup, and when you had finished the soup the leader had set a clean folded paper on the table next to your bowl — a writ of attendance, signed by the post-priest, with a small brass pin clipped to the corner — and had said, in the tone of a man stating a fact and not requiring an answer, that the wagon-stop east of the second milestone had at present a second man on its table, taken at the river before first light, and that the priest of the question who was riding back from the country had been delayed by an hour and would not, for that hour, have the second pair of hands his work in the second hour required. The leader did not look up from his ledger when he said this. He did not need to.

The wagon-stop east of the second milestone was a one-room post built into the side of the wagon-yard, brick floor, a small high window the colour of a slate sky. You knocked. The door was opened by a warden you did not know — a thin man with a clean tabard — who looked at the writ on your collar and stepped aside without a word. You went in.

The room smelled of wet wool and cold brick. A brazier in the far corner was beginning to warm; the coals were red but the wood at the top was still raw and smoking. A long plank table had been set across the middle of the room, and on it lay a man perhaps twenty-five, stripped to the waist, his wrists bound to the table's two front legs by strips of soft cord, his ankles bound to the back. His eyes were open and on the ceiling. There was a small star-shaped brand at his temple. A silver fish-and-tail charm lay on a folded square of linen on the table by his feet, where the warden who had brought him in had set it for the writ. He had not been touched yet.

The brown-blood robe was hanging from a peg on the wall. The man wearing it was not yet wearing it. He was at the corner table by the brazier in his under-shirt and his trousers, washing his hands in a basin, his back to you. The leather case stood open on the corner table next to the basin. Inside the case, neatly racked, were the instruments — not so many as you might have thought, nor so few. Mehrdad turned at the small sound of the door closing behind you. He did not look surprised. He had been told. His eyes were the same eyes they had been at the crossroads of some other man's morning that you had not, by this particular accountancy of yours, walked.

"Friend." Pleasant. "The leader has lent you for the hour. I am grateful. The work in the second hour does want two hands. The first hour does not; the first hour is mine, and a man does not, in his trade, want company at the laying-out of his hands. Sit on the bench by the window. There is water in the small jug. When the second hour begins I will call you."

He turned back to his basin. He worked the soap into the webbing of his fingers as if there were no man on the table.

The man on the table did not look at you. He did not look at Mehrdad. He looked at a place on the ceiling where a brick had been replaced once and the new brick had not, by the candle-smoke of years, gone the same dark colour as the old. His breathing was steady. He had been here, perhaps, since first light. He had had, by the brick at the ceiling and the smell of his own fear, time to settle.

The brazier breathed once, a small dry breath, and put up a small grey curl into the slate light of the window.
