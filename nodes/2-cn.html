The Reeds

Three riders, an empty road, and a body breathing under the mud at the bottom of your morning.

You pressed him down. The reeds, which had folded around him like reluctant attendants, accepted him fully now — your hand on his good shoulder, his head turning into the wet bank with what may have been the last of his strength. The charm at his throat was the thing the riders would see first. You took it between thumb and forefinger and dug it down into the mud, then brought up a fistful of cold ditch-clay and worked it into the silver until the little fish was only another wet stone. You smeared the same mud across the line of his collarbone, across his cheek, across the back of his hand. He did not flinch. His eyes — open, still open — tracked yours with the patient attention of a creature who has placed his life in another's keeping and is now waiting to see what kind of keeping he has bought.

You climbed back up the bank, careful with your bootprints, walking in the same places you had come down. At the top of the bank you stepped onto the road's pale crown and turned your face east, away from Rabad, away from the bend behind which the hooves were growing into shapes.

The first rider rounded the bend. He saw you at once — a single dark figure on the empty road at the colour of dawn, exactly where blood had been spilled on the verge — and his hand went to the rein in the small particular way of a man for whom the next gesture is the sword. The second rider rose behind him, then the third. Hard leather coats, the badge of road-wardens, the additional white cord at the shoulder that — without knowing how you knew — you knew marked a man riding under church writ. Hunters. Not merely keepers of the road.

They came up at a walk. They were in no hurry now; the verge had begun to answer them. The leader stopped his horse three paces from you. He was older than the others, narrow-faced, with the slow eyes of a man who missed very little. He looked at you, at the blood, at the reeds, at the road behind you. He did not look long at the reeds. He looked longer at you.

"Friend," he said, in a voice that was not friendly. "You are early on the road."

"Yes," you said. Your voice was steadier than you would have credited.

"Did you pass anyone? A wounded man. Older. A coat like a tradesman's, but better wool."

The lie was in your mouth before you decided whether to tell it. Your tongue tasted it — the shape, the cost — and waited for you to choose. Below the road, in the reeds, the man did not move. The youngest of the riders had dismounted and was walking, with no great urgency, toward the verge where the blood was. He was younger than the others. His gloved hand had come up to his collar and was touching, very briefly, the badge of his rank as if for steadiness — the gesture of a man trying very hard to be a man on a morning that was not going the way it had been promised.

The third rider, who had not yet spoken since the bend, leaned down from his horse and said something in the leader's ear. It was short. The leader's eyes did not leave yours. He nodded once.

"We are not asking idly, friend," he said — the word had lost a small amount of its earlier formality. "The man we are looking for came east on this road within the hour. The blood at the verge is his. The bootprints down the bank are his. The bootprints up the bank are someone's. We would like to know whose."

You said nothing at first. You let the silence go on for a beat longer than was strictly comfortable, and the leader's slow eyes narrowed by a fraction, and you felt — somewhere under your ribs, in a small useful place you had not known about until this morning — a steadying of your breath, the way a sword settles in the hand of a man who has held swords before. The road was, in this moment, very simply yours. The wardens were three; their attention was three different things at once; the wounded man was a heartbeat under the mud; the bend was a hundred paces of empty light behind them; and you were — for one cold short interval — the figure who could decide what the morning meant.

The youngest reached the verge. He stooped. He picked up something with his gloved fingers, looked at it, and let it drop without speaking.

Three breaths. You take them.
