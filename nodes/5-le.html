Father Karim's Ledger

A back room behind a curtained door, an iron strong-box set on a clerk's stool with its key already in the lock, and the paymaster of an order's southern circuit reading a column of figures in a hand he has known for half his working life.

The post-priest had asked you to wait, with a small particular bend of the head that had not been the bend a man gives to a witness whose statement has been signed and is finished. He had taken the corporal aside and said something quietly. The corporal had gone out into the yard. The post-priest had said to you, in the small low voice of a man giving an instruction that was also a kindness, that there was a paymaster in from Khaboran on the second-hour cart with a small piece of work that touched the post's ledger and your morning was, by the cart's bell, the morning the paymaster had asked for, and would you, friend, wait. You had waited.

The paymaster came in by the back curtain. He was perhaps fifty-five. He had the soft careful step of a man who had spent his working life crossing the same floor between a strong-box and a desk, and the smell at his coat as he passed was the smell of seal-wax and iron and the small clean honey-smell of an old prayer-book that had never quite left his trade. He carried a clerk's leather satchel under his arm. He carried, at his hip, on a short brass chain, the kind of long flat key the question-order's paybooks were kept under in Khaboran. He set the satchel on the small table by the wall. He did not, yet, sit. He turned. He looked at you.

He had the eyes of a man who had had a long week. He let those eyes settle on you for the length of perhaps two breaths. He did not, by his face, show what the settling read.

"Friend." He took out a chair from under the table, set it across the table from a second chair, and gestured to the further one. You sat. He sat. He opened the satchel. He took out the heavy paybook bound in brown leather and the small iron strong-box and set them at the table's centre. He laid the strong-box's key into the strong-box's lock and did not, yet, turn it. He opened the paybook to a page he had marked at the spine with a strip of red silk thread.

"I am Karim. Father Karim of the southern circuit. I have been the paymaster of the order of the question at Khaboran for nineteen winters and I had, four years past, the unpleasing morning of writing the half-pay column for a field-man of the order who did not, by his last reporting day, return." He turned the page round on the table so you could read it. The page was a long single column of figures, season by season, in the small careful hand the order's clerks had used at Khaboran since the empire's first inventory of the province. The name at the head of the column was a name he had not yet, in his voice, said. He laid a finger at the head of the column. "I am told by the post-priest that a man matching the description has come into his post this morning and given an account. The post-priest is a careful man. The post-priest has done his work; I am here, friend, to do mine."

He kept his finger at the head of the column. He did not, even now, say the name aloud — but the column under his finger was four years long, half-pay every season, with a small marked notation in the margin of the third year that read, in the order's old shorthand, presumed living, location unknown. The last entry was for the spring of this year. The spring of this year had paid out half a soldier's wage to a column with no man at it. Four years; half a soldier's wage each season; the strong-box at the table's centre.

Karim folded his hands.

"There are three ways," he said, "by which a column of this kind is closed at my desk. I will not, friend, ask you which of the three you would like. I will set the three on the table and you will, by your own mouth at the end of the bell, lay your hand on the one you prefer. The order is not, in my office, a kind office. It is, on the question of its own field-men, a precise one. Precision is what I have to give you. The Khaboran cart leaves at the bell of four. You and I are at the table, friend, until the bell of four. The strong-box is full enough that all three doors fit through it. The paybook is open enough that all three lines can be written under your name in my own hand by the time the cart's wheels are at the yard." He folded his hands tighter. "The post-priest will not be coming back into this room. The corporal at the door has been told. The afternoon is, in every direction, ours."

The strong-box ticked once, very faintly, in the way a small lock ticks when the brass cools. The four-year column on the page lay in front of you under the paymaster's finger like a sentence half-written that you had not, in any other room, been given the choice of finishing.
