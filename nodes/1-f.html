The Man in the Ditch

The imperial road. A grey dawn coming.

You stand. The body you have woken into is lighter than you expected, narrow at the shoulder, with long dark hair that has come unbraided in the night and clings, damp, to the side of your neck. There is a soreness across your back that suggests you have, recently, been moving heavy things — or being moved, heavily, by other people. The purse on your hip clinks. Four coins, by the feel of them. Three small, one larger. You leave them where they are. You are aware, without remembering why, that a woman alone on the imperial road at this hour is a specific kind of problem to a specific kind of man, and you should keep walking.

The road runs east toward Sadaiziche, away from the yellow lights of Rabad behind you. You choose east. You do not know why, exactly, except that west feels like the direction you were carried from, and there are some directions a body refuses without explanation. Your boots — and they are good boots, soft Persian leather, scuffed but not cheap, cut a little too large for your feet as if they had not been bought for you — find the road's pale crown and begin.

You walk perhaps a quarter farsang. The night begins to grey. The hills on either side resolve into low folded country: oak scrub on the slopes, vineyards stripped for winter on the flats, a single shepherd's fire smoking a long way off. You pass a roadside shrine to one of the old gods — Vabum the Cup-Bearer, you somehow know, though the name surfaces without context — its little stone pillar smeared with dark fingerprints, an offering of bread gone to rot at its foot. You touch your fingertips to the stone in passing without knowing why, and find them already half-raised before your other half-mind tells them to stop. You pass a milestone. SADAIZICHE 2 ½ FARSANG.

And then you hear the moan.

It comes from below the level of the road, from a stand of dry reeds along the irrigation ditch that runs alongside. You stop. You listen. It comes again — a thin, drawn-out sound, the kind a man makes when he has been making it for a long time and has stopped expecting to be heard.

You go down the bank, slow, one hand to the muddy slope. The reeds part. There is a man on his side in the wet at the bottom of the ditch, one arm beneath him at a wrong angle, the front of his coat black with what is not yet dry. He has a thin beard going to grey. He has a fine wool coat that someone has gone through — pockets pulled inside-out, the lining slit by a knife in three quick lines. He has, around his neck, a small silver charm on a thong: a fish, biting its own tail. You do not know the symbol. You know that you should not know it, and that knowing it would be dangerous.

His eyes are open. They find you. They focus, slowly, on the unbraided hair, the line of your throat, the small wet handprint on your trouser leg.

"Sister," he says, in a voice like a door scraping on stone. It is the Khvordemian word — khahar — but said softly, the way a man says it when he is not sure whether the woman standing over him is one of his.

You crouch. He is younger than the beard suggests. He has been beaten badly about the ribs. His good hand finds your wrist and grips it with surprising force, the calluses of a man who has worked.

"They are coming back," he says. "Listen. Listen. I heard them turn at the bridge. They will see the blood on the road. The river-fish — they cannot find me here. If they find me here —" His grip tightens. He does not finish. He does not need to. There is a kind of being-found that ends in a pyre, and the empire has not been shy about lighting them.

You listen. And yes — there, very faint, but unmistakable now that you are listening for it — the sound of hooves on the imperial road. Two horses, maybe three. Coming this way, but not fast. Searching.

The man's hand loosens. His head tips back against the cold mud. His eyes do not leave yours. "Khahar," he says, and now it sounds less like a guess and more like an asking. "Whatever you do — do it now."

The light is still grey. The riders are not yet at the bend. Above the ditch, the road waits — empty, and very long, and yours.
