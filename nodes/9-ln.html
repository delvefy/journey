The Lieutenant at the Map-Table

The senior warden-post's morning room a half-bell after Faramarz has ridden east, a long map-table at the inner wall under a hanging oil-lamp with a southern-circuit map pinned at the corners and three small lead weights at the markings, the dust-blue coat with the silver olive at the shoulder set on the back of a chair at the table's near end, and a young officer of perhaps twenty-six in the dust-blue of the imperial border force at the map's third milestone south of Sadaiziche with the morning's first dispatch from the bishop's clerk under his elbow.

The captain had ridden east at the bell of the dawn. He had said, at the desk's lamp the previous evening, that the morning's first ride was the morning's first ride and the desk at the back room was the desk at the back room, and that the lieutenant of the second tower would be up from Khaboran by the bell of the second hour of the morning with the morning's first dispatch from the bishop's circuit-clerk and a small particular packet from the order's drawer that the captain's own hand had not yet, by the morning's reading, set into the brown-leather folder. The captain had laid the dust-blue coat with the silver olive at the shoulder over the back of the chair at the map-table's near end. He had said, in passing at the door, that the coat fit a man of forty with a small particular habit at the right knee. He had gone out by the inner door at the bell of the dawn.

The post's brother at the gate had brought up at the bell of the first a small pot of cold strong tea and a piece of yesterday's bread and a small carved cup of dried apricots. The post-priest at the second door had laid the morning's daybook open at the long pine clerk's desk along the back wall and had laid a small folded note at the inner pocket of the dust-blue coat at the chair without lifting the coat from the chair. The corporal at the stable had brought your grey across to the inner yard at the bell of the half-first and had stood at the rein at the small careful waiting of a corporal whose officer had said the brother at the desk was the brother in whose office the captain's morning's column was at this morning's hand.

The map at the inner wall was a southern-circuit map the order's draughtsman at Khaboran had drawn ten years past and the captain's own hand had pinned three small fresh marks into yesterday afternoon — a small dark blot at Bagh-e-Sang, a small careful red ring at the salt-line west of Karkand, a small careful green ring at the cold spring above the southern spur. There were three small lead weights at the map's corners. The fourth corner had nothing at it. The fourth corner was the country east of the dyer's town that the empire's map did not, by the draughtsman's own hand, much have edges of.

The lieutenant came in by the inner door at the bell of the second. He was perhaps twenty-six. The coat was the dust-blue of the imperial border force at the small particular careful pressing of a younger officer's coat the second tower at Khaboran had been minded to keep at its standard these last three years. The silver olive at his shoulder was the silver olive of a lieutenant of the second tower. The hair under the cap was a small careful dark brown. The hands at his sides were a fencing-master's hands at a small careful interval the officer had been working at his sabre at every dawn for three winters. He stopped at the four paces from the map-table. He saluted the chair at the table's near end at the small particular salute the captain's officers used at a captain's chair when the captain's chair had a covering coat at it.

"Brother." He did not, by his face, lift the voice. "Lieutenant Hosrov of the second tower at Khaboran. The captain's orders at the dawn were for me to bring up to the brother at the desk the morning's first dispatch from the bishop's circuit-clerk and the small particular packet from the order's drawer, and to wait at the map-table at no greater volume than the post has at the bell of the second for the brother to read." He laid the dispatch — a single folded leaf of country paper at the small careful wax of the bishop's clerk — on the map-table at the third milestone south of Sadaiziche where the dust-blue ring had been pinned yesterday. He laid the small particular packet — a brown paper wrapper a hand wide at the small careful red cord of the order — at the corner that had nothing at it. He stood at the table's far edge.

The dispatch's wax read at the small letters of the bishop's clerk. The packet's red cord was the red cord the order's drawer at Khaboran tied at a packet whose first leaf was a name the order's pen had not, in four years, been minded to write at any further paper.

The lieutenant did not look at the dispatch. He did not look at the packet. He stood at the table's far edge at the small careful waiting of an officer who knew the small careful particular of a packet at a corner of a map.

"The dispatch," he said, "is the morning's first column out of the bishop's circuit. The bishop's pen has named a small dust-blue riding at the brick-floor post at the bell of the noon today for the matter of the half-fold at Behesht. The packet at the corner is the order's drawer's small particular reading of the same matter at the column at the head of the order's southern circuit's open columns. The captain's word at the dawn was that the brother at the desk has the morning's first hand on both — the imperial paper at the dispatch and the order's paper at the packet. The post's grey is at the inner yard. The lieutenant of the second tower is at the map-table. The map is at the wall. The fourth corner is the corner the empire does not, this morning, much have edges of. Speak first, brother. The bell of the third is, by the captain's word, the bell the morning's hand has."
