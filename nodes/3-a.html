3-a · Kinsman

Kinsman

A lie on the imperial road, told plain, with both hands open.

You speak before they have finished arriving. The lie is in your mouth and you let it out the way a stonemason lets a chisel-stroke go — once, cleanly, with no second blow.

"Forgive me, sir," you say, and you gesture down to the bundle in the lee of the milestone. "My kinsman. He fell from his cart at the turning of the night. Word came to me in Rabad before the lamps were out. I have walked from there in the dark to find him."

The leader does not answer at once. His horse blows steam between you. He looks at the milestone, at the shape under your coat, at the smear of blood on the verge, and then at you — and his slow eyes settle there longer than is comfortable.

"A fall," he says.

"A fall," you say. "He had been drinking. He keeps a small vineyard above Sadaiziche. The cart is somewhere east on the road, I think. I have not yet looked for it. I found him first." You make yourself stop. A long lie wants more words; this is how long lies are caught.

The leader's gaze flicks to the bundle. The wounded man, with what is either grace or the simple economy of a body running short of blood, makes a small sound at exactly that moment — half-cough, half-moan — the kind of sound a man might make if he had spent the night in a ditch with broken ribs from a fall.

It is convincing because it is the truth, only with the wrong cause attached.

The youngest rider has dismounted. He is looking at the verge, at the blood, at the line of bootprints going down the bank and back up again. He does not say anything. He looks at the leader. The leader does not look at him.

"What is his name," the leader says — not asking it; setting it down between you like a coin on a counter.

The name comes to you, the way the word in your mouth came to you at waking, from no place you can find. "Daiyan," you say. "Daiyan of the small vineyard above Sadaiziche."

The leader's mouth moves — not a smile; an acknowledgement that you have said a thing without flinching. He glances once more at the bundle. Then he turns in the saddle and looks east along the road, the way he is going. Then he looks west along the road, the way he came from.

"We are looking for a different man," he says. "Older. River-trade. Not a vineyard man." He says river with the particular weight of a man who has used the same word in three different rooms this week, each time meaning the same particular fish.

"I have seen no one but my kinsman," you say.

The third rider, who has not spoken, leans down from his horse and says something in the leader's ear. It is short. The leader nods once. The youngest rider, after a long quiet beat, walks back to his horse.

The leader looks at you a final time. The look is not friendly. The look is not unfriendly. The look is what a man does when he files a thing for later.

"Then walk your kinsman to Sadaiziche, friend," he says, "and walk him quickly. The road is uncertain this morning."

He clucks his tongue. The horses begin to move.

And then — when the leader has already half-turned — he reins in, and looks back, and the air goes out of you. "We will be in Sadaiziche by the noon bell," he says, "at the warden-house by the south gate. Bring your Daiyan there before the bell, friend. So that we may write the road's record properly."

It is not a request. It is the lie being held, gently, between his fingers, where you both can see it.

He rides on. The hooves dwindle eastward. In the cooling silence on the road, you turn to the man at your feet, and the man at your feet opens his eyes, and the lie that just kept him alive begins, very quickly, to make its second set of demands.
