The Bond-Boy in the Byre

A stone byre roofless at one end, an evening coming down on country the empire has not bothered to map, and a small body breathing in the corner where the lamp will not reach.

The afternoon had spent itself the way afternoons do when you have nowhere in particular to be in them — slowly, and then not at all. You had walked off the line of any road a clear man could give a name to; the line you walked instead was the kind a body chooses when the bones have agreed, without being asked, that an inn at a gate would be the wrong end to a day like this one. The country east of Sadaiziche thins quickly. Stone walls go down to dry walls and dry walls go down to lines of thorn, and the thorns end at the edge of an old grazing-ground long since gone over to the goats and to the rooks. The sun, when you looked up at it, was already a hand off the western ridge and the colour of the sky over your shoulder had begun to make the small particular promise that, by the time you found a roof, the roof would be more roof than choice.

You found the byre at the bottom of a fold, half-walled into the hillside on the lee. The roof was sound at the back end, gone at the front, two long beams across the dry-stone and a layer of black thatch that had survived in the lee what it had not survived in the open. The doorway had no door. You stooped through. The smell inside was old wool and older dust and a faint sourness — sweat, fresh, recent — and you did not, for a long beat, move.

A small breath caught itself in the dark at the back.

"I will not hurt you," you said, the words coming out of your mouth in the voice the morning had set there: low, careful, a stranger's voice that did not lie. "I am looking for a roof. The dark is coming. I do not need the corner."

A silence. Then a movement. A small body — a boy, twelve years old or thereabouts, thin in the way underfed children are thin — came forward into the grey light of the doorway, one step, and stopped. He was bare to the waist. There was a brass collar at his throat — narrow, the bond-collar of a Sadaiziche house, the merchant-mark stamped into the front of it where any warden on any road could read the owner from twenty paces. His left eye was closed and the swelling along the cheekbone was the colour swelling goes when it has been a day in the making. His fingers on the right hand were splinted with two slips of reed and a twist of dirty cord. He had been running. He had been running long enough that the running had used most of him up.

"Friend," he said. The word a child says when it has been taught the polite word for a stranger and is using the only polite word it has left. "I am running away. I will not stay if you do not want me here. I am running to my mother's people at the coast." He swallowed. "I have not eaten. The collar comes off if the cord at the back is cut. The master is Tirzah, in the dye-yard street; I will not say the house. I beg you to not, friend, take me back."

He was holding himself very straight, the way children do when they think their straightness is what is being judged. Outside, behind you, a long way off and going the wrong direction, a dog barked once and was silent. The lamp-oil at the bottom of your pack would last you, by careful use, until the moon was up. The bread was three palmfuls. The small knife — the practised one, that you had not, in the morning, drawn — would cut the cord at the back of a brass collar in one motion if you held the cord taut against the inside of the brass.

The empire's law on a bond-collar was the empire's law on a bond-collar. The Church post at the third milestone had a slot in the door for collars exactly the size of this one.
