2-d · The Climb

The Climb

A wounded man, a milestone, and three horses still hidden by the bend.

The decision moves through you before you have time to weigh it. You crouch lower, you take his good hand in yours, and you settle his arm across your shoulders. He is heavier than he looks, the way the badly hurt are always heavier than they look — the body, once unwilling, becomes weather.

"Forgive me, sister," he manages. "I cannot walk."

"Then you will be carried," you say, in a voice that surprises you — flat, practised, the voice of a woman who has lifted bodies before. You have no memory of any such lifting. The voice is in your throat anyway, ready, like an old coat hanging in a doorway you do not remember.

You strain. He cries — small, swallowed — and your knees nearly fold under the both of you. You set your jaw, you find the dry stone in your spine that your body knows about and you do not, and you stand. The world tilts and then steadies. You begin the climb.

The bank is the first problem. It is not steep but it is wet, and the reeds part and close under your boots in ways that hide the holes between their roots. You go up sideways, a crab's climb, his arm hooked over your neck and your hair coming further unbraided with every step. Twice you nearly go down with him. The second time, you brace against the milestone — the same milestone you passed only minutes ago, SADAIZICHE 2 ½ FARSANG — and feel it accept your weight like an old, reluctant friend.

Then the road. The road is the second problem.

The hooves are nearer now. Three horses; you are sure of it now; the rhythms of three. The riders have not yet rounded the bend ahead but the bend is a hundred paces, no more, and the road behind you stretches a clean unbroken line to where they will come from. Two heartbeats and they will be in sight.

You lay the man flat in the lee of the milestone, on the road's downhill side, where its weathered hip throws a thin sleeve of shadow. You drape your own coat over him to flatten his shape against the stone. The silver charm at his throat — that small fish swallowing its own tail — you lift on its thong and slip down inside the neck of your shirt. The cold of it against your collarbone is shocking, the way a wedding ring is shocking on a stranger's hand.

You straighten. You take a breath you do not know if your lungs are ready for. A woman alone on the imperial road of the Khvordemian Empire in the grey before dawn, with a heretic dying at her feet and the empire's hooves growing louder by the moment, is a particular kind of figure to come around a bend on. You understand this. You did not have to be told.

The man's eyes are still open. They are watching you with an attention that hurts, the way a kind dog watches a man putting on his coat.

"Whatever happens," he says, low, in the Khvordemian — and now he is speaking in the practised rhythm of someone who has rehearsed what to say at the end, "I was on the road. I had no fish at my throat. I gave you nothing." He coughs, and the coughing is wet. "And — khahar — thank you. Whoever you were before today."

You almost laugh. He has, by some accident of phrasing, named the only thing you also do not know.

From the bend, very near now, the dark crown of a rider's helm begins to rise into view, the way a sun rises out of a bad sea.

You have three breaths. You take them.
