A Man's Pockets

A heretic's silver, a morning of small theft, and a road that asks no questions of a tradesman's pace.

You worked fast. Your hands knew how to do this, which was the second thing that morning that had lifted small cold hairs along your arms. You cut the thong of the charm — there was a small knife folded into the lining of your own coat that you had not registered until your fingers reached for it — and the silver fish came free into your palm with a wetness you did not pause to examine. You went through what was left of his pockets, which was little: the men who came before you had been thorough. You found a single small wax-stoppered clay phial, no larger than the joint of your thumb. You pocketed it without thinking why. You found a folded scrap of vellum, three lines of writing you could not read in the half-light. You found a copper ring with no stone, a whetstone, three more small coins. You left him his boots and his coat: to roll the body would have left marks in the mud, and the riders were a hundred breaths away.

You went up the bank. You did not look back. You stepped onto the road and you kept your face turned forward and you walked — not too fast; never too fast on a road; a fast walker invites questions — east, toward Sadaiziche, away from the bend that was beginning to clatter with the unmistakable approach of three horses.

The hooves came up behind you and you did not turn. You counted their walking — three sets at a search-pace — and you let them count yours. They would see a single figure on the road, moving east at the pace of an ordinary morning. They would see the smear of blood on the verge behind you, which they were presumably arriving at now, and they would see your back, and they would make their calculation. The calculation was this: a figure who had just robbed a wounded man did not walk east in plain sight at a tradesman's pace. Therefore the figure ahead of them was not that figure. Therefore the figure ahead of them was the morning's first innocent traveller, who had, by bad luck, walked past a verge where blood had been recently spilled.

It was a calculation you had made, or your body had made for you, with a coolness that frightened you slightly when you had time to examine it.

The hooves stopped at the milestone. There was a pause, the length of three horses' breaths, and then a quiet word, and two of the three sets of hooves moved on, eastward, behind you. The third stayed at the verge. To go down the bank, presumably. The youngest, you guessed. The boy.

The two horses came up at a controlled walk. They drew level with you. You did not look at them. You kept your pace.

"Friend," the leader said, from above.

You looked up at him then. You did not flinch. You composed your face into the small mild expression of a traveller who had noticed three armed riders and was mildly relieved that they were not bandits.

"Sir."

"You are early on the road."

"Yes, sir. I am for Sadaiziche, for the morning market." You shrugged — a small economical shrug, not a performance. "My uncle, in Rabad, sent me ahead with what coin we had. He keeps a small vineyard above the third milestone. He needs new wax-stoppers, and a length of cord for the press. I am to be back by dusk."

The lie was in your mouth and you let it out the way a tradesman lets out his change — counted, but without making a show of the counting. The leader watched you. He glanced back along the road, at the milestone, at the youngest dismounting at the verge. He did not, you noticed, look at your pocket where the silver fish was. He did not, you noticed, look at your hip where the heavier purse was. He looked at your face, and your hands, and your boots — particularly your boots, those good Persian leather boots that fit you a little too well today and a little too loosely yesterday — and he filed all three.

"Walk on, friend," he said. "Sadaiziche is two and a half farsang. The market is opening."

"Sir."

He clucked his tongue. He and the third rider moved past you at a slow trot. The third rider gave you a small thin nod as he passed — not friendly; an acknowledgment that you and he had, this morning, agreed to walk in the same direction without trouble. They drew ahead of you. The hooves grew smaller. The road opened.

You kept your pace. The silver burned cold in your pocket. The phial knocked against it with each step. The vellum scrap was folded against your hip and you could feel it there, the way you could feel a blade pressed too close. The shrine of Vabum the Cup-Bearer stood at the next turning of the road, fifty paces ahead, and Sadaiziche lay two and a half farsang beyond it on a morning that was, for now, still yours to spend.

Behind you, very faint — perhaps imagined; perhaps not — the youngest at the verge had begun to call up to the road.
