The Stone

A horse remembers a hand it should not have seen. The road forgets nothing.

You stoop as if for your boot. The youngest rider is three paces away, his back half-turned to you, his attention on the verge. The stone in your hand is no larger than a pigeon's egg, but it is the right weight, and your fingers know how to set it. You do not know how your fingers know. You let them.

You whip it sidearm into the flank of the third rider's horse — the one still mounted, the one closest — and you do not wait to see the strike land. You are already moving.

The horse screams. Not a whinny; the high outraged shriek a horse makes when something it cannot see has insulted its body. It rears. The third rider goes off backwards, one foot clearing the stirrup, the other catching. The leader's horse takes the scream as instruction and shies sideways into the youngest, who is already off-balance from his crouch and goes down on one knee with a startled grunt.

The youngest is the closest body and the youngest is the one with the sword still sheathed. You go for him.

The cold practised thing in your hands surfaces again — that flat, accustomed muscle-memory which has been showing itself in small accidents all morning — and now it shows itself in full. You take the youngest by the back of the neck the way a man takes a goose, you fold him forward into the mud, you put one knee on the small of his back, and you draw, from the lining of your own coat, the small folded knife you did not know you had until you cut a charm's thong with it.

The blade is at his throat. He has stopped struggling. He has — he is — he is breathing very fast, too fast, the breaths of a young man whose first morning of real work is not going the way it was promised.

"Captain," he says. Just that, into the mud.

The leader is on his feet again, ten paces off, hand on his sword's hilt but not yet drawing. The third rider is twenty paces beyond him on the road, dragged on his back by his caught foot until his horse, which is sensible, has stopped to consider the situation. The third rider is not standing. He may not be standing for a while.

The leader's slow eyes are not slow now. They are calculating, very fast, and they have nowhere good to land. He looks at the boy under your knee. He looks at the knife at his throat. He looks at you.

"Friend," he says. The word has lost the small earlier irony. "We are talking now."

The youngest's breath is hot on your knuckles. The wounded man in the reeds, somewhere below the level of the road, makes no sound at all — either because he has the wit not to, or because he has stopped having the wit to do anything. You cannot, in this position, check. The morning that two minutes ago was wind in the reeds has become the kind of moment that a road only makes when it has decided to remember a name.

From very far off, somewhere east along the imperial road, a different bell sounds — a long single brass note that you do not recognise but that, somehow, your shoulders do; they tense, very slightly, at the second pulse of it. A morning bell. Sadaiziche, perhaps, calling its bakers and its priests to their first hour. The empire wakes up around you.

The leader has not drawn his sword. He has not, you notice, also unbuckled the strap that holds it. He is waiting. He is waiting to see, before he commits, whether you are the kind of figure who is committing to this fully, or only the kind who has done a clever thing once and now needs a way out.

You have a breath. Maybe two. The boy under your knee is whispering a prayer in a register so private it does not have words yet.
