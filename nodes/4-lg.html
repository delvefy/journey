The Deacon at the Mile

A wagon-stop east of Sadaiziche, a man in Church grey propped against the trough, and a morning that has decided to repeat its question in a brighter light.

The empire had taken your name and written it down. You were walking out of the morning with a folded paper in the inner pocket of your coat — the bottom edge of it just touching the small white scar at your wrist when your arm swung — and the road was wider and harder than the road of yesterday, because the imperial road east of Sadaiziche runs metalled out to the third milestone before it gives over to the country track. You walked it. Carts passed. Two riders in pale wool went the other way and looked through you because looking through travellers is how riders preserve their afternoons. The sun had crossed noon perhaps a half-bell before. You had eaten bread at the south-gate market and you had drunk water at the public trough inside the wall and the morning, by every accountancy the body was making, was concluded.

You came up to the wagon-stop a little after the second milestone. It was the empire's own kind of mercy: a stone bench under a single thin almond tree, a stone trough kept full from a leather pipe up the bank, a hitching-post worn smooth at the height a draught-horse rests its head. A grey horse stood at the post untethered, blowing through wide nostrils at flies. Against the bench — and you saw him by the line of his foot in the dust before you saw the rest of him — sat a man in the grey wool of a Church deacon, head against the back of the bench, wrists tied behind him at the post, a thread of blood drawn from his nose down to the small embroidered cross at his breast.

He was alive. His eyes were open. The bench was at a small turning of the road where the brush came down close on the south side, and on a stiller afternoon you might have ridden past it at a trot and called it a man asleep. The horse had been bridled with someone else's hand and abandoned; the deacon's saddlebags had been cut and lay turned out on the gravel.

You went to him. You crouched. You did not speak before he did.

"Friend." The voice was thin but clear. "Two of them. Sons of the country south. They rode out of the brush before the second mile and came back into it before I could get a hand to the bridle. They have the strongbox. They have ridden — I think — west again, back toward the town; my eyes were closed on the gravel and I did not see. I am Father Asad. The strongbox holds the morning's collections and a sealed letter from the bishop at Sadaiziche to the dean at Khaboran. The seal matters more than the silver. The seal must reach the dean by tomorrow's first bell or three good men in a Khaboran prison will be there by writ for another week." He drew a breath he did not entirely keep. "There is a Church post at the third milestone, friend. Three farsang east. If you ride the grey hard, you will be at the post before the dust over their backs has settled, and the post-priest will set riders behind them by the bell."

He let his head rest. He was perhaps fifty. He had the long careful hands of a man who had spent more years over books than over reins. The grey horse blew once at the trough. The almond tree above you made a small dry sound in a wind that came up the road from the east.

The paper at your wrist did not weigh anything. It weighed everything.
