The Question-Priest

A crossroads, a dark horse drinking at a trough, and a man in a robe the colour of dried blood who has been waiting for a traveller exactly like you.

You came off the road by ways the road did not record. There is no good account of this country to be given; there is only the going. Hill-folds and goat-tracks and the dry beds of summer streams; the long pale grass of late autumn; a sky going slowly to white. You walked, you sat, you walked again. The morning's work was on your hands and on your sleeves and at the back of your throat where a particular smell — copper, and the wet salt of the bottom of an irrigation ditch, and something fainter under both — would not, for the present, leave you alone. You did not, when you tried, weep. The weeping had not arrived. It would arrive, you understood, on its own schedule. You walked.

By a little after noon you came to a crossroads. It was the kind of crossroads that occurs in country no one had bothered to name — a track from the south meeting a track from the east, neither marked by a milestone, both meeting at a small stone trough that had once been the foot of a roadside cross and now served only as a stone trough. There was water in the trough. A horse was drinking at it. The horse was dark and well-kept and tied to nothing — its reins lay loose across the trough's rim, as a man's reins lie when he trusts his horse. The horse looked up at you with the unbothered glance of a creature in the middle of its work.

The horse's rider sat on the low wall beyond the trough. He was a tall man in a long robe the brown of dried blood — not freshly red, not black, but the particular brown of old blood on linen that has been washed two or three times and still not quite let go of it. The robe was clean. A small leather case stood at his feet — narrow, the size to hold rolled instruments. A wooden cross hung at his throat, plain, no chain, threaded on a piece of waxed cord. He had a long face and a clean shave and the kind of stillness one earns by sitting through other people's noises for many years.

He watched you come. He did not stand. When you were perhaps four paces from him he raised one hand — neither a greeting nor a forbidding — and you stopped.

"Friend," he said. The voice was low and pleasant. "You have come to the right person."

You said nothing.

"I am Mehrdad," he said, in the same pleasant voice. "I serve the south-gate warden-house of Sadaiziche, by appointment of the Persian Church, in the office of the question. The leader of the riders sent for me an hour past, because the morning's verge had grown more interesting than the morning's bread, and because the leader is a man who likes a thing finished by the bell. I rode out. I am, by trade, a finisher of mornings." He let his hand fall. "I see you. The seeing did not require much. You wear the morning on your sleeves, friend. Sit. The wall is wide. The horse is patient. I have water for you, and bread, and one small honest question, the answer to which I will accept whether spoken or not."

You did not sit. You stood. He did not insist.

"The question is this," he said. "There are three doors a man stands at, after a morning such as the one you have had. I have seen many of them. I am, by trade, the keeper of the doors. The first door is mine, and through it walks a man who has discovered, in himself, this morning, a particular cold capacity, and who would like to put that capacity in the service of an office that pays in copper and broth and the small protection of a robe like this one — apprentice to the question, friend, three years for journeyman, seven years for priest. The empire does not have so many men of your morning. We are short. We pay." He smiled briefly. The smile was not unkind. "I am told I am not a bad man to apprentice with."

He paused. His eyes did not leave yours.

"The second door is yours, and through it walks a man who has discovered, in himself, this morning, a thing he does not wish to discover any more of. Such a man — and I have known such men, though fewer than the first — sometimes asks me, before going on, where I am riding next, in order that he may ride in some other direction; and sometimes, more rarely, in order that he may ride ahead of me to whoever I am riding to find. I am riding, today, to a farmhouse a half-farsang east of here — the family of the brother who died in the ditch this morning. It is not, by my office, a complicated visit. There is a child in the house. There is an older sister. The leader at the south gate would like names."

A long pause. The horse at the trough lifted its head and shook the water from its bridle.

"The third door, friend, I will not speak. There is no need. You will know it if it is the door you stand at. The leather case at my feet contains my instruments and my purse; my purse this morning is heavier than my instruments. I will not stand. I will not draw. I will sit on this wall as I have been sitting, and you will choose, and we will both find out what choosing you have left in you after the morning you have had."

The horse drank. The water settled. The crossroads was quiet.
