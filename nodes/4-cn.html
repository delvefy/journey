The Chain at the Bend

A road-bend in country no one's chart names, a column of eleven men chained at the ankle, and a small wind that asks the open question of an open afternoon.

The country had taken you in. You had ridden, or you had walked, or you had done both turn and turn about; the morning had not, since the moment you set your face away from the road you had been brought to in the first place, asked you for the name of any particular footpath. The track you were on at midday was the lower spur of a sheep-walk that came down through a fold of dry hill onto a wagon-road no one in Sadaiziche would have called by name. You had with you what you had with you — the small things you had taken when you had taken them, the small things you had carried because there had been no time to set them down. The sky was clean. The wind was up from the south. A pair of crows worked the open ground above the bend, in the slow patient way crows work ground they have already worked once.

You smelled the column before you saw it. The smell of eleven men a day unwashed, in chain, downwind, walking in a dust they could not step out of. You took the bank of the track at a small hurry — not far enough to hide, only far enough to read — and crouched in the dry broom on the lee side.

The column came round the bend at a walk. Eleven men, ankle to ankle, on a long single iron chain looped through a ring at each man's right boot. Three wardens, red shoulder-cord — not the empire's white, but a private licence, a debt-bondsman's contract that the empire honoured without writing it — rode the chain front, middle, rear. The front rider was a heavy man with a heavy whip. The middle rider was a boy with a worn sword and a worn face. The rear rider was a woman, narrow, riding side-saddle on a small bay; she was watching the country off the chain's left side with the steady half-glance of a woman who had ridden this country a hundred times. The chained men did not lift their faces. There was one boy in the chain, perhaps fourteen, near the front; he was the smallest. The others were grown.

You read the column for the length of two breaths. The chain ran through a single open hook at each ankle and was closed by a brass pin at the back of each hook — a soft pin, a quick pin, the kind that came out under a thumb with a turn of the wrist if no one was watching the back of the ankle in particular. The wardens were not watching the back of any one ankle. The wardens were watching the country.

The bend in the road had a small culvert at its lowest part — a stone arch over a dry summer stream — and the front rider drew up at the culvert to water the horses from a leather bucket he had unstrapped from the saddle. The chain shuffled to a stop. The rear rider turned her bay slowly in a half-circle, looking down the verge to where you were not, by her count, present. The middle boy with the sword took the moment to lean down and drink from his own skin.

The boy at the front of the chain — the small one — was four paces from where you crouched. He had not looked at the broom. He was looking, with the small fixed look of a thing that is no longer making any choices for itself, at his own boots. The brass pin at the back of his ankle was the same brass pin the others wore. The boot was a boot a country boy wears who has been taken off a road south by a man with a debt.

The wind came up the road, turned at the culvert, and laid the smell of the chain along the broom and back out again to nothing.

Your right hand, without your asking, was on the haft of the small folded knife in the lining of your coat. The cold practised thing had come, again, into the body. The afternoon had not, yet, decided what to spend it on.
