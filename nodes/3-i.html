3-i · The Open Road

The Open Road

Two and a half farsang to a market town. A heretic's silver in your pocket and a clean walking pace.

You go fast at first and then deliberately slow. A fast walker on an empire road in the dawn is the figure a warden remembers. A slow walker is a man on his way to a thing he expects to do. You make yourself the second figure. You loosen your shoulders. You let your hands swing.

The hooves come up behind you and you do not turn. You count their walking — three sets at a search-pace — and you let them count yours. They will see a single figure on the road, moving east at the pace of an ordinary morning. They will see the smear of blood on the verge behind you, which they are presumably arriving at now, and they will see your back, and they will see the road in front of you which is the long pale strip toward Sadaiziche, and they will make their calculation.

The calculation is this: a man who has just robbed a wounded man does not walk east in plain sight at a tradesman's pace. A man who has just robbed a wounded man hides in the vineyards, or runs west, or doubles back to the shrine to make a story out of the body. Therefore the figure ahead of them is not that man. Therefore the figure ahead of them is the morning's first innocent traveller, who has, by bad luck, walked past a verge where blood has been recently spilled.

It is a calculation you have made, or your body has made for you, with a coolness that frightens you slightly when you have time to examine it.

The hooves stop at the milestone. There is a pause, the length of three horses' breaths, and then the leader — you can tell it is the leader, by the cadence — gives a quiet word, and two of the three sets of hooves move on, eastward, behind you. The third stays at the verge. To go down the bank, presumably. The youngest, you guess. The boy.

The two horses come up at a controlled walk. They draw level with you. You do not look at them. You keep your pace.

"Friend," the leader says, from above.

You look up at him then. You do not flinch. You compose your face into the small mild expression of a traveller who has noticed three armed riders and is mildly relieved that they are not bandits.

"Sir."

"You are early on the road."

"Yes, sir. I am for Sadaiziche, for the morning market." You shrug — a small economical shrug, not a performance. "My uncle, in Rabad, sent me ahead with what coin we had. He keeps a small vineyard above the third milestone. He needs new wax-stoppers, and a length of cord for the press. I am to be back by dusk."

The lie is in your mouth and you let it out the way a tradesman lets out his change — counted, but without making a show of the counting.

The leader watches you. He glances back along the road, at the milestone, at the youngest dismounting at the verge. He does not, you notice, look at your pocket where the silver fish is. He does not, you notice, look at your hip where the heavier purse is. He looks at your face, and your hands, and your boots — particularly your boots, those good Persian leather boots that fit you a little too well today and a little too loosely yesterday — and he files all three.

"Walk on, friend," he says. "Sadaiziche is two and a half farsang. The market is opening."

"Sir."

He clucks his tongue. He and the third rider move past you at a slow trot. The third rider gives you a small thin nod as he passes — not friendly; an acknowledgment that you and he have, this morning, agreed to walk in the same direction without trouble. They draw ahead of you. The hooves grow smaller. The road opens.

You keep your pace. The shrine of Vabum, at the next turning, is ahead. Sadaiziche is two and a half farsang. The morning is yours, if you can spend it carefully.
