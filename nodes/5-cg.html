Maziar at the Cold Spring

A small fire under a low stone in the dark of the second night, a man already at the fire when you come down to it, and a voice that asks you, before anything else, who taught you the way to fold a knife into a coat-lining.

The country had thinned again to the small ribs of the spurs that ran down south of the coast. The network's stages had handed you on, each one to the next, by ways that did not, when you tried to retrace them at the day's end, quite hold together as a single road. There had been a stable above a vineyard. There had been a goat-track. There had been a fold of dry oak where a woman with a star-scar at her temple had given you bread and pointed without speaking at the next ridge. The night had come early. The next stage was a cold spring at the foot of a low stone, two farsang on, where a fire was sometimes lit by whoever was passing for the next runner to find. You came down to it in the dark by the small ash-smell on a wind off the south.

The fire was already lit. A man sat at it.

He had a small pot on the coals — a tin pot, a soldier's pot, blackened at the base — and a folded grey blanket across his shoulders against the cold off the spring. His face under the blanket's edge was the colour of a face that has slept rough at six fires in a row. He had a long jaw and a small clean beard and a way of sitting at a fire that put his weight on his back foot for the rising. He did not, when you came down the slope, lift his head.

"Sit," he said. "There is broth. There is enough."

You sat. You took the cup he passed you across the coals. The broth was hot and salt and tasted of an animal he had bought, by the small grey hairs at the bottom of the cup, from a country boy with a goat that had not been quite well. You drank it because the night was a night for drinking what the fire offered. The man's eyes had been on the fire when you sat. They were on the small folded knife in the lining of your coat now, where the sleeve had ridden when you took the cup. He looked at the knife the way a man looks at a piece of work he has, in another life, made.

"That is a Khaboran fold," he said. He said it almost gently. "There is one master in Khaboran who folds a knife into a coat-lining that way. The lining at the inner seam, against the third button, with the haft turned out. He had perhaps eight of us through his hands in the years he was at the seminary. I was one." He poured himself another cup from the pot, slowly. "I have been four years off the road from him. He has been, perhaps, longer. I do not know if he is, any more, with the order. He may have been let go." He took a long drink. He looked up at you over the rim of the cup. The look was a careful, patient look. "I have not seen the fold on a coat in four years until now. I do not, on a road like this one, see it by accident. Khorshid?"

The name went down through you again, the way it had gone down in the bright room with the magistrate, except here it did not stay near the surface; it went down to the small cold place at the centre of the chest where the muscle had been remembering things all week. You did not, with this man, need to be told that he had been at Khaboran with you. You had been with him at Khaboran. You did not, at this moment, remember when, or which winter, or what he had then been called; but the body remembered, the way the body had remembered the cold practised thing in the hand at four paces.

"I am Maziar," he said. "Maziar at the cold spring, if there is a name. Maziar Karimi, otherwise. I turned, friend. The order has had my name in the same paybook as yours for four years. They are not looking for me as hard as they were; they ran out of riders to spare on a man who had only the small list to take with him." He smiled briefly. The smile did not reach the fire. "You have come down a stage to my fire. The stage's runner did not, by his own mouth, say a name. He said only there was a brother behind him on the spur. I have sat at this fire for two hours waiting. I have hoped, friend, half of the two hours that the brother behind would be no one I knew, and the other half that it would be you."

The pot on the coals went on at its small particular boil. The spring at the foot of the stone made its small water-noise into the dark.
