The Stranger in the Dust

A wagon-track at midday, a chestnut mare standing over its rider, and a man's trouble that has not, by any honest accountancy of the morning, asked anything of you.

You had spent the morning in your own keeping. The road had been a road you had not been asked the name of. The country south of Sadaiziche thins to long dry shoulders along a wagon-track between low orchards, and the track had carried you out of the previous day's accountancy at a pace that did not require you to look back. You had slept where you had slept, woken when the cold woke you, eaten what was at hand. By midday the sun was a flat coin overhead and the dust on your boots was the colour of the dust on every other boot that had come this way before yours, and you were thinking of nothing in particular except the small good fact of the second skin of water at your hip, when the track came round the shoulder of an orchard and laid the next quarter farsang out in front of you, and in the middle of that quarter farsang, at the foot of a single twisted poplar, a horse was standing over a man.

The horse was a chestnut mare in good order. She was bridled but not saddled — a soldier's halter, not a merchant's — and her reins lay across her own foreleg, knotted in a quick clove the way a man knots reins when he is dismounting in a hurry and not in a hurry the same time. She did not move when you came up. She blew once through her nostrils and shook her head at the flies. The man on his back in the dust at her hooves was perhaps thirty. He had a long red moustache and a soldier's badge stitched to the breast of a coat that was not, by the cut, the empire's coat. A trooper of one of the eastern frontier provinces; you did not know the badge by name. His hair on the temple was matted with blood. A wineskin lay six paces from him in the ditch. The wineskin had a slow brown leak running out of it into the dust, and the leak had been running, by the dampness of the patch under it, perhaps a quarter-bell.

You stopped at four paces. You did not crouch. You looked.

He was alive. The breathing was shallow but it was breathing. The blood at the temple had stopped of its own accord; the swelling was beginning. The fall had been off the mare, plainly — there was a long graze along the side of the mare's saddle-blanket and a corresponding tear at the rider's coat sleeve. He had been riding drunk; he had been thrown when the mare shied at something or at nothing; he had hit the poplar's root on the way down. The poplar's root was visible, dark with a smear that was no longer wet. There were no other tracks in the dust. The road ahead was empty as far as the next orchard. The road behind you was empty as far as the orchard you had just come round. The poplar's small leaves above moved in a small wind that did not, when it reached you, carry a sound from any direction.

The man was not your man. The mare was not your mare. The wineskin had been opened by a thumb that was not your thumb. The empire's road south of Sadaiziche, when it carries a frontier-province trooper drunk by the bell of noon, has a wagon along it inside the next hour as a matter of pure habit. The next wagon would lift him, or pass him, or send word at the next way-house, by whatever customs the next wagoner had been taught at the south gate of whatever town the wagoner had come from. The road has its keeping. The road does not, on the whole, require yours.

You stood. You looked at the mare. The mare looked at you. The man's breath came and went. The sun on the back of your neck was a hand that had been there a while.
