The Cutter in the Lee

A small fishing-boat at the first crest of the morning's swell beyond the cove's south headland, a low coast-warden's cutter in the lee of the next headland with her sail half-struck, and Soraya at the tiller turning her eye on you with the small flat look that asks what a Khaboran apprentice does with two oars and a quarter-bell.

The boat had taken the wind off the cove at the first quarter-bell after sunrise. The man on the cliff path — bound at the wrist with the small clean cord the boat kept under the bowsprit for such mornings, or laid in his own coat at the back of the grass shelf for the country to find at noon, or somewhere not at the cove at all because the goat-track had given you the shingle in his place — was not, by any measure the boat now had, a problem of the cove any more. He was the cove's. The boat was past the cove. The boat's three figures had become three figures and a fourth and the fourth was you, on the small thwart at the centre of the boat, holding the long ash oar in the way a boy with the body's memory of an ash oar held it without the mind having quite been part of the holding.

The smallest of the three figures at the boat's stern was a black-haired woman with a small star at her temple — Soraya, by the carry of the shoulders against the wind, or another woman wearing Soraya's face. The other two figures at the boat were a tall man with a sailor's leather cap who was working the sail, and a boy of perhaps fourteen who was setting the bailing-bucket against the gunwale at the stern with the small particular care of a boy who had been told the bailing-bucket was the most important piece of equipment on the boat. The wind was off the land. The wind would, by the small turning of the sail, carry the boat down the coast at perhaps three farsang to the bell.

The cutter came round the next headland at the small bell of the second quarter past sunrise.

You saw her first; the body had been watching the lee of the next headland the way the body had been watching the line of poplars at every verge for a week. She was a low cutter of the coast-warden's pattern — single mast, fore-and-aft rig, a small brass piece at the bow no larger than a man's forearm, perhaps eight crew. Her sail was half-struck. She was in the lee of the headland in the small slow waiting of a piece of equipment that had been sat in a lee for a quarter-bell and was minded, by the bell of the boat's coming round the headland, to be at the boat's side in the time another quarter-bell took. Soraya at the tiller saw her at the same breath you did. Her face did not move. The boy at the bailing-bucket had not yet seen. The man at the sail looked at Soraya for half a beat and did not, by his own face, decide.

"They have come up by morning's word," Soraya said, very level. "Word from the cove last night, by a man on a fast horse along the cliff-road. The cove keeps its bell; the cliff-road keeps its bell faster. They are not, by the lee, in a hurry. They are in a hurry to be in front of us at the second headland in a quarter-bell. We have, by the wind, the same quarter-bell." She looked at you. "Khorshid. The man on the path was the order's. The cutter is the empire's. The order and the empire have, this morning, the same business. The boy at the bailing-bucket is my son. The man at the sail is my cousin. The boat is the network's. I have three minutes by the wind to be a person making decisions on behalf of the network. Decide with me. Quickly."

Her hand on the tiller was steady. The cutter at the lee was perhaps eighteen poles off and was, by the slow filling of the half-struck sail, deciding whether to come full out for the chase or hold the lee for the boat to come into. The boy at the bucket had now seen. He had not, by any of the seeing, stopped setting the bucket.
