3-c · The Cup-Bearer's Step

The Cup-Bearer's Step

A cold mercy. A shrine that asks nothing and forgives nothing.

You walk him back. Fifty paces, no more, and each pace a negotiation. He does not understand at first — his body had already arranged itself for the long climb east; now it must reorient for west, and west is harder, the cold air at his back instead of his face. He coughs once, hard, and you feel the warmth of what comes up on your shirt.

The shrine is where it always was. A waist-high stone pillar at a turning of the road, the carved horns of the Cup-Bearer worn smooth by weather, the bowl at the pillar's foot dark with old offerings — bread gone to mould, a fishbone, a small dried apricot blackening. The folk-god of pourers and the patron of poor travellers. No church-warden has yet found a reason to tear the shrine down. The empire tolerates Vabum the way a great house tolerates a faithful old dog in the kitchen.

You lower the man at the foot of the pillar. You arrange him so that he is sitting upright against it — knees a little drawn, head fallen forward, the way a tired pilgrim falls asleep at a wayside god. You take your coat off him. You take, very gently, the silver charm at your own collarbone — the Mardesran fish, dug out of your shirt — and you slide it back over his head and down inside his collar, where it had been when you found him. You owe him that much. You owe him exactly that much.

"Forgive me," you say, and you do not know to whom you are saying it. You touch your fingertips to the worn horns of the Cup-Bearer in passing — the gesture surfaces in your hand without consulting you — and you step back onto the road.

You walk east. You do not look back. The morning is greying into proper colour now: a thin yellow at the horizon, the scrub on the hills resolving from black to dun. Your own breath rolls out white. The cold of the dawn is at its hardest hour, the hour before the sun, when the heat the earth had stolen yesterday has all been given back.

You walk perhaps a hundred paces. The hooves come up behind you.

You hear the leader's call first — "Friend" — flat, the way the leader said it before, the way a man calls another man whose name he intends to write down. You stop. You turn. You wait for them with your hands open at your sides.

They come up at a walk. The leader's slow eyes go over you the way they did before: face, hands, boots, the road behind you. Then the road in front of you.

"You came from the west," he says.

"From Rabad," you say. "I am for Sadaiziche."

"And you passed nothing. Saw nothing. Heard nothing."

You let yourself wait — not too long; not too short — and then you say it. "There is a man at the shrine."

The leader's eyes do something. Not surprise; not interest; only a slow narrowing, as a man tightens a knot he had not yet pulled. "A man," he repeats.

"At Vabum's pillar. A hundred paces behind me. He is — he is not well. I thought he was a sleeping pilgrim. When I touched his shoulder, his coat — there was —" You let the sentence break where a real sentence would break. "I did not know what to do. I am sorry. I walked on. I did not want to be found alone with him by men I do not know."

The leader looks at you for a long unhurried moment. The third rider, behind him, is already turning his horse back. The youngest dismounts and looks at the road, at your boots, at the smear of blood that did not come off your shirt. The leader does not look at any of these things. He looks at your eyes.

"That was wise of you," he says, with no warmth at all.

And the morning, which had been almost yours, becomes the empire's again.
