Parvin at the Trough

A wayside inn-yard at the small bare hour after noon, a wash-woman wringing seminary-grey linens out into a stone trough, and a name spoken sideways the way the wind speaks a thing it does not, by speaking, mean to do anything about.

The road had brought you to a wayside inn on the empire's middle road, half a day from anywhere a chart would print twice. The inn was a small one — a low yard against the road, a stable on one side and a kitchen on the other, a single wide trough in the centre where horses watered at the hour the carts changed and a wash-woman did the inn's small linens at the hour they did not. You had come in for water and bread. You had eaten the bread on the bench by the stable wall and drunk the small cup of thin wine the kitchen-boy had set beside it; now, the bell of the second hour going on somewhere down the road, you had crossed the yard to the trough to wash your face before walking on. The wash-woman did not lift her head when you crouched at the trough's far end.

She was perhaps sixty. She wore a heavy brown apron over a long grey dress and a scarf of the same grey as her dress over the most of her hair. The hair at her temples that had escaped the scarf was the colour of a wood-smoke that had been on a roof a long time. Her hands were red at the knuckles. She had a small piled stack of folded linens at her hip and a larger stack of unfolded ones at her elbow, and she was working the unfolded into the folded with the slow economy of a woman who had done this number of linens at this number of troughs for, by her own forearm, perhaps forty years. The linens were a particular grey, a grey that was almost a brown, the grey that the long Khaboran wash put on white wool by the third winter. You had not, when you came up to the trough, seen the colour. You saw it when you looked sideways at her.

The wash-woman did not, at once, look up. She wrung a linen out into the trough's far end. She straightened it. She laid it on the folded pile. She took the next from the unfolded. She wrung. Then — without lifting her face fully, in the small habit of a woman who had read many faces at troughs without needing to lift her head — she gave you the quarter-look she gave the faces she read.

She did not stop wringing. Her hands went on with the linen. Her eyes went over you for the length of perhaps four breaths and came back down to the linen. She had read you in the time another woman would have read the colour of your coat. She did not, then or after, look at you again.

"Khorshid Daiyani," she said, to the linen, in the small dry voice of a woman observing the day's weather. "Older than the seminary remembers you, friend, but the gait at the knee is the same. You had the bad knee on the right, going down the stair to the laundry on a Tuesday in the year of the second snow. You walked up the stair the same way you walked down. I told the matron to send the boy with the bad knee to the cobbler at the south yard and the matron sent you, by what she called her own counsel, to the dancing-master. The dancing-master told the matron the boy had no bad knee. The boy had a way." She wrung again. "It was a way then. It is a way still. Parvin, friend. I will not have, by your face, asked you to call up the name; I am of the age now of the matron of those years, and I do not, by habit, expect a boy of those years to call up a wash-woman's. The basin is full to your right. The water from the trough is for the horses. The basin is for the face."

She nodded with her chin at a smaller stone basin set into the wall behind the trough — a thing you had not seen, the kind of small inn-yard mercy a wash-woman of forty years' habit set there in passing for the travellers who would otherwise wash in the horses' water. You wet your hand. You washed your face. The cold of the water was the cold of the high country. Parvin did not, again, look up.

"I do not, friend, by my trade, tell rooms what passes my trough," she said, to the linen, almost as a piece of conversation she was having with herself. "It is a long road. It has not yet got me into Khaboran in living memory. It will not, today, send you back. The grey on these linens is from a wash at Vahdat. The matron there is a niece of the one you knew. The niece is no worse than the aunt. I am at this trough until the bell of three. After three I am at the kitchen door for the second meal. After the second meal I am asleep against the back wall. None of these things are your business and none of yours are mine. The basin is for the face. The bread is in the kitchen if you want a second piece. The road south takes the next turning at the lone walnut."

She wrung. She folded. She did not, at any point in the small ordinary motion of her hands, look at you again.
