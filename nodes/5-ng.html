Bahram at the Long Table

A roadside way-house in country no warden's chart troubles, a long pine table with eight men at it, and the seventh man putting his bowl down very slowly when his eye comes round to yours.

The way-house was at a turning of an old track where no imperial road came up — the kind of place country folk knew the name of and travellers did not, the kind of place a farmer's wife had set out a row of three iron lamps on the lintel because the wind off the spur came up after dark and a third lamp made a small additional argument against it. You had come up the track in a grey hour after the rain. You had been brought to the door by a way of getting here that the morning's accountancy was satisfied to leave undescribed. The mistress of the place — a heavy woman with a clean apron and slow eyes that read your boots and then read past them — had set you at the long table by the hearth, set a bowl of mutton-and-barley by your right hand, set a small clay cup of thin red beside it, and gone back to the spit without further commentary. You were the ninth at the table. The other eight were country men, by the hands.

You ate. The table ate with you. The talk at the table was the talk a long pine table makes for itself in such a room — the price of pruning-wire, the new toll at the river-crossing, a story about a goat that had eaten a man's hat that everyone at the table had told before and nobody minded telling again — and you sat in it the way a traveller sits in such talk, eating and not interrupting and not, by any small inattention, drawing the room's eye. The room was at its slowest hour. The mistress was at the spit. The little brown dog was at the hearth.

The seventh man at the table was perhaps thirty-five. He had a coat over a leather apron, the apron worn shiny at the thigh where a cooper's mallet rested, and at his belt a small iron pricker the cooper used to start the band-nails. He had been listening at the table without saying much. He had finished his own bowl a quarter-bell past. He had been working a small clay cup in his hand, around and around, slowly, as men do at the slow hour. He had, you registered at the small back of the head, not been looking at the talk. He had been looking, by the slope of the eye, at the side of your face, since perhaps the time the mistress had set the bowl by your hand.

You did not turn. You had eaten three more spoonfuls before you turned. When you turned he was already setting his cup down on the table at the small care of a man who does not want to spill what is left in it. He had a face you had not seen, and a face you had seen, and the seeing went down through you at the slow rate of cold water going down a cup.

"Khorshid?" he said. He said it very quietly. He said it as a question to the table, and as a question to himself, and not to you at all, in the order of a man who is asking the question first to make sure the room does not, by the asking, hear it.

The other men at the table did not stop talking. The price of pruning-wire was not yet settled.

"Bahram," he said next, to you, at his own breast — the small naming-back a half-brother does to invite the other half-brother's eye to come the rest of the way. "Bahram, the cooper. Your mother's sister's son. I was nine and you were eleven and you came down to us at Behesht the summer your father took the post at Khaboran. You taught me to set a hoop on a barrel-jar in the yard behind my mother's house. I have been on this road for seven seasons looking. I had stopped looking, friend, perhaps a year ago. I had not stopped." His hand on the table was steady. His eyes were not. "The post at Khaboran said you had gone to the country and not come back. The order at Khaboran said other things and I will not, at this table, say what." His voice did not lift. "I will not name you again at the table if you do not wish it. I will sit. I will finish my cup. You will tell me, by the time the dog at the hearth has turned itself once round, whether you came up this track by the sky or by the bones, and whether we walk out of this door together in the morning or whether I have, for the second time, not found you."

He picked up the cup. He turned it once. He did not, for a long beat, drink from it.
