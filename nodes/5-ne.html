Cyrus at the Fence's Door

A back alley off the bottom of the unofficial market, a low door set into the brick at a man's shoulder-height, and a man at the lintel who has lifted his face out of the dark of the doorway and is now, by the small intake of his breath, becoming very careful.

The fence kept a low door at the bottom of the alley behind the slaughter-yard. The door was the kind a man knocked at twice and waited at and did not, by his own hand, push open. You knocked the way you had been knocking at such doors all week — twice, the second knock a little softer than the first — and you stood back the half-pace the door required, the small package under your right arm and the small purse at your left hip, and waited. The alley was the kind of alley an evening came down into early; the brick on either side was warm from the day under your shoulder but the sky over the alley was already the slow violet of an autumn evening when the wind was off the river. There were three other men in the alley at other doors. None of them looked at you. None of them looked at each other.

The door opened a hand's width. The man at the door was not the fence. The fence was a heavy old Persian called Bardas's man, who had taken the door three years and would, in three years more, hand it on to his nephew when the nephew came of age; but the man at the door this evening was not him. The man at the door was perhaps thirty-five, narrow at the shoulder, a black coat over a dyer's shirt, hair drawn back from the temple in a way that put the small particular tightness of the temple into the lamp-light. The lamp behind him was a low lamp. The brand of the empire on a Mardesran it had taken once and let go was a small star-shaped scar at the temple. He had the scar. The lamp behind him put it sharply into the side of his face.

He saw you. He did not move. He held the door at a hand's width for the small breath of his recognition, and then — because the alley was an alley three other men were at three other doors of — he opened the door fully and stepped back into the room behind it as if he were ushering in a small piece of normal trade.

"Friend." His voice was steady. The hand at the door was steady. He let you come past him into the room. He closed the door behind you and turned the small wooden bar across the inside of it without — and you felt this as a particular small care — without looking at you while he did it. The room behind the door was a single low room with a counter at the back and a thin curtain behind the counter into a further room you could not see into. There was a candle on the counter. There were no other men in the room. The fence's man — Bardas's man — was not in the room. Only the narrow man with the small star-shaped scar.

He went round the counter. He did not look at the package under your arm. He looked at the candle. He set his hand flat on the counter — not in the way a fence sets his hand, with the purse-arm at the close of it — but in the way a man sets his hand who needs to feel something steady under it for the duration of what he is about to say.

"Khorshid." His voice did not lift. "Cyrus. Cyrus of the river-house at Yazdebar. You will not remember a face you saw twice across a table at Khaboran nine winters past. I remember it. I remembered it at the door the moment the second knock came. I remembered it the way a man remembers a hand at his father's throat." He breathed in. He breathed out. He did not, in either, take his eyes off the candle. "My father did not, by your hand at the table, live the autumn out. My brother lived the winter and did not live the spring. The third name on the page that morning was mine, and the order let me come up out of the back room of Khaboran at the cost of a star at the temple and the small particular agreement with the order that I would be at this door, on this alley, six nights of the week for as long as the order required a Mardesran to sit at it and listen to who came knocking. I have sat at this door three years. I have not, at this door, named your face to the order. I had not, until this evening, known whether I would. I had not, until this evening, known whether the face would come up out of the country at me."

His hand on the counter did not move. The candle put the small star at his temple into the side of the room and put the rest of his face into the shadow under the brow.

"There is a thing the order pays Bardas's man for, when a face the order has been looking for comes to this door. I know the rate. I know the second rate Bardas pays in addition, which is the more interesting rate. I will, friend, by the bell of the moon's first hour, decide whether to ring the small bell under the counter. The package under your arm is no business of mine. The thing under your sleeve at the third button is no business of mine. The morning's purse at your left hip is the order's rate, near enough. The morning's purse is not what I am asking you for. You are at this counter, friend. You will, before I ring, tell me what to be for you." His eyes lifted finally to yours. "Decide what to be for me at the same time."
