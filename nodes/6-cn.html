The Rooflight Above the Dyer's Yard

A small attic room above the closed-up loft of a dyer's shop, a square rooflight propped open a finger's width on a piece of bent willow, and a sound at the slates of the roof that is the foot of a person who has not, by their own walking, come up the inner stair.

You had taken the attic by the kind of arrangement an unmarked town's edge made for a man with the morning's coin and the morning's quiet. There had been a woman with a small grey-streaked plait at the back of a long dye-yard; there had been a man at a goat-track who had pointed at a roof; there had been a half-bell at a market between two cloth-stalls and a back-alley not far from a Thursday-fair. The country, by some accident of the country's small charities for people who did not, by their own walking, want the country to know the shape of them, had brought you up the back stair to an attic above a dyer's loft and had left you there with the morning's coin in a folded rag and the morning's bread in a knot of paper and a wool blanket against the wall.

The attic was a small room. The roof was low; you could not, in the middle of it, stand to your full height. There was a single square rooflight set into the slates at the south side of the gable, propped open a finger's width on a piece of bent willow the way the woman with the plait propped her own rooflights to let the dye-fumes go. The rooflight gave onto the slates of the next roof at the side and the alley below at the front. The dyer's yard at the back had been quiet for two bells. The town's Thursday-fair had been over since the small bell of the evening. The lamps at the alley were not lit.

You had slept perhaps a half-bell. You had not, by sleeping, slept well; the body was, when you woke, on the small ready edge a body went onto when the body decided the room was a room a sleeper should be only the half of a sleeper in. You did not open your eyes at once. The body knew, by some lower listening, that the rooflight was no longer at the finger's width the bent willow had set it at. Some weight had laid itself, very lightly, on the willow. The willow was bent further. The rooflight was open at perhaps two fingers.

A foot was set, very slowly, on the slate beside the willow.

You did not move. The body, by the back of the right hand, was already at the lining at the third button. The folded knife in the Khaboran fold was already in your palm. The body had read, at the small turning at the rooflight, that the foot at the slate was a small foot — a woman's, perhaps, or a slight man's — and that the second foot at the next slate was set at the careful slow pace of a person who had come up the dyer's gable from the alley by the iron drainpipe at the back. You had, when you came up the inner stair four bells past, registered the drainpipe at the back without quite registering yourself registering it. The body had registered it on your behalf.

The voice at the rooflight, when it came, was the voice of a woman who had not, in her own coming up the slates, decided whether to be a thief or a friend.

"Friend." Very low. Almost a piece of the willow. "I am at the rooflight by the dyer's mother's directions. The dyer's mother is the woman with the plait. She has taken five small coppers of mine this evening to tell me what room a stranger has come into her loft for, and what kind of stranger he is by the colour of his boots. I am not at the rooflight to be at your throat. I am not at the rooflight to be at your purse. I am at the rooflight because the small coin I have to spend at the dyer's mother's mouth was given me by a man in the alley who said he would, by the bell of the second of the morning, be at the front of the dyer's shop with three other men. I am Mira's, friend — Mira's, from the elder-tree hollow above the second vineyard — and I have walked the country a week looking for the face the network said had been turned out of the road in the wrong direction. There is, by the small coin in my hand, perhaps the quarter-bell before the three at the front have decided to come up the inner stair."

She did not, by any small lift of the voice, push the rooflight further. The willow held at the two-finger width. Below in the alley a dog made a small noise about something and stopped.
