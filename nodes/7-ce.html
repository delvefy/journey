The Riding Down the Coast-Track

The bowl at the small grey hour of the morning after Farzad's, a runner come in over the lip with the dust of the imperial road still on his shoulders, and an imperial riding of perhaps twenty horses on the coast-track at the bell of the third hour with the Khaboran circuit-captain's red cord at the lead-rider's saddle.

The morning after Farzad's was the bowl's. The bowl had come up over the lip at the bell of the noon yesterday with one less rider than it had gone down with — the third along the spur, the widow's son, taken by a man at Farzad's gate before the boy had quite understood that the bowl's account of the widow had been an account at the bowl's hand and not at his — and with the small parish bounty out of Farzad's locked drawer and a second pannier of the orchard's olive oil and a third pannier the bowl did not yet, by the leader's small careful unwatching at the fire, want to be specific about the contents of. The bowl had eaten at the back wall. The bowl had drunk. The bowl had slept the short rough sleep of fifteen men on a stone floor. The bowl had woken at the small grey hour.

The runner came up over the lip at the small grey hour also. He was a thin man with a Mardesran star at his temple and the dust of the imperial road at the shoulders of a coat that had not been on the road yesterday. He had ridden through the night. The horse at the rim was the colour of foam. He came down the lip at the small unsteady pace of a man who had not, in the night, eaten. He went to the leader's stone first. Behdad rose from the back wall and met him at three paces from the stone. The runner spoke into Behdad's ear in the small particular voice a man at a stone uses who does not, by his own walking, want the bowl to hear at once. Behdad's face did not move at the speaking. Behdad set his hand at the runner's shoulder when the runner finished. He did not, by the hand, push him away.

The leader turned, then, to the bowl.

"There is an imperial riding on the coast-track," he said. He said it at the small even pitch a leader's voice took at a bowl. "Twenty horses. Khaboran circuit-captain's red cord at the lead-rider's saddle. They came down the imperial road in the night by the cart-stages. They are at the second milestone south of Sadaiziche at the bell of the third hour of this morning. They will be at the foot of our spur by the bell of the noon. The runner is Asha's. Asha's fire was passed at the second hour by a younger rider on the road south of her ridge — a rider in a dust-blue coat with a silver olive at the shoulder, riding ahead of the captain's twenty by a half-bell to put eyes at the spur's foot. Asha sent the runner up at the third bell with the news." He breathed in. "The morning's bell is the bell. The bowl has a quarter of one. The brother at the back wall is the second rider. The second rider has at his coat the order's fold and the order's small careful knowledge of how an imperial circuit-captain rides at the foot of a spur. The brother will, by the bell of the noon, be at the front of the bowl's morning or the back of its evening. I will, before the brother speaks, set the bowl's three doors at the fire. Speak in turn."

He set, at the back wall, in the small careful order of a man setting the three he had had a long bell to decide between, the bowl's three doors:

The first door was to go down off the spur at once by the back goat-track and dissolve the bowl into the country east of Asha's fire — fifteen men, three days, four small country houses, the bowl's saddlebags into the country's hands at half-bounty and the bowl's accountancy a season later in a different stone. The second door was to ride out at the foot of the spur at the eleventh bell, an hour before the captain's twenty arrived, and take the captain's lead-rider at the second milestone where the road bent at the small dust-coloured outcrop — eleven horses against twenty, but eleven horses on a bend with the morning's sun at their back and a captain at the head of the twenty who had not yet, by his lead-rider's eye at the foot of the spur, written a word into his folder. The third door was the door the bowl did not, by long habit, take, and the third door was to go further up the ridge to the high winter-bothy at the small cold spring and wait — wait one day, two, three, until the captain's twenty had had their morning at the bowl's empty floor and had ridden back to Khaboran with the bowl's empty floor in their pannier and the bowl's morning's plan turned over to the imperial road's slow patience for a season.

Behdad sat back on the leader's stone. He laid the flat of his hand on the falchion's grip. The bowl looked at the back wall. The runner from Asha's fire stood with the empty horse-rein in his hand. The grey hour was lifting at the rim of the bowl into the small clean first colour of the morning.
