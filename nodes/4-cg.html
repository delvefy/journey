The Courier Without a Horse

A walled fold at the head of a vineyard valley, a man come up the goat-track on foot when he should have come down it on horseback, and a name written six times in a sleeve.

The network had moved you up a stage. There were houses along the way that did not show on any chart — a stone fold above the second vineyard's pruning-shed, an empty winter-house on a south-facing slope, a hollow under a fallen ilex where bread had been left in a tin against rain — and at the third of these, the small high-walled fold where the south-running track turned into a brown shoulder of the country, you had spent the morning sweeping ash from the hearth and re-laying the fire and reading the small list, in a hand you did not know, of who came up by which day. The fold had two rooms and a roofed yard. The woman of the fold was called Soraya. She had been white at the temple before her star-scar had been put on. She did not speak much. She had been pleased, you thought, by the sweeping.

The afternoon was beginning to slope toward dusk when the courier came up the track. He came up it wrong. A courier comes down a track on the back of a small Persian horse, hooves wrapped in cloth, with a coat that does not show the silver fish at the throat unless it is opened deliberately at the throat by the man wearing it. This courier came up the track on his own feet, one hand at his belly, the other at the wall, the cloth at his belly already through and through with a red that had had an hour to settle into the wool. He was a young man with a broken nose and a beard like a goat's. He fell to one knee under the fold's gate-arch and Soraya caught his shoulder before his head went the rest of the way.

"They had me at the river," he said. His voice did not have much air behind it. "Two of the cord. The road south of Sadaiziche. I went into the water and they did not, but the one of them had a good arm with a stone." He was breathing in small bites. "Soraya. The names. The names need to reach the elder at the second pruning-shed by dusk or there are six wrong doors in the town that go through tonight. The names are in my sleeve. The left sleeve, the lining, six names and the marks against each. I cannot stand to walk them. I do not have a horse."

Soraya carried him inside. You followed. She laid him on the long table — there was no pallet free — and slit the wool of the left sleeve with a small bone-handled knife along the underside seam. Folded into the lining, between two layers of waxed linen, was a strip of vellum a hand long and four fingers wide. There were six lines on it. The hand was small and careful and the same hand you had read on the list of the days. Beside each name was a mark — three rings, a single ring, a half-ring, a cross — meaning things you did not yet know to read. Soraya held the strip up to the lamp once, briefly, and folded it again, and laid it on the table where any hand at the table could reach it.

The courier was breathing in a particular way now. You had heard the way once before this morning.

Soraya looked at the courier. She looked at the strip. She looked at you. She did not say anything. She was not the sort of woman who told a stranger her need; she was the sort who set the need on the table and stepped back from it.

"The second pruning-shed," she said at last, almost to the courier, "is a half-bell at a hard walk. The track goes east of north out of the back of the yard and along the spine of the ridge — three turnings, the third turning to the left, by the lightning-split oak. The elder is a woman called Hirsi. She is white in both braids. She will not know your face. She will know the strip."

The courier on the table closed his eyes. He was not unconscious. He was listening for what you would do next. So was Soraya. So, in its own way, was the lamp.
