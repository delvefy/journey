The Long Light

An evening on a road no one is on, a man at the verge in the position of a sleeper, and a pocket that still has the weight of its morning hanging at its hem.

The afternoon had paid out, the way afternoons did now. You had spent it on your own; you had eaten when you ate, drunk when you drank, walked the road that was the next road. The country here was higher and drier than the country round Sadaiziche, the road a single rutted line between long slopes of stubble and burnt heather where the goats had not yet been brought up from the lower folds. The sun was a hand off the western ridge. The light was the long flat light that the day's last bell brings to country that has no town in it. Your shadow ran ahead of you longer than you were tall.

You saw the man at the verge from perhaps a hundred paces. He was lying in the curl of a sleeper — knees half-drawn, the one arm under the head — and at first glance you read him as a wayfarer who had laid down for the small luxury of an evening's rest in the long grass at the road's edge. You walked the hundred paces without changing pace. Forty paces from him, the body's quiet had a different quality from sleep's. Twenty paces from him, the fly at his open mouth did not lift when the wind turned. At ten paces you stopped.

He was perhaps forty. A traveller — a small pack at his shoulder, a stout walking-stick laid down beside his hand, country boots in good order. There was no wound on him that you could see from the road; you crossed to him at three paces and crouched and looked, in the long light, and saw the small dark line at the side of his neck where the skin had gone the colour bruised skin goes inside the half-bell of the going. A stroke. The kind that comes for a strong-looking man in late autumn on a country walk. He had set himself down at the verge and laid the stick beside his hand and put his head on his arm and gone. There was no sign in any direction of any other body, footprint, hoof, or cart. The road was empty up to the next bend and empty back to the bend behind. The sun was a hand off the ridge and the light was the long light.

You looked at him a long beat without thinking. Then you began, as bodies do, to think.

The pack at his shoulder was a small canvas pack, worn smooth at the straps. The walking-stick was a stout one of pale ash with a copper ferrule at the bottom. Under the coat at his hip there was, by the small even hang of the cloth, a purse — not large; not small. At his left hand, the third finger, a ring of plain dark silver. The boots were country boots; they were good country boots. There was no badge on the coat. There was no token at the neck. There was, in the inner breast of the coat — the small particular fold that a careful man puts important paper into — the edge of a folded square of vellum. He had been a man going somewhere with a letter.

The wind turned again. The fly went up. The fly came back. The long light lay along the slope and made of every small thing a small bright thing — the buckle of his pack, the copper of the ferrule, the dark silver of the ring. The road did not have, in any direction, a witness. The next bend was a quarter-bell away. The bend behind was the same. The country between had no shepherd, no goat, no rook to write him in any account other than the one that the next cart down the road tomorrow morning would, by its driver's own habit, write.

You were the bell at the verge. The day was waiting on your reading of it.
