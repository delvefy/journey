The Daybook's Open Column

A warden-post's main room at the small bell of the third hour, a daybook open on the desk at a column the desk-clerk has been minding for the past quarter-bell, and a leather pannier on the floor by the side-board with a folder of writs the morning has assigned to your hand.

The post had taken you on. The taking-on had been the bell of the morning's third hour, in a room you had walked into the night before with hands flat and a writ in your coat, or by a captain's small hand at your elbow at the back of a desk, or by a paymaster's curtain into a back room with a strong-box on a stool. The room was not, by morning, much changed. The desk-clerk had a fresh quill. The corporal at the door had a fresh pot of tea. The leader with the slow eyes was somewhere in the back room going over the night's returns and would not, by his own habit before the bell of the fourth, come into the front room except to read what the morning was. The morning was, by the desk-clerk's small clean glance up at you when you came in, a morning that had a column at the desk.

The column at the desk was the day's writ-list. Six writs, in three columns of two, in the desk-clerk's small neat hand. The first column was the morning's two south-gate cases. The second was the morning's two river-quay cases. The third was the morning's two cases on the imperial road east. Beside the third column, in a faint pencil-line the desk-clerk had drawn at first bell, was an empty column the width of a man's thumb where a hand at the post would, by the bell of the noon, write its own name as the warden of the case-file. The empty column had your name at the head of it. The name was the new name. Khorshid Daiyani. The desk-clerk had written it without ornament and without remark, in the same small hand the morning's other columns had been written in.

The folder of writs was at the side-board. Six folded writs, sealed in the post's brown wax, weighted with the same piece of river-slate the corporal had laid across the morning's returns the day you had first come into the post. The corporal had set the slate in the same place by habit. The same place this morning was the place that had your folder under it.

You stood at the desk. The desk-clerk did not lift his head. The corporal at the door was, by the small slow turning of his cup in his hand, not looking at you either. The room — by the small careful unwatching of three men who had been told by the leader at the morning's first bell what kind of man would be coming through the door — was offering you the open column.

You looked at the six writs. The names at the head of the writs were names a desk-clerk's pen had made small and neat in the way a desk-clerk made names neat for a man whose hand would, by the bell of three, be at every one of them. The names were the kind of names the post worked through on a morning the leader had given his corporal the daybook on. There was a name on the second writ — Hamza, at the river-quay — that you remembered, perhaps, from a paper the post had worked at the day you came in; there was a name on the fifth writ — Mehri at the south-gate — that you did not. There was a name on the sixth — at the imperial road east at the third milestone — that the desk-clerk's hand had written with the small particular care a desk-clerk reserved for names whose carrying-out the post-priest had taken pains, the night before, to underline. The name was an old name. The name had two short consonants at the front and a long open vowel at the end, in the southern fashion, and the body that read it knew, by the slow turning at the centre of the chest, that the name was a name a question-priest's apprentice had once written into a paper at Khaboran without writing it into any drawer the order had since let him read.

The corporal at the door said, without looking at you: "The leader has set the bell of three for the sixth, friend. The other five are by the noon. The grey at the stable is the post's. The pannier at the side-board is your hand's. The desk-clerk will read the column at the noon's first bell."

The desk-clerk's quill rested at the rim of the inkwell. The empty column at the head of the daybook waited.
