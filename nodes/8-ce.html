Cyrus's Letter at the Bothy

A small stone winter-bothy at the cold spring above the southern spur, the bowl's saddlebags piled at the back wall, a thin runner with the dust of Asha's ridge at his shoulders standing at the doorway with a folded letter in his hand, and Behdad at the leader's stone with the falchion across his knees and a face that has not, in the bell since the runner came up, taken its eye off the letter at the door.

The morning after the bell of the third hour at the foot of the spur — whatever that morning had been — had become the morning the bowl had ridden east through the deep country. The captain's twenty had been at the bowl's back, or at the bowl's saddle in the small careful canvas-wrapped bundles eleven horses can carry between them off a country road, or at the small grey dust at the foot of the dust-coloured outcrop at the second milestone south of Sadaiziche where the morning's bell of the noon had been; or the captain's twenty had been at none of those, because the bowl had dissolved over the lip at the back goat-track and ridden east through Asha's pass at the small unhurried pace fifteen men on country horses kept when no morning's bell was at their back. Either of the mornings had taken the bowl east. The cold spring was at the small upper fold past the southern spur where the country's stone went grey-white. The winter-bothy at the spring was a low stone house of four walls and a roof of slate the bowl kept at the small particular winter-station of the new moon.

The bowl had come up at the bell of the noon of the second day. The fifteen men were thirteen now — two were at small particular country graves south of Asha's pass — or were fifteen still — depending on the morning the bowl had ridden east out of. The brother at the back wall was at the back wall again. The leader's stone in the bothy was the same flat slab of grey-white the bowl had set at the back of the bothy's hearth at the year of the open winter; Behdad's hand at the falchion's grip had, in the four hours since the bothy had drawn its breath in at the cold spring, been the small careful hand of a leader whose afternoon at his stone had not yet, by the bell of the fourth, decided which of the three doors the bothy was at this season opening.

The runner had come up over the upper lip at the bell of the third. He had not, by his own walking these last bells, eaten. He was Asha's; the small star at his temple was the same small star the morning at Farzad's had come up by. He carried a folded letter in his hand. The letter was a piece of brown paper folded in three. There was a small piece of dark red wax at the spine. The wax bore the small print of a man's right thumb — a man's right thumb the body had read at a counter at a fence's door at Sadaiziche on an evening the body had not, at the time, quite been in the room for. Cyrus's thumb. The letter was Cyrus's.

The runner stood at the doorway. He held the letter out. He did not, by his own walking, come further into the bothy. The brother at the back wall did not stand. The leader at the stone did not stand. The bothy at the cold spring waited.

"From Bardas's door," the runner said. The voice was very thin. "Cyrus the younger brother of Yazdebar. The letter has been at my fire since the bell of the dawn yesterday. The bell of the dawn yesterday the wash-woman at the river-quay at Sadaiziche brought it up to my fire by the second goat-track. Cyrus wrote it at the back of the fence's door the night the bell of the midnight rang at Sadaiziche. He had heard, by Bardas's man at the inner counter, that the bowl was at the high spur above Farzad's. He had heard, by Bardas's clerk at the morning's bell, that the brother of the third name at the leader's stone was the brother whose hand had closed the third name's column at Yazdebar in the autumn of the open winter. He wrote it at the back of the fence's door in the small dry hand of a Mardesran who has had four years at the fence's stool to write a letter. He sent the letter up at the wash-woman's hand. The wash-woman put it at the second goat-track at the second hour past the dawn. I put the letter at the bothy at the third bell of this afternoon. I am not, brother, asked to wait."

He held the letter out in the doorway. He did not, by any small motion, look at the brother at the back wall.

Behdad set the falchion across his knees. He did not, by his face, lean. He looked at the letter in the doorway. He looked at the brother at the back wall. He looked at the cold spring at the lip outside the bothy. He looked back at the letter. He said, in the small even pitch a leader's voice took at his stone:

"Brother. The letter is at the door. The letter is in the hand of Cyrus the younger brother of the third name's house at Yazdebar. The third name was given up at the second hour of a winter morning by a boy of fifteen at the question-priest's apprentice's small careful four paces. The boy of fifteen was branded. The boy of fifteen walked. The boy of fifteen built a bowl. The boy of fifteen is at his own leader's stone. The boy of nineteen who took the third name's column is at the brother's back wall this afternoon. The letter is at the door." He set his hand flat on the falchion's grip. "Read it. Tell me, brother, what the letter is for. Tell me, by my own small careful patience at this stone these four years, whether the letter is for the bowl or for the brother at the back wall or for the leader at the stone. The bothy's evening is the bothy's evening. The cold spring at the lip is the cold spring at the lip. Speak first."
