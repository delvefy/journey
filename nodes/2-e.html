The Reeds

Three riders at the bend. A heretic under the mud. An empty road that wants explaining.

You press him down. The reeds, which had folded around him like reluctant attendants, accept him fully now — your hand on his good shoulder, his head turning into the wet bank with what may be the last of his strength.

"Lie still," you say. He does. He has been still for some hours; he knows the shape of it.

The charm at his throat is the thing the riders will see first. You take it between thumb and forefinger and dig it down into the mud, then bring up a fistful of the cold ditch-clay and work it into the silver until the little fish is only another wet stone. You smear the same mud across the line of his collarbone, across his cheek, across the back of his hand. He does not flinch. His eyes — open, still open — track yours with the patient attention of a man who has placed his life in another woman's keeping and now waits to see what kind of woman that is.

You stand. You climb back up the bank, careful with your bootprints, walking in the same places you came down. At the top of the bank, the road. You step onto its pale crown and turn your face east, away from Rabad, away from the bend behind which the hooves are growing into shapes. You braid your hair quickly with cold-fumbled fingers and pin it under your collar. It is the gesture of a woman who has been alone with strange men before, though you do not remember any of those men.

The first rider rounds the bend.

He sees you at once — a single dark figure on the empty road at the colour of dawn, exactly where blood has been spilled on the verge — and his hand goes to the rein in the small particular way of a man for whom the next gesture is the sword. The second rider rises behind him, then the third. They are uniformed, after a fashion: hard leather coats, the badge of the Khvordemian road-wardens on the chest, but with the additional white cord at the shoulder that you somehow know — without knowing how you know — marks a man riding under church writ. Hunters. Not merely keepers of the road.

They come up at a walk. They are in no hurry now. The blood on the road has answered some part of their question already. You stand with your hands open and visible at your sides, in a posture you find your body adopting without your asking, as if your body has answered questions of this kind before.

The leader stops his horse three paces from you. He is older than the others, narrow-faced, with the kind of slow eyes that miss very little. He looks at you, at the blood smear on the road's verge, at the reeds, at the road behind you. He does not look long at the reeds. He looks longer at you.

"Friend," he says, in a voice that is not friendly. "You are early on the road, and alone."

"Yes," you say. Your own voice is steadier than you would have credited.

"Did you pass anyone? A wounded man. Older. A coat like a tradesman's, but better wool."

The lie is in your mouth before you decide whether to tell it. Your tongue tastes it — the shape of it, the cost of it — and waits for you to choose.

Below the road, in the reeds, the man does not move. The youngest of the riders has dismounted and is walking, with no great urgency, toward the verge where the blood is. His eyes do not leave you as he goes. There is a particular smile beginning at the corner of his mouth that has nothing to do with the questions and everything to do with the hour and the road and the figure standing alone on it.

You have, perhaps, until he reaches the verge.
