The Brick-Floor Post at Bagh-e-Sang

A low brick room off the back of an imperial-road post a half-day south of Sadaiziche, a basin of cold water set beside the brazier, and an old man on the table whose hands are tied at the wrist with the small clean cord the order has been using at brick-floor posts since the empire's first inventory of the province.

The order had had you on the road since the bell of the fourth. The order had given you, by the strong-box at Karim's table or by the writ in the desk-clerk's pen, a grey at the stable and a brown leather pannier with three folded writs in it and a small clay flask of the dark eastern wine the order's field-men did not, by the order's habit, drink until the work was done. You had ridden the half-day south. The brick-floor post at Bagh-e-Sang was at the road-bend behind a stand of dust-coloured cypress, a single-storey building of new bricks the imperial road had paid for in the second year of the present bishop. The post-priest at Bagh-e-Sang had come out to the yard with the dull careful gladness of a man who had been told by the morning's runner that a colleague was on the road for him, and had taken your grey by the bridle, and had given you the door at the back of the post without yet asking your name.

The room behind the door was a low brick room with two small high windows, a brazier in the corner with a long iron at the coals, a small wooden table at the centre with a basin of cold water set beside it, and the man on the table.

The man was perhaps sixty. He was thin. His shirt had been cut up the front. His hands were tied at the wrist over his belly with the small clean cord the order had been using at brick-floor posts since the empire's first inventory of the province. He had not been worked on yet. The marks of an evening's holding were on him — the rope at the wrist, the small bruise at the cheekbone where a corporal had laid a slap to keep him at the question — but the iron at the brazier was a cold iron and the basin of water was a basin of water, and the post-priest at Bagh-e-Sang, by his own particular care, had been keeping the morning's instruments at their drawer until the colleague the runner had announced had come into the room.

The post-priest stood at the door behind you. He folded his hands.

"Friend," he said. His voice was the voice of a man whose voice had been at this door before. "Behshad. Almond-orchard, the small one south of Vahdat by a half-farsang. The charge is the Mardesran charge by hearsay. The witness is my own brother-in-law, who is a careful man and not, by the table's measure, a man much given to seeing a Mardesran where the country does not put one." He took a step forward, into the room, at the small careful pace a post-priest used in his own brick room. "The afternoon is, friend, yours. I have, by the morning's runner, the order's leave to have a brother of the office at my table. I have the iron at the brazier from the third hour of the night. I have the basin of cold water at the fourth. I have a small purse at the cabinet behind the door that is the brick-floor post at Bagh-e-Sang's small annual gift to the office of a question-priest who works a Mardesran case at this table." He smiled, briefly. The smile was the smile of a tired man. "The third year's harvest at Behshad's orchard is half-paid into Bagh-e-Sang's parish coffer. There is no son. The fourth year's harvest will be, by the man on the table not coming back to the orchard, the post-priest's at this door's full. I have no quarrel with sharing the fourth year with a colleague whose hand has done the morning's work at the iron."

He gestured at the table. The man on the table did not lift his head. The man on the table had heard the post-priest's small careful explaining and had, by some long discipline of his own, decided that the explaining was a piece of weather over a country he had been at the back of for sixty winters and was not, in the small bell ahead, going to outlive.

"There is a third name in the case-file, friend," the post-priest said, more quietly. "I have not yet read it to him. I have been told, by the brother-in-law's own caution, that the third name is the name of a man at Khaboran who has had reason to know the Daiyani family three winters back. I have a piece of paper in the drawer that wants signing at the bottom by a man who can, by his own seal, give the third name out at the imperial road. The third name is the morning's other piece of work. I will, friend, be at the desk through the door if you are minded to ask me to read the case at length. The cabinet behind the door is unlocked. The grey at the yard is fed. The bell of three is the bell."

He stepped back through the door, and laid the door against its frame without quite closing it, and his footstep on the brick of the front office went away.
