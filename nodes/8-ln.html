Faramarz's Folder Closes

A senior warden-post a day east of the previous, the room at the back of the post-priest's office set out with a long oak desk and four chairs along the wall, a brown-leather red-cord folder open on the desk's centre with a single sheet at the head, and Captain Faramarz of the second tower at Khaboran standing at the window in the small careful waiting of a man whose desk has been waiting four years for an afternoon's reading.

The morning at the previous post had been the morning's letter. Whatever the leader's voice at the seventh bell had read aloud — the first line in the room's hearing, the rest at the back of the post-priest's curtain or in the leader's own small even pitch at the front desk between two men — had been the letter the captain had written at the Khaboran circuit-office at the close of the previous afternoon by the small careful hand of a man whose desk had been waiting four years for an order in a column that had been writing itself back into the order's drawer at the wrong end of the empire. The corporal had brought up your grey at the bell of the dawn. The post-priest had stood at the door at the morning's leaving. The desk-clerk had set into your inner pocket — without your asking — a small folded packet of cold bread and a paper of dried apricots for the road.

The road east had been an empire road. The milestones had run at the proper farsang, the cypresses had stood at the proper interval at the verge, the third milestone had had a small Church post-priest at it who had stood at the doorpost and lifted a hand at your grey in the small particular acknowledgement of a careful man who had received word of a senior writ-rider on the road. You had ridden at the order's pace. You had eaten at the saddle. You had come up to the senior warden-post a half-bell after the bell of the third hour.

The senior post was the kind of post the imperial border force kept at a junction where the empire's lawful paper crossed the country's lawful paper at a desk that, by the office, settled which paper rode out under which seal. The yard was swept. The stable held seven horses. The post-priest stood at the gate; the leader of the post stood at the second door; the room at the back of the office had been set out at the morning's bell for an afternoon's particular reading.

Captain Faramarz was at the window when you came in by the inner door. He was the man you had seen at no level until this one — a man of perhaps fifty, narrow-shouldered, the dust-blue of the imperial border force at the coat with the silver olive at the shoulder more carefully kept than a working officer's coat usually was. The small white at the temple was a small careful white. The hands at the back were folded behind in the same small careful fold Hossein's had been at his bright table the morning the matron had been minded to read the name aloud. The folder on the desk was the brown-leather red-cord folder, open at its first leaf, with a single sheet at the head and the captain's pen and the order's stamp on the side-board.

"Khorshid Daiyani." He did not turn. He spoke into the window. "Field-man, southern circuit, the Sadaiziche-coastal route. Reported absent without leave at the morning of the second snow of the year of the open winter. Column carried at half-pay against the matron of the year of the second snow's recommendation. The order's drawer. The empire's folder. The bishop's folder, also, by the morning's last small careful hand at Sadaiziche's. Three drawers. One column. One afternoon." He turned. He came to the desk. He sat. He pointed, at the small careful gesture of a captain who knew the angle of his own desk, to the chair opposite. "Sit, friend. I have read the morning's letter. I have read the letter that came down with it from the matron. I have read the bishop's small particular note also. I have ridden a day to come up to this desk on an afternoon I had begun to think the empire would not give me. Sit."

You sat. The chair held the body in the small careful upright the empire's chairs had been holding the empire's field-men's bodies in for as long as the empire had had field-men. The captain set his pen at the inkwell. He turned the sheet at the head a half-turn so the head of the column read at his side of the table.

"Three lines, friend." He set the pen between two fingers. "The first line is the closing of the column. The second is the rate at which the closing pays. The third is the small particular column the closing opens — or the small particular small space at the end of the desk's drawer that the closing fills. I have my hand on the pen. The order has its hand on the rate. The drawer has its hand on the third. Speak first. Tell me, by my own ear in this small back room at the bell of the third hour, in what hand the three lines are to be written."
