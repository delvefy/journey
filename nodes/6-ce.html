The Bowl's First Ride

A small high ridge at the bell of the fourth hour of a grey morning, eight riders strung along the spur in the small careful order a leader's band rides in when the leader has set them at a particular work, and a low country house at the foot of the long slope with the morning's first smoke at its chimney.

The bowl had taken you. The bowl had taken you by a particular door — Behdad on the leader's stone, or the smoke at the bend of the burning farmstead, or the rooflight of the dyer's loft over a market town where a Mardesran woman had been at the willow and had not, by the bell of the morning, walked away again — and the bowl, by the morning of the next day, had decided what to make of the door. There had been a hide-fire at the back wall of the bowl through the grey hour, and at the hide-fire the leader had laid the morning's work into the air the way a man lays the warp into a loom: by small even gestures, in the small even voice that men with a fire's back at their shoulder had been laying down such warps in the country since the country had been the country. The morning's work was a country house at the foot of a long slope, three farsang up the coast-track from the bowl, on the imperial road's side of a low ridge.

The man at the house was a small landlord named Farzad. Farzad held three small almond-orchards on the slope and a piece of the river-bottom land at the back of them and had been, for the past four winters, the empire's listed tax-collector for the country-houses between the slope and the coast. The leader had named Farzad at the fire in the small flat voice of a man whose accountancy did not, in its naming, much distinguish a tax-collector from a post-priest. Farzad had, by the leader's voice, perhaps three days past sold the Mardesran widow at the second orchard to the Bagh-e-Sang post for the small parish bounty the post had been paying the imperial road's tax-collectors for such sales since the present bishop. The widow had a son at the bowl. The son was on the third horse along the spur. The son was perhaps fourteen. He had not been allowed to weep at the fire and was not weeping now.

You had been given a horse and a short heavy falchion of the bowl's pattern and the second rider's place along the spur, behind the leader at the head and ahead of the son. The bowl had not, in the giving, said a word. The bowl had read your morning's silence and the cold practised thing at your right hand and the Khaboran fold at the third button at your coat and had decided, by the small grunts at the fire that men of a bowl made instead of opinions, that the morning was a morning the second-rider's place would do for the new face. The morning was the morning. The hooves on the spur were the careful hooves of horses ridden by men whose horses had learned the spur in the dark.

The country house at the foot of the long slope was visible from the spur's third bend. The first smoke at the chimney was the morning's. The yard at the front of the house had two long carts and a brown ox and a small boy with a pail of water at the well at the back. The boy at the well was not yet awake to the spur. The house had a small enclosed garden at the back with a low wall and a wooden door at the side, the door the bowl's plan, by the leader's quiet word at the fire, was the door the leader and the first three riders would take. The morning's plan after the door was a plan the bowl had used at three country houses already this season and had not, in the using, given an account of itself to any party but the bowl.

The leader at the head of the spur lifted his right hand at the third bend. The spur stopped. The eight horses bunched in the small economical bunching of horses whose riders had been on a spur together for six winters. The leader did not turn his head. The son on the third horse did not lift his face. The country was a long grey country down the slope. The bell of the fourth hour was somewhere east at the imperial road and would not, by this slope, reach the country house at the foot for another quarter-bell yet.

The leader's voice came back along the spur in the small low carry a leader's voice had when the leader had ridden the same spur enough times.

"The second rider. Speak first."

The eight horses waited. The morning's first smoke at the chimney of the house at the foot of the long slope was the colour of the morning. The body, by the slow careful turning at the back of the chest, had already decided the three things it could speak at the lifting of the leader's hand. The body had not, by your own mind's part of the deciding, yet been asked which of the three the morning was being made for.
