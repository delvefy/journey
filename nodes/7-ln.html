The Sealed Letter from Khaboran

A warden-post's main room at the bell of the seventh hour of a long day, the leader at the desk with the day's daybook half-closed, and a single folded letter under the heavy brown wax of the Khaboran circuit-clerk that the corporal has laid at the centre of the desk without yet breaking the seal.

The day at the post had gone the way the daybook's open column had said it would go. Five writs were ridden out and ridden back; the sixth was still under the desk-clerk's pen because the body at the third milestone had been a body that, by the morning's particular accountancy at the south-gate, had taken longer at the bringing-in than the post-priest had penciled. The sixth was closing now, slowly, in the small careful hand the desk-clerk used at the end of a long day. The fourth of the morning's writs had paid the post the small statutory fee at the side-board; the third had not, by the desk-clerk's small note in the margin, paid anything. The leader with the slow eyes was at the desk. He had not yet, by the bell of the seventh, gone back through the curtain. The leader was, by the unhurried look of him in his chair, intending to close the day's column himself.

The runner came in at the small grey hour before the seventh bell. He was a boy of perhaps sixteen with a Khaboran post-runner's pin at the shoulder and a leather pouch at his hip. He had ridden a long way, by the dust at the boots, and was not, by the dust at the knees, the kind of post-runner who had been on a horse long. He laid a single folded letter at the corner of the desk in front of the leader, took the leader's small sign at the receipt-book the desk-clerk pushed at him, took the half-copper the corporal pressed into his palm for the kindness, and went out at the door at the small unsteady pace of a boy who had not yet eaten dinner.

The leader looked at the letter. The leader did not, at once, reach for the seal-knife. He looked, instead, at you on the bench by the back wall, where you had been since the sixth writ's bringing-in at the bell of the sixth. He did not, by his face, change.

"Friend," he said. He was a careful man at the small particular hour of the day a careful man minded most. "The Khaboran circuit-clerk's wax. The Khaboran circuit-clerk does not, by his own hand, write to the post at the close of a day unless the close of the day at his own desk has something at the bottom of a column that has not been there at the bottom of any other day's. The letter is at the centre of the desk. The seal-knife is at my hand." He set the small bone-handled seal-knife between two fingers in the absent gesture of a leader who was, by long habit, only ever absent on purpose. "I do not, friend, by the morning's runner at first bell and the captain's folder at three and the daybook's column at the head, suppose this letter is not for your reading. I am, however, the leader of a post. The post's letters are read at the post's desk by the post's hand. I will, by my office, break the seal. I will, by my own habit, read the first line aloud. The room will hear the first line. After the first line the room will, by my own decision, hear what the rest of the letter contains or it will not." He looked at the corporal at the door. He looked at the desk-clerk at the pen. He looked at you again. "I have, friend, an interest in your morning. I would not, by my own person, be sorry to know what the letter intends my afternoon for. Speak first. Tell me how I should — by the office and by your own person — read the letter."

The seal-knife rested at his fingers. The wax at the letter was the heavy red-brown of the order's official, not the bishop's. The first line, by the small visible bleed at the bottom of the fold, was a long line that began with the empire's full salutation and not with the order's shorter one. The room — by the small careful unwatching of two men at posts they had each been at for many years — was offering you the desk.
