Behdad in the Hide-Fire

A bowl of stone in the ridge above a coast-track, a fire banked low against the back wall of the bowl, and a tall man on the leader's stone whose eyes do not leave you from the moment you come down off the lip.

The country had brought you here by a way of getting here that the country had chosen on your behalf. There had been smoke at a bend. There had been three horses watered at a far spring. There had been a rider on a low ridge who had read you across the half-farsang at a glance and lifted one hand and pointed up the spur, not down it. You had gone up. The bowl of stone in the ridge above the coast-track was the shape of the inside of an old mortar — limestone, weathered, two paces lower at the centre than at the rim — and the fire at the back wall had been banked into a long low line of coals because a fire in a stone bowl made a wind of its own and a wind of one's own at the wrong hour was the kind of thing a coast-warden's eye picked out at four farsang. There were perhaps fifteen men in the bowl. Three of them were eating. Two were sleeping. The rest were waiting, in the small economical waiting of men whose afternoons had given them nothing to spend.

The leader's stone was at the back wall by the fire. The leader was on it. He was a tall man, taller than the men around him by half a head, with a long black beard turning grey at the chin and a coat of plain dark wool over a leather under-shirt and a short heavy falchion across his lap with the leather wrapping at its grip darkened by the working of a thumb at the same place over many seasons. He did not stand when you came down the rim. He did not gesture. He watched you the length of the descent — six paces from the rim to the bowl-floor, four more to the fire — and he did not, at any pace of those ten, take his eyes off your face.

You came up to the fire. You did not, by any small movement, set yourself in a fighting stance; the body had read the bowl and the fifteen men and the leader on the stone and had decided, before you had decided, that the bowl was not a bowl any one body got out of without the bowl's leader saying so. The body had read this the way the body read most things now. You stood at four paces from the leader's stone with your hands open at your sides and waited.

The leader looked at you. He looked at the small folded knife you had not, since coming over the rim, touched. He looked at the morning's coin at your hip. He looked at the side of your face where, by some accident of carrying, a piece of the morning's smoke had laid a small streak of black under the cheekbone. He looked, last, at your eyes, and his face did not move when the looking arrived there.

"Khorshid."

He said it almost gently. The bowl heard it. None of the men around the fire stopped what they were doing — three were still eating, two were still sleeping, the rest were still waiting — but the small particular silence that a name gets when fifteen men in a bowl agree, without speaking, that the name is the night's first business, came up under the coals' small noise like a stone coming up under a stream.

"Behdad of the river-house at Yazdebar." His own naming. He did not stand. "You sat across a table from my brother Cyrus in a room at Khaboran nine winters past. You took my father's throat to be the third name on a page my father had not, in life, been able to read. I was the second name. The order let the second name come up out of the back room because the second name was a boy of fifteen who had given up the third at the second hour and had nothing more left to give. The second name walked. The second name took the brand the order gives a Mardesran it has taken once, and the second name walked east, and the second name has been the leader of this bowl for six winters now." He shifted the falchion on his lap. He did not lift it. "Cyrus has been at a fence's door in Sadaiziche three years for me. I had thought it would be his door you came to first. The country has, by my own surprise, brought you instead to mine. I do not know whether to be grateful to the country or to take this as the country's particular cruelty."

He breathed out slowly. He set the flat of his hand on the falchion's grip — not closing it round the grip, only setting the flat of the hand against the leather — and the small smoothing of the thumb at the leather where the leather had been darkened in a thousand other small smoothings began again, slowly, at the rate of a man's own pulse.

"There is a thing the bowl does to a man it has been waiting nine winters for," he said, "and the bowl will do it tonight if I do nothing. There is a thing I could ask the bowl to do, which is the slower thing, and the bowl would do that also. There is a third thing, which I have not yet — by my own surprise at the country — decided whether to give a man like you, on a night like this one, the chance of. Speak first, friend. Then I will speak. Then we will both see what the bowl gets."
