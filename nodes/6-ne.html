6-ne · The Other Counter

The Other Counter

A back room behind a wine-seller's shop in a town the chart called Karkand, a heavy curtain across the door to the inner trade, and a fence's man at the inner counter who has lifted his face from the small lamp's edge and is now reading the lining of your coat the way another fence read the same lining last night.

The morning had handed you out by some particular gate the morning preferred. The fence at Sadaiziche had been dealt with — the candle at the counter, the man at the door, the purse at the hip, the package at the elbow — or the trooper at the verge had been dealt with, or the wash-woman at the trough had been dealt with, or the woman behind the cloth-stall had been dealt with, and the morning, in every case, had stayed on the road by no small piece of luck and the quiet feet the body had taught itself before the body had remembered the teacher's name. You had walked west by the dust of a low cart-road for two bells past sunset, slept three bells under a hedge of dry tamarisk, and come into Karkand at the small grey hour with the morning's purse and the morning's package — or the package's smaller cousin, the things the morning's first counter had not, by its own taking, finished with — in the inner pocket of your coat at the third button.

Karkand was a small town. Karkand had no Church post and no warden's stable. Karkand had four wine-sellers, two coopers, a farrier, and a man with a back room behind one of the wine-sellers who took, by his own quiet bell, what the country brought him at the back door between the second and fourth hours of the afternoon. You had come into the back room at the bell of the second. You had paid the boy at the curtain a half-copper to be let through. The boy at the curtain had not lifted his face.

The man at the inner counter was perhaps forty. He had a heavy embroidered coat over a yellow shirt and at his shoulder a small bone-handled awl on a cord. He had a lamp at the counter's edge — a small lamp, an oil lamp, the wick at a low even burn — and a brass weight on a piece of folded leather and the kind of small careful book a fence's man kept his prices in. He had looked at you at the curtain and looked away. He had taken your half-copper and gestured with the awl at the bench. The bench was the bench a man waited at while the man before him finished his business. The man before you was a country woman with a small leather rope of brass rings; the rings were the marriage-rings of country women whose husbands had died at last spring's flood; the fence's man at the inner counter was reading them at the lamp's edge in the small precise way he read.

The country woman left. The bench was empty. The fence's man at the counter set the brass rings into a small pouch behind him, wiped his hands on the side of the yellow shirt, and lifted his face to you.

He looked at you a moment longer than he had looked at the country woman with the rings. Then he looked at the right cuff of your coat. Then he looked at the lamp.

"Friend," he said, very evenly. "I will say a thing, by the bell, that does not, by my house's habit, get said at this counter. Bardas's man at Sadaiziche has a man at his door whom he is, by the small particular bell of this house's morning, very interested to be at the door of. Bardas's man's runner reached me at the second hour of the morning by the river-road. The runner was a boy with no shoes and a fast knee. The runner had a face for me to be looking for that the runner had been given at the back door of the alley off the slaughter-yard. The face had a coat. The coat had — by the runner's own particular wording — a Khaboran fold at the lining at the third button." He set his hands at the edge of the counter, palms down, in the way a man set his hands who wanted both hands seen. "I do not, friend, by my own preference, be at the door of Bardas's man's quarrels. I am at this counter to do the kind of trade my counter does. The runner has gone west by the river-road. He will be at the next house at the bell of the fourth. The next house is, by the river-road, the only other house between Karkand and the western salt-line that I know of. The bell of the fourth, friend, is two bells off."

His hands stayed flat. The awl at his shoulder did not move. The small lamp at the edge of the counter burned at the wick.
