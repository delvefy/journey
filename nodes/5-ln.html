Captain Faramarz at the Desk

A warden-post in the morning of a new bell, a leather folder out of a saddle-pannier laid across the desk's centre, and an officer of the imperial border force who has come into the room with your face in his hand.

You had been at the post a little before the bell. The corporal at the door had taken your pin and your folded writ and laid them on the side-board with the morning's other returns — three pins, two writs, a small stack of stamped affidavits weighted with a piece of river-slate — and had bid you sit on the bench by the window until the leader was free for the day's closing. You had sat. The morning was the kind of morning that puts long pale light into a one-window room, and the long pale light was on the bench beside you in a slow rectangle that had crept perhaps two fingers down the bench in the bell you had been sitting on it. The desk-clerk was at the desk. The leader was in the back room. The corporal at the door was reading the daybook with a thumb at the bottom of the column.

You heard the hooves first. Two horses, ridden well, drawing up at the post's outer rail. The corporal closed the daybook. He stood. He went to the door before the knock came. He opened it before the knock.

"Captain," he said, in the small voice a corporal uses to an officer he was not expecting before the bell.

Two men came in. The first was a senior officer of the imperial border force — the dark blue coat with the silver olive at the shoulder, the heavier sword at the hip, the long dust-stained riding-cloak over one arm — and behind him a younger officer with a leather pannier under his arm and a clerk's neat ink-cuff at the wrist. The corporal showed them to the desk. The desk-clerk stood. The leader came out of the back room at the small particular speed of a man who has not been told, and the leader and the captain exchanged the two small particular nods of two men who had ridden the same country at different times and did not, by the nods, need to be introduced.

"Captain Faramarz," the leader said. He gestured to the desk. "Sit. The bell is not yet. There is tea. There is the morning's daybook. There is — by the corporal's pile on the side-board — the morning's returns, which I had thought to read out to you over the bell. You are an hour ahead, captain. I am pleased to see you."

"The morning rode well." The captain laid his cloak across the back of the chair. He did not sit. He went to the side-board first. He took up the small stack of stamped affidavits in his right hand and the folded writ — yours — in his left. He read the writ. He read the affidavits. He read them in the unhurried order of a man who reads as a kind of breathing. He set the writ back on the side-board. He set the affidavits on the desk. He turned, with the leather pannier the younger officer had set on the desk, and opened it, and drew out a single brown leather folder bound at the spine with red cord.

He laid the folder on the desk. He opened it. The top sheet of the folder bore, in a careful clerk's hand, a name written across the head of the page. The captain did not, when his finger went down the page, read the body. He read the head. Then he looked up, and across the room, at the bench by the window.

He held your eye for the length of three breaths. He did not let his face show the recognition. The recognition was, by the leader's mouth and the corporal's ear, not yet a thing the room had. The leader at the desk had not yet read the head of the page.

The captain spoke to the leader without taking his eye off you.

"The morning's writ on your side-board," he said, "is signed in a hand I have a folder for. The folder has been at my desk in Khaboran four years. The folder's first sheet bears a name the order does not, by its own habit, write outside the order's drawer. I will read the name aloud, with your permission, friend, because the man at the bench will not, by my reading of his face, be surprised to hear it. Khorshid Daiyani." His eye did not move. "Take the bench, leader. Sit, corporal. The bell is not yet, and there is a thing the morning's returns have brought to my desk that the order has been minded, four years, to settle."

The desk-clerk's pen lifted off the page. The corporal sat. The leader, at the captain's small nod, took the bench by the back wall. The captain did not, yet, take the chair. He stood at the desk with the folder open under his hand, and his eye on you, and waited.
