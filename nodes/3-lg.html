The Clerk's Pen

The south gate before noon; a registry, a registry-keeper, and a lie that has come to be set in ink.

The road delivered you to Sadaiziche before the bell. You came in slow because you were holding him — one of his arms across your shoulders, the other across his own ribs as if to keep something in place that would otherwise leave — and the gate-wardens watched you the length of a hundred paces, two of them at the south gate's brazier, neither moving. The west wall of the town opened on your left into a stink of dye-vats and wet wool, and beyond the wall the river ran low and brown under a sky beginning to pale. The bell was perhaps a quarter-hour off. You passed under the arch.

The warden-house sat against the inside of the wall, plaster cracking off in slow grey scales, a stable-yard on one side and on the other a single door set above one shallow step. You knocked. The door was opened by a clerk no older than yourself — narrow shoulders, ink on the side of his hand, the small particular squint of a man who lives indoors by reed-lamp. He looked at the wounded man, at you, at the blood gone tacky on the wool, and his expression did not change.

"Walk him in," he said. "The riders are not yet returned. There is a bench."

You walked him in. The room was one room: a table set across the back wall, the registry open on it under a stone, a brazier with the coals dying, a bench along one wall on which sat already an old woman with a basket of dried fish and a boy bandaged about the head. The clerk closed the door behind you. He did not bar it.

He sat. He took up his pen. He dipped it once. He did not look up.

"The leader," he said, "left word an hour past, of a man to be brought before the bell. A kinsman. From the vineyards above the town. Daiyan, was the name." He wrote a small mark in the margin. "Yes?"

"Yes," you said. Your voice was a stranger's voice and it was steady. The wounded man, on the bench, did not move. His eyes were half-shut. A small tic ran along his jaw.

"The vineyard." The clerk's pen was patient above the page. "Above what milestone, friend."

You named the second. Your tongue knew the lie before your mind had reached for it. The clerk wrote it down.

"The father's name."

You named one. Khorshid. The name surfaced the way Daiyan had — out of nowhere with the small bright certainty of a coin found in a pocket whose owner you did not remember being. The clerk wrote it.

"The mother." He did not look up. "And the price, friend, of last year's grapes — by the man not the cart, your kinsman's father would know — and the year your kinsman was confirmed in the Church. And the name of the deacon who confirmed him." His pen waited. "Take your time, friend. The bell is not yet."

You felt the silence of the room arrive at you. The old woman with the fish was not pretending not to listen. The boy with the head was not pretending not to listen. The wounded man on the bench did not open his eyes; only his jaw worked once, very slightly, as if to swallow.

The clerk wrote nothing. He waited the small space of three breaths. Then — and only then — he looked up.

He was reading you. He had been reading you since the door. The reading was not unkind. It was the reading of a man whose work was making writing match the world, who had seen many lies pass across the desk and had a way of looking at the liar that gave the lie a chance to come apart on its own. He looked at your hands first. He looked at the cut of your boots — those Persian boots that did not match your purse. He looked at the small white scar above your wrist where the cuff had ridden up when you laid the wounded man on the bench. He looked at your jaw. He looked, last, at your eyes, and he held them.

"Friend," he said. "You have given a name to a clerk before. I cannot say where. I can say that you sit on that side of the table like a man who has sat on it. Sadaiziche has only two vineyards above the second milestone, and neither has had a Khorshid in it inside ten years. The man on my bench is not your kinsman. He has the wrong hands for a man who picked his own grapes." His pen had not moved. "I am to write what is brought to me. I will write what you tell me. The riders return at the bell."

Outside, very faintly, hooves entered the gate.
