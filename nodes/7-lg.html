The Magistrate's Second Hand

A wayside post at the bell of sundown of the same long day, a magistrate-father's grey at the rail and a second grey beside it, and a folded packet of three writs the morning's runner has brought down from Khaboran with a red cord at the spine.

The day's work was at the desk by the time the long light came in at the window. The writ at the third line was signed and stamped at the post-priest's office somewhere south or back at the originating post; the man on the brick-floor table was at his own kitchen by the bell of four with the rope-mark still at his wrist; the daybook's open column at the warden-post was closed for the day with three of the six writs ridden out and the fourth on the desk-clerk's pen. The corporal had taken your grey by the bridle when you came back in at the bell of the seventh hour and had not, by his small careful unwatching, asked you the manner of the day. The post-priest had laid a cup of pale tea by your hand and a heel of barley-loaf beside it and had gone back through the curtain to his own desk. You had eaten. You had not, by the eating, much tasted the loaf. The body had walked or ridden a long bell longer than the body had been minded to.

The hooves at the road outside the post came up at the small slow pace a senior rider used when he meant to be heard. Two horses. The corporal at the door closed the daybook for the second time that day. He stood. He went to the door. He opened it before the knock.

"Father," he said, in a small voice that knew the rider.

Magistrate-Father Hossein came into the room without ceremony. He was dustier than he had been at the bright bishop's table; the long grey wool robe at the hem had taken half a farsang of road-dust at the lining, and the small gold cross at the collar had a thin powder of the same dust at the upper arm of the cross. The folded pair of reading-spectacles was at his breast in their leather case. The bundle of files was under his arm again, thicker than it had been the first time you had seen it. The younger officer who had ridden with the captain at Khaboran — or another like him, with a leather pannier under the arm and a clerk's ink-cuff at the wrist — came in behind him and stayed at the door without sitting.

"Sit, friends, sit." The magistrate set his bundle on the long table by the window. He set the spectacles on top. He looked at you. He looked, briefly, at the desk where the day's signed writs were piled. He looked at the post-priest at the curtain. He did not, in any of the looking, let his face show what the looking read.

"Khorshid." He let the name settle, as he had let it settle in his first room. "I have ridden today by the same road you have, in the opposite direction. I have read at the post-priest's at Bagh-e-Sang the writ at the third line in your own hand under my name. I have read at the south-gate at Sadaiziche the desk-clerk's column at the head of his daybook in your own hand under my name. I have read at the riverside-quay the affidavit on Hamza the dyer's brother in your own hand also." He sat. He did not, yet, open the bundle. "The order has not, in four years, had a column closing in the manner this day's three sheets close it. I am not, friend, by my office, the man who reads a day's three sheets and tells the man who wrote them that he is pleased. The office is the office. I am, in my own person, in this small room at this hour, pleased."

He let that sit between you a small breath. Then he opened the bundle. He set, on the desk where the day's writs were piled, a folded packet bound at the spine with a red cord. The packet had three writs inside it, by the thickness, and a single covering sheet at the front. The covering sheet bore an old name at the head — a name the order had been minded to read these last four years — and three small place-names underneath that the body, by some slow turning at the back of the chest, registered before your mind quite had time to.

"The order has had your column open four years," Hossein said. "The order has, by the morning's runner from Khaboran, three more columns it would like a careful hand to read. The covering sheet names a man who has been at Khaboran a long time without the order's leave. The three place-names underneath are the three southern stages of his country. I am the magistrate, friend, not the field-man. I will write the day's three sheets at the bottom of my own circuit-record at Khaboran by the bell of tomorrow's first. I have come, in addition, to ask you what hand the order's red-cord packet would, by your own preference, fall under." His own hand, brown and dry, rested at the edge of the packet. "You may decline. You may take. You may, by a third habit, take and not write the closing in the order's drawer at all. I have my own ear for the third." He did not, by his face, lean. "The morning's bell is the bell. The packet is on the desk."
