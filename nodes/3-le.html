The Indenture-Broker

A small table in the shade of the south gate, a ledger with three columns, and a man who has already named your trade.

You came down the last quarter farsang into the town the way travellers come into towns when they have somewhere to be in them. The pace was not the pace of a man with nothing on him. The pace was not the pace of a man with everything on him either. It was the pace of a man who had spent his morning's accountancy and was on his way now to collect the consequence; and the wardens at the south gate, who had stopped looking at men an hour ago, did not look at you. You passed under the arch.

The market-shanty inside the south gate had not yet opened in full — the morning's trade was in cloth and bread and the bright wet smell of a butcher's first cuts — but the unofficial market outside the wall, in the dust-strip between the gate and the first proper houses, was already settled into its quiet trades. Money-changers at one stall. A scribe with a low desk and a string of cheap reed-pens. A woman selling bound bundles of dried herb. And, in the shade of a faded canopy that had once been blue, a small neat man at a small neat table with a leather ledger open before him and a string of pierced copper tokens at his elbow.

He was watching you. He had been watching you, you realised, since the milestone; he was the kind of watcher who took the morning's count of arrivals as a matter of professional habit. When he saw you take note of him, he gestured — not summoning, more an offering, the way a host gestures to a chair — and you walked the dozen paces to his table because it would have been more conspicuous to refuse than to come.

"Sit, friend." He smiled. The smile did not reach the eyes, but the eyes did not pretend it did. "I am Yusef. Indenture-broker, by the Persian Church's licence — the certificate is on the post of the canopy if you would like to read it. Settlements of seven years, occasionally less. Debtors, orphans, the occasional petitioner who has put himself beyond his uncle's table. The town's clerks send their overflow to me." He tapped the ledger. "I do not, by trade, ask travellers what brings them through my gate. I ask what I might do for them."

You sat. There was nothing for it but to sit. The dust-strip was not somewhere a man stood still on without a reason that an indenture-broker's table would not buy.

Yusef poured a small cup of cold mint-tea from a clay jug. He did not push it toward you. He set it where you could reach it if you chose. He folded his hands.

"I will tell you what I see," he said, "because it is what you came to my table to be told, and because telling it costs me nothing and costs you only the small embarrassment of having been read. You are a traveller who walks like a man who has, this morning, transacted in a man. The walk is particular. The shoulders are at rest, the eyes are not. The boots are better than the coat — which is the configuration of a man on his way to dress himself further before nightfall. The four wardens at the gate did not look at you, which means either they have not yet been given your description or they have, and you are walking into the description on your own feet, which I do not believe; you are not stupid."

He paused. He tasted his own cup of tea.

"I do not need to know what you transacted in," he said. "I need to know what you would like to transact in next. Sadaiziche is a town with appetites. The Church's leader at this south gate, may his beard be long, pays a small but reliable rate for the names of certain travellers seen on certain roads — names you may not yet have given. There is a man inside the wall called Bardas who pays better than the Church for the same names, because Bardas keeps a small private prison and is presently short of bodies for his counting-house. And there is, in the alley behind the dye-yard, a woman called Mehri who has need of a steady hand to walk a particular child to a particular ship at the river-quay tomorrow at dawn — a settlement her late husband contracted, which she cannot, by her grief, herself complete." He smiled the small smile again. "Three doors, friend. I will draw the contract on whichever you knock at. The pen is the same pen."

The mint-tea sat between you. The wardens at the gate had not looked at you yet. The morning's coin in your purse — whatever had come into it, by whatever route — was a weight you had not yet set on any table. The blue canopy made the air under it a different colour to the air outside it.

Yusef waited. He had the patience of a man whose ledger had three columns and did not, by itself, care which of them was filled by the close of business.
