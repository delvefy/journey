The Bishop's Folder at the Brick-Floor Post

A brick-floor post's front office at the bell of the morning of the next day, the post-priest at a small triangular table with a fresh pot of tea, and a thick green-bound folder of the Sadaiziche bishop's office that has come down on the dawn-cart with three thumb-stained pages already turned at the spine.

The afternoon at the brick-floor post had been the brick-floor post's afternoon. The man on the table had — by the iron at the brazier, by the basin, by the case-file at the front office, by the third name out at the bell of four — been the office's; the half-purse at the cabinet had been the office's also, and the post-priest's particular share of the fourth year of the orchard at the side-board, and the small additional purse the post-priest had unlocked at the bell of seven with a small particular smile of fellowship. You had slept, in the small back room behind the office, on a wool-stuffed pallet the post-priest's cousin had made up; the pallet had been clean. You had woken at the bell before the dawn at the small careful rest the work had earned you. The post-priest had a fresh pot of tea on the small triangular table in the front office and a plate of dried apricots beside it. He had set out a second cup for you without asking. He had read your face as you came in by the curtain and had not — by the small particular pleasantness of a man whose afternoon's work had earned him the morning — said anything at all yet.

The dawn-cart had brought a thick green-bound folder.

The folder was at the centre of the triangular table. It was a folder of the Sadaiziche bishop's office, green linen at the boards in the bishop's pattern, the bishop's mark stamped in gilt at the upper corner; the post-priest had untied the spine-cord and had turned three thumb-stained pages and had laid the folder open at the fourth. He had then poured the second cup of tea and had pushed it across the table to your hand. He took up a small dried apricot. He chewed it without hurry.

"Friend," he said. "The bishop has a quick pen in the afternoon when an afternoon at a brick-floor post has been written into a case-file with the third name out. The folder is the bishop's standing case-list for the southern circuit. He has, in his own particular kindness, marked the three cases at the head of the list that the morning's runner thought best to bring down with the dawn-cart. The three cases are at the head of the folder. The fifteen cases below the three are the routine. The post can, by its own office, work the routine at the rate the post has been working the routine for the past four winters. The three at the head, friend, are not the routine. The three at the head are the cases the bishop has minded to ride the man at the cup of tea on." He smiled. "I am, by my own particular fortune, not the man at the cup of tea. You are."

He turned the folder to face you. The fourth page was the first of the three head-cases. The case at the head was titled in the bishop's clerk's hand at the upper margin: the matter of the half-fold at Behesht; brother of the order's question. The body of the page named a small house at Behesht — a village the body, at the slow turning at the chest, registered before the mind quite caught it — and a man at the house called Bahram, a cooper by trade, half-Mardesran by the mother's line, who had been minded for some seasons to make the small enquiries of a half-brother of the order that the bishop's eye had not entirely been able to ignore. The bishop's pen had asked, in the margin, whether the morning's hand at the brick-floor post might, by the post-priest's good office, ride out to Behesht for the small particular interview the half-brother had been earning by his enquiries.

The second of the three head-cases was at the next page. The third was at the page after.

The post-priest set the small dried apricot on his tongue. He chewed. He did not, by his face, hurry.

"The bishop's bell is the bishop's bell. I have, friend, my own bell on this morning's table. The bell of the noon is the bell of the post's routine work. The three head-cases are an afternoon's work or three afternoons' work depending on the hand. I have, by the side-board's half-purse this morning, the bishop's standing rate for each case at the head; I have, in the locked drawer at my own desk, my own rate also." He set the cup of tea at the edge of his hand. "Read the three pages. Tell me, by the half-bell, which of the three is the morning's first ride. Tell me, by the same half-bell, whether the bishop's rate alone is the morning's, or whether the morning has, by the small particular hand of last night's apricots, a share for the post in addition." He smiled again. "I have, friend, by my own honest accounting at three years at this brick-floor post, never had a colleague at my table whose first morning the bishop has been minded to write to in the green linen. We are, this morning, an interesting post."
