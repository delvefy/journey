The Column at the Bell of the Third

An upper writing-room at the south end of the third-floor corridor of the order's seminary house at Khaboran, the long pine desk under the east window with a fresh brown-leather folder of three cases at its centre and the matron's small particular pen laid at the inkwell's lip, and a young woman of perhaps nineteen in the dark-grey scarf of the order's outer house at the doorpost with the morning's first sheet face-down at her hand and the small careful waiting of a scribe whose desk-keeper has told her that the morning's first column is a column the first hand at the desk has not, in four years, been minded to read.

The bell of the dawn at the seminary house had been the bell the body had known. The matron had not, the previous evening, come back up from her chair by the south-stair parlour window; she had taken the pot of tea off the side-board at the bell of the fourth as she had said, and she had set the brown earthen-ware cup on its small thin saucer at the side-table's far edge, and she had spoken at the bell of the dusk to the brother at the gate in the small particular even pitch the matron's mothers had taught the order's gate-keepers at the year of the first snow. The brother at the gate had nodded once. The lemon-tree's one yellow fruit had — by some small careful motion of the matron's afternoon hand or by some small careful walking of the boy of forty's at the gate — been set into the matron's own lap at the bell of the fifth. The lemon-tree at the upper bough now showed a small bud at the place the fruit had been.

The cot in the matron's old guest-room at the back of the south stair had held the body the night through. The body had not, at any small particular bell of the night, woken. It had woken at the bell of the third at the small three-tone chime of the chapel-roof's bell-rope at the east wing's wall. There had been a small clay basin of cold water on the wash-stand at the corner. There had been a clean linen shirt folded on the chair. There had been a small dish of bread and white cheese at the side-table that the matron's scribe had set there at the bell of the second of the morning. The body had washed. The body had eaten. The body had put on the order's clean grey shirt at the small careful particular the matron's house kept its shirts at.

The upper writing-room at the south end of the third-floor corridor was the room the order's boys of the seminary's fourth year had been minded to come up to once a week at the year of the second snow for the master's afternoon exercise. The east window looked onto the lemon-tree courtyard at the south wall. A long pine desk sat under the window. A tall stool stood at the desk's near side; a lower stool sat at the further side for the scribe. The inkwell at the desk's right corner was the small bronze inkwell with the cypress lid the matron's own desk had carried these forty years. The matron's small particular pen — silver at the nib, olive-wood at the shaft, the small carved cross at the haft the body had not, in twenty years, seen at any other hand — lay at the inkwell's lip. A fresh brown-leather folder of three cases sat at the desk's centre. No red cord. The morning's three were the matron's morning's three.

You sat at the high stool. The stool held the body in the small careful upright the order's stools held bodies in. The matron's pen at the inkwell's lip was the pen the boy of fourteen had taken once at a small inner exercise in the matron's particular long-hand. The matron had let him take it without comment. The boy of fourteen had not, by his own honest accounting, been able to write the matron's long-hand at all.

The young woman in the dark-grey scarf of the order's outer house was at the doorpost. She was perhaps nineteen. The hair under the scarf was a small careful dark brown braided into the small inner fold the order's outer house wore at the back. The hands at the folder she held were small careful hands the seminary's scribe-master had been training these last three years. The matron's word the previous afternoon at the side-board had named her: Marziyeh, the first woman the order had set at the south-stair scribe's stool since the year of the second snow.

She crossed the four paces at the small unhurried pace the matron's scribes were taught to cross a writing-room at. She laid the folder at the centre of the desk. She did not, by any small motion, set her hand at the lip of the folder. She stood at the desk's right side.

"Brother." She did not, by her face, lift the voice. "The matron's word at the first hour was for me to lay the morning's three at the desk in the order I have laid them, and to wait, at no greater volume than the room has at the bell of the third, for the brother at the desk to read the morning's first." She bent. She turned the head sheet a half-turn so the column read at your side of the desk. "The case at the head is the matter of Maziar Karimi, brother. Taken at the bell of the noon the day before yesterday at the salt-flats four farsang south of Karkand. He is at the cell of the senior warden-post a day east of here. The post-priest's brother-in-law has read the column at the head and has — by the bell of the dawn yesterday — asked the matron's hand at the south stair whether the column at the salt-flats closes at the cell or whether the column is, by the matron's small particular fold at the corner, a column the order's first hand has the morning's reading of."

She set her hands at her front in the small careful fold the matron's had been in. She waited. The lemon-tree at the courtyard moved a single small breath of wind into the small bud at the upper bough. The morning's first sheet read, at its head, in the matron's small careful long-hand: Karimi, Maziar; seminary year of the open winter; left the order's column at the cold spring four years past; taken at the salt-flats by the post-priest's brother-in-law's hand; cell as above; brother at the desk's discretion.

The body sat at the matron's pen.
