Master Yazdan at the Ferry-Bench

A ferry-yard at a junction crossing of a small brown river, a long bench under a half-roof of cane at the inn-yard's wall, an old man at the bench with a piece of dry bread in his hand and a sleeping grey lurcher at his foot, and an inn-yard boy at the ferry-rope below who has been told to come up to the bench when the third man on the western bank has crossed.

You had walked out of the country inn at the lone walnut by the small kitchen door at the back, or sat the small round table at the chimney for the conversation of two men, or set the back of the right hand to the back of the man's neck and walked out at the kitchen with the description-sheet folded into the inner pocket. The night had taken you west by the cart-track. You had slept four bells in a small barn at the foot of a low ridge. You had eaten the inn-keeper's daughter's lentils at the cold of the dawn the way the body, by the small careful patience the body had been at all week, ate cold lentils at a dawn. You had walked the second farsang at a particular small even pace. The country had thinned at the foot of a long slow slope into the small flat meadow at the bend of a small brown river where the country crossed at a ferry the empire did not maintain.

The ferry-yard was the yard the country had set there because the country had been crossing the river at this junction for as long as anyone at the river minded. The yard had a small inn at the back wall, a half-roof of cane along the inn-yard's south wall under which a long bench ran, two donkeys at a trough, a goat at the inn's door, and a single small flat-bottomed ferry at the rope across the brown current. The bench under the cane was the bench the country waited at when the ferry's third crossing of the morning was not yet at the eastern bank.

An old man was at the bench. He had a brown coat the country had been bleaching for many afternoons. He had a piece of dry bread in his hand and a sleeping grey lurcher at his foot. He had no scarf, no cap, no staff at his elbow; he had a small leather satchel at the hip and a small carved cup at the belt's loop. The hands at the bread were the kind of hands a man's hands went to at the seventh decade when the rest of the body had been put by an early career into a small careful slow gear. He was, by any small particular reading of the country's bench, a man at the country's bench. He was Master Yazdan. He had set the third-week field-test for the boys of fourteen at the south stair's small inner room every February of Khorshid's seminary years.

He saw you at the yard's south wall. He did not, by any small motion, stand. He took a bite of the bread. He chewed the small careful chew of an old man whose teeth had not all been with him these last winters. He spoke without lifting the voice. The voice carried easily the eight paces.

"Friend. The ferry's third crossing of the morning is at the western bank. The boy at the rope has been told to come up to the bench when the third man on the western bank has put his foot at the boat-step. The boy will be up at the bench in perhaps three minutes. The bench has, at its further end, a small space for a man who has come down from the inn at the lone walnut at the bell of the dawn." He set the bread on the bench at his thigh. "I am at the bench, friend, because the bell of the dawn at a junction the empire does not maintain has, for many years, been an honest bell. I am not at the bench at any office. Sit."

He did not, by his face, ask anything. The lurcher at his foot turned an ear at the new voice and did not, by any other motion, decide. The boy at the ferry-rope had not yet, by his own small unhurried hand at the rope, finished the third pull.

You stood at the yard's south wall. The body had read the bench at perhaps thirty paces and had read, at perhaps twenty, the small particular angle the old man's right knee held at the bench's seat — the same small particular angle he had held at the south-stair's inner-room cushion every February of the boy of fourteen's seminary years. The body had not, by the seeing, leant. The body had stood at the wall.

The ferry-boy, at the rope, finished the third pull. He looped the rope at the post. He turned at the yard. He came up at the small unhurried pace of an inn-yard boy who had been told to come up when the third man on the western bank put his foot at the boat-step. He came to the bench. He stood at the master's elbow. He said, in a small voice, "The third has crossed, master. Shall I bring the ferry round for the bench's next?"

Master Yazdan did not answer the boy. He looked, instead, at you at the wall. The lurcher at his foot lifted its head a half-finger. The river went on between the banks.
