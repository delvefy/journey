The Forge at the Second Pruning-Shed

A small country forge in a half-built lean-to at the back of the second pruning-shed three farsang east along the spur from the cold spring, a charcoal fire under a clay-and-stone hood at the slope's bend, an old country woman at the bellows in a Mardesran's long grey coat, and a thin young runner at the lower path with the dust of two ridges at her boots and a small leather satchel of news the country off the empire's eye has read at the small star at her temple.

The cold spring at the upper fold had been the cold spring. The Reader of Behesht had — by the bell of the dawn or the bell of the dusk or by the bell of some careful particular afternoon's reading — closed the small leather-bound book at the corner where the woman's thumb had been, or had read the third page aloud at the small careful low pitch the circle used at a fire, or had laid the book open at the last leaf for the brother at the slope to write a name into and the brother at the slope had written it or had not. Soraya had come up the slope at the bell of the second of the morning to lift the small bowl off the goat-hide and to set a new pot of barley-and-fat at the small slow fire under the rock. The cousin's boat at the cove had gone south at first light with the boy at the bailing-bucket and the cousin at the sail and an old country bishop's-runner the network had picked up at the third cove. The half-roof under the basalt slot was at the morning's careful quiet a station-keeper kept it at when the morning's onward stage was at the slope's east.

You had walked the small ridge-line east along the spur with Soraya at the small unhurried pace the country at the southern spur allowed. The country here was country off the empire's eye. The imperial road kept three farsang to the north along the river. The cart-tracks kept to the willows. The donkey-paths kept to the contour at the small green folds. The country between had been folding into itself for two days at the small unhurried pace of feet that did not, by any small particular, hurry. The second pruning-shed sat at the foot of a low ridge of olives three farsang east of the cold spring. It was the same pruning-shed Hirsi had been the elder of at the first reading of the network's stages four winters ago. The shed was a low stone shed with a goat-hide door. A small forge had been built at a half-finished lean-to at the back.

The forge was a charcoal forge at a clay-and-stone hood the country had built off the bend of the slope where the wind worked at the bell of the morning. The bellows at the side was a small leather bellows the country had been working on for many winters. The old woman at the bellows was perhaps seventy. She had a Mardesran's long grey coat of the country's particular weave and a small dark blue scarf at the temple and the small star — a small five-pointed scar, no bigger than a fingernail's edge — at the white at her left temple under the scarf. She had a small iron bar in the fire and the small careful eye of a country forge-keeper who had been keeping a small country forge for many seasons. She lifted her face at your coming up with Soraya. She nodded once. She did not lift the bellows-hand.

"Khoda's road, brother." The voice was the small dry country voice the network's older woman used at a forge. "Soraya, child, set the bar at the second hook. The cousin's coat is at the bench. The brother's coat is on the peg. The morning's runner is at the lower path."

Soraya laid the small bundle she had carried at the bench. She lifted from the peg a long grey country coat the same weave as the woman's at the bellows — a coat for a man of forty, the cut the country wove for the small particular travellers the network put down its onward stages — and she laid it across the bench's near end. She did not, by any small motion, ask whether the brother was minded to put it on. She lifted the small kettle off the second hook and poured into a clay cup a thin tea the country brewed of mountain thyme.

The thin young runner came up the lower path at the small unhurried pace a country runner came up a lower path at when the morning's news was the news the morning's news was. She was perhaps twenty. The hair under the dark scarf was a small dark brown braided at the back. The small star at her temple was a small fresh thing — the empire had taken her once and let her go perhaps two winters past, and the scar had not yet weathered into the small dry white the older woman's was. The satchel at her hip was a small leather satchel the network's runners carried at the small careful angle the country had taught them.

She stopped at the four paces from the forge. She nodded once at the older woman. She lifted, by her gait at the right knee, her face to the brother at the bench.

"Brother. Anahid; the deeper country, three days east." The voice was very low. "The Khaboran circuit-clerk's description-sheet came up to the dyer's town the morning the brother went over the rooflight at the willows. It is at four wayside inns now along the southern circuit and at two country priests' tables. The bishop's pen at Sadaiziche has — by the bell of the dawn yesterday — set the matter of the half-fold at Behesht at the head of the green linen's morning. A small dust-blue riding has left the brick-floor post at the noon yesterday. The cooper at the next valley is, by the bell of this morning, the post's first standing column. The cooper's mother at Behesht is at the doorstep. The cooper's wife is at the bread-oven. The lane at the foot is, by the bell of the noon today, in the post's eye." She lifted the satchel half a hand. "I carry the second satchel for the cousin's onward stage to the deeper country. The first satchel is at the slope's last step under the basalt for the brother at the bench, by Soraya's word at the morning. The country, brother, has an afternoon of its own."
