The Rider Without a Cord

Down the irrigation ditch, two travellers in the wet reeds, and a horseman who knows both their names without asking.

The wardens went west. You stood on the road and watched them go — three white shoulder-cords riding hard into a low sun, their lead lengthening from a hundred paces to two hundred, the empire's patience burned at last by a lie you had set very lightly in their leader's hand — and only when the dust had folded over their backs did you go down the bank to the reeds and lift the man out. He was lighter than the morning had implied. He weighed less, perhaps, than the silver had cost him.

You did not take the road. You took the ditch. The irrigation channel ran east of the bend at the bottom of a long sloped trench overhung with dry reeds, and the water in it was a slow brown trickle the depth of two fingers — enough to take your footprints and not enough to slow you. The wounded man set his good arm across your shoulder. You walked. The road above you was empty now, and would be empty perhaps an hour, perhaps less, depending on how far west the wardens rode before the leader's slow eyes asked the obvious question.

You had gone perhaps a quarter farsang along the ditch when the horse came up behind you. You had not heard it. You had not heard it because it had come down off the slope through the dry vine-stocks at a walk on hooves wrapped in cloth, and that fact had registered before you turned, and you were already turning into the small cold space where the body knew what to do, the knife in the lining of your coat under your hand, when the rider on the horse said — quietly, not loudly, the way one calls down a well — baraadar.

He used the masculine; he used the feminine; he used both in the same breath, the old way, a greeting that did not need to know which of you he was greeting. He stopped his horse at five paces. He was a smaller man than his horse made him look. He wore no shoulder-cord. He wore a brown coat with no badge and a hood thrown back on his shoulders and at his throat — only because he wanted you to see it — a silver fish biting its own tail, the same as the wounded man's, but his was older, the silver gone dark where the thumb of the wearer had touched it a thousand times.

"Khahar. Baraadar." He nodded to each of you. "You are slow. The wardens will be back at the bend in half a bell, and the bend is six hundred paces. Help me lift him onto the saddle behind me."

You did not move at once. He did not press. He sat his horse and let you read him, which was — you understood, a beat late — what he had stopped his horse at five paces to permit.

"I am Behnam," he said. "I came down from the second vineyard when Asha sent word at first light. We knew this brother was coming up from Rabad. We did not know he would not arrive. We do not, by habit, ask travellers their names; we say the road tells us. The road has told us — about you — for nearly an hour, friend. The shape of bootprints up a bank and down the same. Three horses riding west who should be riding east. A fish in the mud that was not there yesterday and is not there now." His eyes did not leave yours. "You did a brave thing without owing us a brave thing. The road sees this. I see it."

He swung down off the horse. He was a head shorter than you. He had moved like a man who had been broken and put back together by his own hands. Up close, you could see the small star-shaped scar at his temple — a Mardesran's mark, the brand the empire put on those it had taken once and let go to make a lesson of them.

You and Behnam lifted the wounded man together onto the saddle behind the saddle's cantle, the way you would lift a sack of grain, except more careful at the ribs. The wounded man — your wounded man, by now, in some accountancy you had not signed for — let out a thin breath and held to the saddle's back. Behnam tied him there with a long strip of soft cord, around the waist, twice, in two quick knots that came from the same workshop as your own hands.

Behnam took the reins. He turned the horse, but did not yet mount. He stood at the bridle and looked back at you across the wet ditch.

"Friend," he said. "The road is sharp behind us, and you are on the wrong side of it. You can come up with us. The second vineyard above the town keeps a stable; Asha's house keeps a roof; from there a road runs south through country the wardens do not love and emerges, in two days, on a coast where the Church is a rumour. Or you can stay here and walk east in plain sight to Sadaiziche, and we will speak of you sometimes, and you will speak of us never, and we will not see each other again. The choosing belongs to you. The horse can carry one extra at a long walk. It cannot carry two."

He did not say what the third choice was. He did not need to. The four coins in your purse, the knife folded in your coat-lining, and a wounded man tied to a stranger's saddle made their own small accountancy at your hip with the weight of a question.
