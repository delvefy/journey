The Cup-Bearer's Hand

A goat-track, a hollow with a roof in it, and an old woman who does not ask whose blood it is.

You went south. Off the verge, through the second milestone's stripped vineyard, up into the oak scrub on the spur, and then along a goat-track that climbed without seeming to climb, the way old paths do. You carried him the first half-farsang and walked beside him the second; he insisted on his own feet when the slope allowed it, and the insistence took something from him you would not be able to put back. You did not speak. There was nothing to spend breath on. Above the second vineyard, where the oak gave way to thorn-broom and a thin grey turf, a column of smoke marked a shepherd's fire, and the shepherd — a woman in a man's coat, white at the temple — straightened from her cooking and watched you come, and did not call out.

"Asha," the wounded man said, when you were close enough. He said it the way one says a name that has been a key in many doors.

Asha nodded once. She looked at him, at you, at the slope behind you for a long beat. She did not ask. She pointed with the haft of her stick — east of north, along a fold of land you would have missed — and said, "Mira. The hollow with the elder-tree. The road-fish do not climb so high before the bell." Then she turned back to her pot, and the audience was over.

The hollow was perhaps a half-farsang on. You found it because the elder-tree she had named stood alone in a small drop in the ground, and under the elder was a doorway no taller than your shoulder set in a bank of turf, and over the doorway hung a small clay cup, painted blue — a votive, an old one, the paint worn off where many hands had touched it for luck. The shrine of Vabum the Cup-Bearer; a wayside one; a hospice in the old way, before the Church had made hospices its own. You ducked through.

The room beneath the turf was bigger than the doorway promised. A hearth burning low. A pallet against one wall, empty. A pallet against the other, occupied by a man whose breath rasped slow and even. Bundles of dried herb hanging from the rafters. A pot of water steaming on a hook. And by the hearth, an old woman — Mira, presumably — sat on a low stool with a clay bowl in her lap and a small grey cat at her feet, picking through a handful of dried sorrel as if it were the only matter the morning had brought her.

She looked up. She looked at the wounded man's coat. She looked at the silver fish you had not, by now, thought to hide.

"Lay him on the empty pallet," she said, in a voice the colour of the smoke. "The water is hot. There is broth in the small pot. Whatever you have done this morning, friend, do it again tomorrow. Today there is mending."

You laid him on the empty pallet. He grunted once and was quiet. Mira rose without hurry, set the bowl of sorrel on the stool, and crossed to him. She undid his coat with the brisk competence of a woman who had undone many. She did not flinch at the wound; she made a small dry sound at the back of her throat that might have been disapproval or might have been a prayer in a tongue you did not know. She washed the wound with the hot water and a piece of clean linen torn from a folded stack. She put a poultice on it. She bound it with strips. The wounded man's breath went deeper. The grey cat watched.

You stood in the doorway. You did not know what was expected of you. Mira did not look at you.

"The Cup-Bearer takes what comes to his door," she said, as if to no one. "I do not ask. The asking belongs to the road-wardens, and they have a desk for it down at the south gate of Sadaiziche, and you are not there." She tied off the last strip. She straightened. She turned to you, finally, and the look she gave you was clear-eyed and careful and entirely without judgment, which was harder to receive than judgment would have been. "You are also tired, friend. The other pallet is empty. The broth is for both of you. I have herbs to grind and a man on the far pallet whose fever broke an hour ago and who will want bread when he wakes. The day is long."

She returned to her stool. She picked up her bowl of sorrel. The hollow settled around you like the inside of a folded cloth. Outside, the day climbed. The shrine waited. The wounded man slept.
