The Girl at the Post Door

A small post-priest's room at the first bell of a new morning, a girl of perhaps fifteen on the bench by the door with a clean grey shawl pulled tight at her shoulders, and a brick-floor post a half-day's ride south that the post-priest will not, by his own habit, ride at without a writ.

You had slept under a roof. The roof had been a small upper room at the back of a way-house, or the long pine bench against the back wall of the magistrate's outer room, or a folded blanket the matron's niece at Vahdat had laid out at the foot of her kitchen fire — the morning did not, by its own light, much care which roof had given you the sleep. You had woken at the small grey hour before the bell and washed your face at the basin and sat for a long quiet moment at the bench by the window listening to the road wake. The name in your coat-lining, last night a thing said aloud across a table, had become a thing your own breath was now keeping inside the cage of your chest by the slow regular work of breathing.

The post-priest came in by the back curtain at the first bell with a folded sheet of vellum under one arm and a small earthenware cup of milk in the other hand. He set the cup beside you. He did not, at once, sit. He looked at the bench by the door. The girl on the bench was perhaps fifteen. She had a grey woollen shawl pulled tight at the shoulders against the small cold of the morning and a face that had been crying in the dark before the bell and was not crying now. She had come up to the post door before the door was open. The post-priest had let her in. She had been on the bench, in the post-priest's account at the curtain, for the length of two of his Pater Nosters before he had decided the morning was a morning that needed to wake you for it.

"Friend," he said. His voice was the careful voice of a man who had been a careful man for a long time. "There is a piece of work at the door. The piece of work is younger than my own daughter and it has come up the road at the small hour after midnight by a way I do not, by my office, want to ask the manner of. The piece of work belongs, by my parish, to the brick-floor post at Bagh-e-Sang a half-day south. The post-priest at Bagh-e-Sang is a man I have known fifteen winters and do not, friend, much love. He has a man on the table this morning — the girl's uncle by her mother — and the bell of three this afternoon will, by his own pen, be the bell the man does not, by his own throat, come back across." He laid the folded vellum on the table. He sat. He did not, yet, look at the girl. "I have, by your own mouth in this room last night, a paper with your hand at the bottom of it and the magistrate-father's name above it. I have, by the order's own habit, a writ-form open in my drawer. I have, friend, no man at this post but myself who is, by his own seal in the order, allowed to put a hand on the writ at the third line. The bell of three is not, by any road from this door, more than five bells from the bell ringing now."

He folded his hands.

"The girl is Sahar. The uncle is Behshad of the small almond-orchard. The charge is the Mardesran charge by hearsay; the witness was the post-priest at Bagh-e-Sang's own brother-in-law. I do not, by my own reading, believe the charge. I have, by my own office, no standing at Bagh-e-Sang's door. I will, friend, sit at my desk for the next half-bell and do whatever paperwork the morning gives me. The girl is at the bench. The writ-form is at the drawer. The grey at the stable is a mare, not the post's, that the matron has lent for the morning because the post's own grey is lame at the foreleg. The morning, friend, is in this small room. I have given it to you because by the bell of last night I am persuaded it is a morning the order has, in your hand, written for."

He stood. He did not look at the girl as he stood. He went back through the curtain. The girl on the bench was not crying. She was looking at you. The bell of the first hour had finished. The bell of the second had not yet rung.
