Roshanak in the Crowd

A market-day in a small town between the road and a river, two hundred shoulders pressed between three rows of stalls, and a woman at a cloth-stall straightening from her stack as your shoulder passes hers.

The market was at its second bell. The town was the small unmarked one a half-day off the imperial road where the country brought its small surpluses down on a Thursday to sell to the country, and the country was here: a farrier doing trade at the head of the long row with his small forge alight under a leather hood, a woman at a board of dried figs and a single brass weight, three brothers from the river at a flat tray of carp on a piece of wet straw, a man with a bundle of new mattock-handles, a boy with a small basket of cheap brown chestnuts, an old man whose stall was nothing but two pairs of mended sandals laid neatly on a folded cloth. You moved through it the way you had been moving through such crowds all week — at the small unhurried pace that the body had taught you was the pace of a man no warden would, on a Thursday at a country market, single out by the eye. The body had been a careful body for a long time before the morning of yesterday. The body remembered.

You came down the second row at the dye-cloth stalls. The cloth was a quick blue and a quick yellow, both turned out by the river-yard at Sadaiziche, both folded in the small bolts the country bought in for marriage-shifts and harvest-cloths. The stall you passed at the middle of the row had a stack waist-high and a small black-haired woman straightening from the bottom of the stack with three folded ells of yellow across her arm. She straightened. She turned. Her shoulder almost brushed yours in the small space between the stack and the stall opposite. She looked at you the way two strangers in a market look at each other when one of them is moving the wrong way through the row. Then she did not look the way two strangers look. Then she stood very still.

You did not stop. You kept walking. You did not, at the next breath, look back. The body knew to keep walking and the body, by some lower habit, was already counting the paces between her stall and the next gap between the rows — six, seven, eight, the gap on your left — and the body's right hand had loosened the lining at the third button by the third pace, by no decision your mind had been part of. You went into the gap on your left.

She caught up with you at the back of the cloth-stall, in the small space between the canvas of her stall and the dust-wall of the building behind it. She came up at a small dignified hurry — three folded ells of yellow still across her arm — and she did not, when she got there, put out a hand. She stood at three paces. She looked at you very directly. She was perhaps thirty. Her hair under the scarf was the black of a horse's mane and there was a small white scar along her jaw that ran into the hairline below the ear. She had been weeping recently and was not weeping now, and the not-weeping was the small particular not-weeping of a woman who had decided, in the four breaths it had taken her to get to this gap behind the stall, what she was going to do here.

"Khorshid." She said it the way a woman says a name she has not been allowed to say in a room for many years. She did not lift the voice. "I am Roshanak. You will, I think, not remember the rest of the name today; I have not been called by it in eight years either. The house at the river-bend at Sayedabad. The boy of mine you saw born one summer and saw the once and never again. I have been at this market every Thursday for four years and I have stopped looking, friend, for perhaps the last two, and I had not stopped." She let her breath go out slowly. "I will tell you, plainly, what I will do. I will walk out of this gap to the head of the row and I will say the name at the farrier's stall in a voice the row will hear. The farrier is my husband's cousin. The husband is my husband and the cousin is a man who has owed my husband three winters of work without paying for them. The cousin will hold the row at the head until the warden comes up from the river-stair. I will, by no other hand, ruin a Thursday I have come to for four years; I will, by this Thursday, ruin yours."

She did not move. The three folded ells of yellow on her arm were the only things in the gap that did not weigh anything. Somewhere over the canvas of the stall the brothers from the river were calling the price of the carp.
