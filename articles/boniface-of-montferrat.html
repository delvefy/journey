Boniface of Montferrat

c. 1150 – 4 September 1207, marquess of Montferrat, leader of the Fourth Crusade, king of Thessalonica

Boniface was born about 1150, the third son of William V, marquess of Montferrat, called William the Old, by Judith of Babenberg, sister of the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa. Montferrat in the twelfth century was the principal lay state of Piedmont, a long marquisate of vine and pasture between the Po and the Maritime Alps, and the marquesses were already, through Judith’s marriage, cousins of the Hohenstaufen and through the marriages of their daughters cousins of every important house in southern France, northern Italy, and Provence. They had also, alone among Italian dynasties, made the East a family policy. Boniface’s elder brother William Longsword had married, in 1176, the princess Sibylla of Jerusalem and had died in his bride’s arms within a year, leaving an infant son who reigned briefly as Baldwin V; his second elder brother Conrad married the empress Theodora Angelina at Constantinople in 1187, defended Tyre against Saladin later that year, and was elected king of Jerusalem in 1192 days before his assassination outside Tyre by the Hashashin; his fourth and youngest brother Renier married Maria Komnene, the eldest daughter of the emperor Manuel I, and was murdered with her in the coup of Andronikos in 1182. Boniface, the third son, alone of the four was still alive when their father William died at the great age of about ninety in 1191. By the family arithmetic of the time he was already the heir to every claim his brothers had left in Asia.

The first three decades of his own career were spent in Italy and Provence, in the wars of the marquesses for control of the western Lombard plain. He fought at first as a lieutenant of his father, then as marquess in his own right, against the communes of Asti and Alessandria; he served the emperor Henry VI in his Sicilian campaigns of the 1190s; he received, as the patron of the troubadour Raimbaut de Vaqueiras, the most polished of the late Provençal courts; Raimbaut’s long verse epistles to him are still the chief literary monument of the marquisate. The death of his cousin Theobald III of Champagne in May 1201 changed his life. The French princes assembled at Soissons to elect a new leader for the crusade just preached by Innocent III chose Boniface unanimously in his place; the marquess took the cross at Soissons on 16 September 1201 and at once travelled south to Genoa, then to Venice, then in the winter of 1201–1202 to the court of his cousin and ally Philip of Swabia, then king of Germany.

It was at Philip’s court that he met the boy Alexios Angelos, the fugitive son of the deposed Byzantine emperor, who had just arrived in the West by way of a wine cask. The proposal that the assembling crusade should restore him and his father in return for a vast subsidy and the union of the Churches was floated almost at once and was carried back by Boniface to Venice in the spring of 1202. Throughout the year that followed, while the crusader army assembled in the Venetian lagoon, the marquess held in his head the threads of a complicated diplomacy with Pope Innocent in Rome, with the Doge Enrico Dandolo in Venice, with Philip of Swabia and the boy in Germany, and with his own ambivalent French lieutenants. He sailed with the fleet in October 1202, accepted with Dandolo the agreement with Alexios at Zara in January 1203, and led the crusader army across the Bosphorus in July 1203.

He was the leader on land of both sieges of Constantinople. After the storm of the walls on 12 April 1204 he was the leading candidate for the imperial crown of the new Latin Empire; Dandolo, however, had decided that a Frenchman would be a more docile partner than an Italian and arranged the election in such a way that Baldwin of Flanders, not Boniface, was chosen. In compensation Boniface received the great prize of the second city, Thessalonica, with the lands of Macedonia and Thessaly down to the Vale of Tempe; he was crowned king of Thessalonica in August 1204. To attach himself to the dispossessed Byzantine order he married, almost at once, Margaret of Hungary, the widow of Isaac II, who as the dowager empress retained a residual loyalty among the Greek population of the city, and accepted as his stepson her child by Isaac, the prince Manuel. By her he had a son of his own, Demetrius, who was acclaimed king while still an infant at his father’s death.

The two years that followed the coronation were spent in subduing Greece. He marched south through Thessaly, took the great Byzantine fortresses of Larissa and Thebes, accepted the submission of the Athenian Akropolis from its archbishop Michael Choniates (the brother of the historian), and pushed on into the Peloponnese, where his lieutenant Geoffrey of Villehardouin the younger, nephew of the marshal of Champagne, won the principality of Achaea, and where his own vassals, the Burgundian and Lombard knights of the second wave, carved out the lordships of Athens, Negroponte, and Salona that would persist as Latin states for two centuries. Boniface himself was at the head of his column in the marches of the north-western frontier, where the war with the Bulgarian tsar Kaloyan had broken out almost immediately after the death of Baldwin in a Bulgarian prison in 1205. On 4 September 1207, in the defile of the Rhodope above Mosynopolis, the marquess’s rearguard was ambushed by a small Bulgarian raiding party. Boniface, riding in the rear with a few knights, turned to support them; he was unhorsed by a thrown lance, fought on foot until cut down, and was beheaded where he fell. The Bulgarians sent his head to Kaloyan, who set it on a pole over his tent at Tirnovo. He was fifty-seven.

The Kingdom of Thessalonica, his foundation, did not long survive his death. His infant son Demetrius reigned until 1224 under his mother’s regency; in that year the city was recovered by the Greek Despot of Epirus and the Latin kingdom in Macedonia came to an end. The Lombard and Burgundian states he had planted further south, however, persisted: the Duchy of Athens, the Principality of Achaea, the Triarchy of Negroponte, the Marquisate of Bodonitsa. Through his step-grandson and namesake Boniface of Verona, and through his great-granddaughter Yolanda of Montferrat who became empress of the Romans in 1284 as the second wife of Andronikos II Palaiologos, his line ran on in the politics of the Levant for two centuries after his death.
