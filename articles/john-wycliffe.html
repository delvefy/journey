John Wycliffe

c. 1330 – 1384, the Morning Star of the Reformation, Oxford theologian, translator of the Bible into English

John Wycliffe was born in the early 1330s, almost certainly in the Yorkshire village of Wycliffe near Richmond, of a family of small gentry. He matriculated at Oxford about 1346, took his degree in arts in the early 1350s, and pursued the long route through the higher degrees that culminated in a doctorate in theology in 1372. He was Master of Balliol College in 1360 and held a series of livings in the church — Fillingham in Lincolnshire, Ludgershall in Buckinghamshire, Lutterworth in Leicestershire — that supplied him with the income of a benefice in absentia while he continued to teach at Oxford. He was, by every contemporary report, the foremost philosopher of the Oxford schools in the 1360s and 1370s, on the older realist side against the prevailing nominalist current.

His political prominence dates from 1374, when he was sent to Bruges with a royal commission, headed by John, Bishop of Bangor, to negotiate with envoys of Pope Gregory XI on a series of long-running disputes between the English crown and the papal court over the taxation of the English clergy and the system of papal provisions. He impressed John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, who from that point became his political protector. The protection mattered because the philosophical positions Wycliffe was already developing — that lordship over property is a function of grace, that an unrighteous priest holds no real authority, that the church ought to be disendowed of its temporal wealth — coincided with the secular programme of Gaunt's faction at court. The cooperation was, on each side, sincere within its limits; the noble lord saw a theologian whose conclusions matched his political interests, and the theologian saw a great lord whose patronage would shield him.

The shield was tested in February 1377 when William Courtenay, Bishop of London, summoned Wycliffe to St Paul's on charges of heretical preaching. Gaunt and his ally Henry Percy, Lord Marshal, attended in person; the meeting collapsed in a quarrel between the duke and the bishop over whether Wycliffe should be permitted to sit, and the citizens of London rose against Gaunt the same evening, broke up the proceedings, and burned his banner. Five papal bulls against Wycliffe were issued by Gregory XI from Avignon in May 1377; they were tabled at Oxford in December and reached no decision before Gregory's death in March 1378 and the outbreak of the Great Schism between the Roman pope Urban VI and the Avignon pope Clement VII the same summer effectively distracted the curia from further action.

The Schism freed Wycliffe to radicalise. From 1378 onward he moved beyond the disendowment programme to a frontal critique of the doctrine of transubstantiation, the central sacramental teaching of the medieval church. His tract De Eucharistia of 1379 to 1380 argued that the substance of the bread remained after consecration, alongside the real presence of Christ; he called the orthodox doctrine "philosophically incoherent" and "introduced into the church by the malice of friars". The position broke his patronage. Gaunt, who had been content to support a disendowment theorist, would not openly back a eucharistic heretic; the duke is recorded as personally visiting Wycliffe at Oxford in 1381 to ask him to keep silent on the issue. Wycliffe would not.

The Peasants' Revolt of June 1381, in which one of the rebel demands had been the disendowment of the church, was used against him by his enemies, though his teaching had no direct part in the rising; the rebels' chaplain John Ball preached his own gospel and was hanged for it. The Blackfriars Council convened by Archbishop William Courtenay at the Dominican house in London in May 1382 condemned twenty-four propositions extracted from Wycliffe's writings, fourteen as heretical and ten as erroneous. The condemnation was confirmed by parliament in November. Wycliffe was expelled from Oxford the following year, along with the principal of his Oxford followers, Nicholas Hereford, Philip Repingdon, and John Aston.

He retreated to his Leicestershire living at Lutterworth and continued to write at unparalleled speed for the last two years of his life. The vernacular translation of the Latin Vulgate Bible into English, which would carry his name, was the work of a school rather than of his hand alone: the so-called Earlier Version, completed about 1382, is closely associated with Hereford and the Oxford group; the Later Version, in smoother English, was finished by John Purvey at Lutterworth in the late 1380s. Wycliffe's exact contribution is no longer recoverable, but the project would not have happened without him. The Lollard Bibles, copied by hand in increasing numbers through the next century, were the first complete translation of the scriptures into English; about two hundred and fifty manuscript copies survive.

He suffered a stroke at mass in the parish church at Lutterworth on 28 December 1384 and died on the last day of the year. He was buried in the churchyard there. His followers, the Lollards — the name was originally an insult, perhaps from a Dutch word meaning "mumbler" — survived him as a movement of preaching, vernacular Bible-reading, and clerical disendowment that ran underground through the fifteenth century. The episcopal counter-attack under Archbishop Thomas Arundel, the Constitutions of Oxford of 1408, and the statute De heretico comburendo of 1401 broke its noble wing, particularly after the rising of Sir John Oldcastle in 1414, but Lollardy persisted in the towns and survived to merge into the English Reformation a century later.

His doctrines passed by Czech students returning from Oxford to Prague, where Jan Hus took them up in the first decade of the fifteenth century; the Hussite movement that followed was the first large-scale European application of Wycliffite ideas. The Council of Constance in 1415, which burned Hus, condemned Wycliffe by name in two hundred and sixty articles; in 1428 the same council ordered his bones to be exhumed from Lutterworth churchyard, burned, and the ashes scattered in the river Swift, an instruction carried out the same year by the Bishop of Lincoln. The seventeenth-century historian Thomas Fuller observed that the river Swift carried his ashes into the Avon, the Avon into the Severn, the Severn into the sea, and from the sea his doctrine to every part of the world.

The phrase "Morning Star of the Reformation", coined by John Foxe in the 1560s, captures Wycliffe's place in English Protestant memory; the historical Wycliffe was a fourteenth-century scholastic theologian whose programme was at once more conservative (he never doubted the unity of Christendom) and more radical (he denied transubstantiation) than the comparison suggests. His effect on the religious geography of the next two centuries was, in any case, very large: every vernacular Bible printed in English from Tyndale onward worked in the shadow of the Wycliffite version, and every English-speaking critic of clerical wealth from the Reformation to the Civil War operated within a tradition he had largely invented.

The road continues to Jan Hus.
