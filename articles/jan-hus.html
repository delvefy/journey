Jan Hus

c. 1370 – 1415, Bohemian reformer, rector of Prague University, burnt at the Council of Constance

Jan Hus was born about 1370 in the village of Husinec in southern Bohemia, of peasant or small-trader parents. He matriculated at the University of Prague in 1390, took the bachelor's degree in arts in 1393, the master's in 1396, and a year later began teaching at the philosophical faculty. He was ordained priest in 1400 and was elected dean of the philosophical faculty in 1401. From 1402 he held the chaplaincy of the Bethlehem Chapel in the Old Town of Prague, a chapel founded ten years earlier with the explicit purpose of providing vernacular Czech preaching for the city; it held three thousand. Hus preached there for the next twelve years.

The intellectual climate at Prague was already shaped by the long circulation of Wycliffite manuscripts through the city. The marriage of Richard II of England to Anne of Bohemia, sister of King Wenceslaus IV, in 1382 had opened a small but steady traffic of Czech students to Oxford; the most important of them, Jerome of Prague, returned with Wycliffe's philosophical writings about 1402. Hus, who had already absorbed Wycliffe's metaphysics in the realist mode current at Prague, took up the moral and ecclesiological writings as well: De Ecclesia, De Potestate Papae, the disendowment tracts, the polemics against the friars. He translated some of them into Czech. He did not, however, follow Wycliffe in denying transubstantiation, and was at pains to make the distinction clear in his own preaching.

The split with the Prague establishment came over the issue of national representation in the university and over the related politics of the Western Schism. The Council of Pisa convened in 1409 to end the Schism by deposing both rival popes; Wenceslaus IV, persuaded by Hus and his ally Jerome of Prague, agreed to recognise the Pisan pope Alexander V, against the wishes of the conservative archbishop of Prague Zbyněk Zajíc and of most of the German masters of the university. Wenceslaus issued the Decree of Kutná Hora in January 1409, which reversed the voting balance at the university from three German nations to one in favour of the Bohemian; the German masters — about a thousand of them — left Prague in protest, mostly for the new University of Leipzig founded for them by the Wettin princes. Hus, in the empty university, was elected rector in October 1409.

Archbishop Zbyněk in 1410 procured a bull from Alexander V condemning Wycliffite books and the unauthorised preaching of Bethlehem Chapel; he had Wycliffe's books publicly burnt in his palace courtyard. Hus refused to be silenced; he preached against the burning the same week and was excommunicated. The new pope, John XXIII — the Pisan successor, later deposed as a schismatic by Constance — declared a crusade against Ladislaus of Naples in 1411 and authorised the sale of indulgences in Bohemia to finance it. Hus's sermons against the indulgence sale in the summer of 1412 broke his last support at court: King Wenceslaus, whose treasury benefited from the sale, withdrew his protection, and three of Hus's followers were beheaded in Prague the same week for protesting the indulgences. Hus was placed under aggravated excommunication and the city of Prague under interdict for as long as he remained within its walls.

He went into the countryside, sheltered first at Kozí Hrádek in southern Bohemia by friendly nobles, then at Krakovec in the west. During the two years of his exile he wrote his major theological works, including De Ecclesia, the long treatise on the church that was the formal occasion of his condemnation at Constance, and a large body of vernacular Czech sermons and devotional writings. He also conducted the reform of Czech orthography — the introduction of diacritical marks to replace the digraph spelling of Czech — that bore his name (the háček, "little hook") and remains the basis of modern Czech writing.

In November 1414 he travelled to the Council of Constance, summoned by the Emperor-elect Sigismund of Luxembourg to end the now triple Schism, to reform the church in capite et in membris, and to settle the Hussite question. Sigismund had given him a safe-conduct, the language of which was disputed even at the time: it guaranteed his journey but, as Sigismund and the council ultimately interpreted it, did not extend to the council's right to try and condemn a heretic. Hus arrived at Constance on 3 November, was at first lodged in private, was arrested on 28 November and lodged successively in the Dominican monastery on the lake, the bishop's palace at Gottlieben, and the Franciscan convent in Constance itself. His trial, conducted over a series of hearings in June 1415, condemned him on thirty articles drawn from his writings, principally from De Ecclesia, and offered him recantation. He refused unless he was shown the error of his teachings from scripture — a condition the council declined to entertain.

He was burnt at the stake outside the walls of Constance on 6 July 1415, in the meadow that is still called Hussenstein. His ashes were collected and thrown into the Rhine to prevent any cult forming around his remains. His friend Jerome of Prague, who had followed him to Constance, was burnt on the same spot on 30 May 1416. The witness account of Peter of Mladoňovice, who was present at the trial and at the execution, is the most detailed surviving record of any heresy proceeding of the late Middle Ages, and remains the central source for the events.

The news of his death produced an uprising in Bohemia that the council, the emperor, and the orthodox church together failed to suppress for two decades. The Hussite Wars of 1419 to 1434 — in which the Bohemian armies under Jan Žižka and Prokop the Bald defeated five papal crusades from a fortified war-wagon-based tactical system that was the first significant European battlefield use of small firearms — ended in the Compactata of Basel, a concession by the moderate church of communion in both kinds (bread and wine, the cup for the laity that had been one of Hus's central demands) to the moderate Hussite party. The Bohemian church remained Utraquist for the next two centuries.

Hus's longer afterlife was Reformation. Luther, in his disputation at Leipzig with Johann Eck in 1519, was charged with being a Hussite and replied that "we are all Hussites without knowing it"; from that moment Hus was assimilated into the genealogy of Protestant martyrs and became, with Wycliffe, one of the two pre-Reformation prophets of the Reformation tradition. The Czech national revival of the nineteenth century took him up as the model of the Bohemian against German and Roman authority alike; the day of his execution, 6 July, has been a national holiday in the Czech Republic since 1925.

The road continues to Sigismund, Holy Roman Emperor.
