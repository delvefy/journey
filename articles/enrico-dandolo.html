Enrico Dandolo

c. 1107 – June 1205, forty-first Doge of Venice 1192–1205

Enrico was born about 1107 into one of the small group of old patrician families that had governed Venice since the lagoon settlements coalesced into a city. His father Vitale Dandolo was a long-lived merchant prince who served on every important embassy of the middle of the twelfth century; his uncle Enrico Dandolo the elder was patriarch of Grado from 1134, and the family quarter at San Luca, in the elbow of the Grand Canal, was already a power within the parishes of the city when the future Doge was a young man. He grew up in the commercial system of Venice as it stood at its great mid-twelfth-century pivot, when the eastern trade had become the single dominant fact of the republic’s economy and when the privileged colony in Constantinople, secured by the chrysobull of Alexios I a century before, was the most valuable commercial holding in Christendom. The first three decades of his career, of which little detail survives, were spent in commerce, in legal office at home, and on the embassies that his father’s name made him fit for.

The decisive event of his middle life was the crisis of 1171. In March of that year the emperor Manuel I Komnenos, suspecting Venetian complicity in a raid on the Genoese quarter, ordered the simultaneous arrest of every Venetian in the empire and the seizure of all their property; ten thousand merchants were thrown into prison from Constantinople to Adrianople and their goods inventoried for the treasury. The Doge Vitale II Michiel sent a great punitive fleet to the Aegean in the same year; it was destroyed by plague before it could engage the Byzantines, and the Doge on his return was lynched in a Venetian street. Enrico Dandolo accompanied the diplomatic missions that followed; he was in Constantinople in 1172 as a member of an embassy sent to recover the property and prisoners. The chronicler Niketas Choniates and the much later Venetian tradition both say that on this embassy he was blinded by a Byzantine action of some kind. Modern historians regard the lurid story of a deliberate burning of the eyes with a glass mirror as embellishment; what is certain is that by the time he became Doge twenty years later his vision was severely impaired, that contemporary witnesses describe him as effectively blind, and that he never quite trusted Byzantium afterwards.

He was elected Doge on 1 June 1192, by which time he was a man of about eighty-five, sightless or nearly so, and politically the head of the most experienced faction in the city. He spent the next decade rebuilding the eastern trade. In 1198 he negotiated with Alexios III Angelos a new chrysobull restoring and enlarging the Venetian privileges in the empire; in the same years he concluded fresh treaties with the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia, with the Sultan of Aleppo, and with the kings of Sicily, and reorganised the Venetian coinage by introducing the silver grosso, the first major European silver coin minted in more than a century. The Venetian state, in his hands, ceased to be a republic of merchants negotiating from city to city and became a coherent Mediterranean power.

The Fourth Crusade was the masterstroke and the scandal of his reign. In April 1201 envoys of the French princes, led by Geoffrey of Villehardouin, the marshal of Champagne, came to Venice to negotiate the transport of an army to the Holy Land. Dandolo presented to the assembled people in San Marco an offer of unprecedented generosity: Venice would build, in eighteen months, a fleet capable of carrying thirty-three thousand men and four thousand five hundred horses, would feed them for nine months, and would join the expedition in person at the head of fifty armed galleys. The price was eighty-five thousand silver marks, payable in advance. The contract was sealed in the basilica; for the year that followed the entire shipbuilding capacity of the lagoon was reserved for it. When the crusaders assembled at Venice in the summer of 1202 they were only one-third the number for which the fleet had been built and could pay only one-third of the contracted sum. Dandolo refused to release the ships until the balance was made up. In its place he proposed that the army first help Venice recover the Adriatic port of Zara, which had revolted to the king of Hungary; the agreement was sealed at the end of August 1202.

The army sailed from Venice on 8 October 1202 with the old Doge in person on the flagship of the lead galley. Zara was stormed in November; the Pope, Innocent III, excommunicated the crusaders for an attack on a Christian city; Dandolo answered that Venetian Christians did not consider themselves bound by an excommunication procured by a Pope who knew nothing of the contract he had countersigned. The fleet wintered at Zara. There, in January 1203, the envoys of the boy Alexios Angelos arrived with the offer that turned the expedition east. Dandolo and the marquess Boniface of Montferrat, the elected military leader of the crusade, accepted the offer over the protests of a minority of the French knights; the fleet sailed for Constantinople in April. The first siege of the city, in July 1203, ended with the flight of Alexios III and the restoration of Isaac II and his son Alexios IV; the second siege, in April 1204, ended with the storm of the walls and the sack of the city.

The personal role of the Doge in the assault was famous. At the first attack on the harbour walls in July 1203, when the Venetian transports hesitated to come in close, Dandolo, by then about ninety-seven, had himself rowed to the bow of his galley in full armour with the banner of Saint Mark beside him and ordered the rowers to ground the ship under the wall; the Venetian galleys followed and carried the harbour quarter. Geoffrey of Villehardouin, who saw it, wrote that he could think of nothing like it. After the sack the Doge presided over the partition of the empire. He refused the imperial title for himself, on the ground that a Doge of Venice could not also be emperor, and arranged that the crown go instead to Baldwin of Flanders; for Venice he took three-eighths of the city and of the empire, the great commercial colonies, the long arc of harbours from Modon to Crete to the Black Sea, and for the patriarch of Venice the see of Hagia Sophia. The diplomatic and commercial geography of the eastern Mediterranean for the next three centuries was settled by his pen.

He died at Constantinople in June 1205, almost ninety-eight, and was buried in the upper south gallery of Hagia Sophia, the only Latin to receive such a tomb. The slab, with his name carved in plain capitals, remained in place until the Ottoman conquest of 1453 emptied the grave; the inscription is still visible in the floor of the gallery today. He had given Venice an empire and left it the most powerful commercial state in Europe.
