Francis of Assisi

c. 1181 – 1226, founder of the Order of Friars Minor, apostle of evangelical poverty, the Poverello

Francis was born about 1181 at Assisi in the duchy of Spoleto, the son of Pietro di Bernardone, a prosperous merchant of cloth trading on the route between the Champagne fairs and the towns of central Italy, and of Pica, of whom little is known beyond her name and a tradition that she was of Provençal origin. He was baptised Giovanni; his father, returning from a buying trip to France, took to calling him Francesco — "the Frenchman" — and that name displaced the other. The Assisi of his youth was a small commune of perhaps three thousand inhabitants, set on the lower slope of Mount Subasio, riven like every Umbrian town between the lesser nobility of the upper city and the rising merchant class to which his own family belonged. He was educated for the cloth trade, learned a serviceable French in his father's accounts, and through his teens spent freely among the young men of the town — expensive clothes, public entertainments, the prospect of buying a knighthood that his father's purse could pay for and his birth could not provide.

In 1202 the commune of Assisi went to war with Perugia, its larger neighbour. The Assisans were beaten at the bridge of Collestrada in November of that year and Francis, then about twenty, was among the prisoners. He spent something over a year in a Perugian gaol, came home in 1203 to a long illness, and emerged from it a changed man. The chroniclers describe a slow conversion across the next two or three years: a confused attempt to ride south to join the army of the papal mercenary Walter of Brienne, broken off near Spoleto by a vision; an encounter with a leper on the plain below Assisi which he forced himself to embrace; a voice heard in the abandoned country chapel of San Damiano below the walls, while he prayed before a painted crucifix, telling him to "rebuild my church, which as you see is falling into ruin". He took bolts of his father's cloth, sold them with the horse he had ridden to market, and offered the proceeds to the priest of San Damiano. His father, infuriated at this disposition of his goods, hauled him before the bishop of Assisi to demand restitution. Francis, in the bishop's courtyard, stripped off the clothes he was wearing and handed them back to his father with the words, "From now on I can say with full freedom, 'Our Father, who art in heaven', and not 'my father, Pietro di Bernardone'". The bishop covered him with a peasant's cloak, and Francis went off to live as a beggar in the country around Assisi, repairing the small chapels of San Damiano, San Pietro, and the Portiuncula of Saint Mary of the Angels with stones he carried himself.

In February 1208, at the Portiuncula, he heard read at mass the Gospel passage in which Christ sends out the twelve apostles with neither purse nor sandals nor staff, to preach the Kingdom and to heal. He resolved to live by it literally, exchanged his hermit's tunic for a rough wool habit knotted at the waist with a rope, and began to preach in the streets and the market squares of the Umbrian towns. He drew followers almost at once: Bernard of Quintavalle, a wealthy townsman who sold all he had; Peter Catanii, a notary; and a series of young men of mixed station who lived with him at the Portiuncula and on the missionary circuits he sent them on, two by two, into the surrounding country. When the community was twelve in number he wrote a brief rule, little more than a stringing-together of Gospel passages, and in 1209 or 1210 walked to Rome with his companions to ask Pope Innocent III to confirm it. After hesitation — the curia was wary of new orders, especially of lay brothers who claimed apostolic poverty in a manner uncomfortably close to the condemned Waldensians — Innocent gave an oral approval and tonsured the brothers, making them clerics in a minimal sense. The order, now called the Friars Minor, was launched.

Its growth was startling. In 1212 the eighteen-year-old Clare di Favarone of Assisi ran away from her noble family on Palm Sunday night, met Francis and a few brothers at the Portiuncula, and had her hair cut off as the sign of consecration; from her followed the female second order, the Poor Ladies, who lived at San Damiano under the rule of strict enclosure and absolute poverty. A lay third order, for married men and women who could not leave their households, took shape over the next decade. By 1217 the order held its first general chapter and Francis was dividing the brothers into provinces: he sent them to France, to Spain, to Germany, to Hungary, and across the sea to Syria and to Morocco. Five missionaries despatched to Morocco in 1220 were the first Franciscan martyrs.

In 1219, in the fifth year of the Fifth Crusade, Francis took ship for Egypt to join the Christian army then besieging the port of Damietta on the mouth of the Nile. The siege had gone poorly; the crusader leaders refused his request to be allowed to cross the lines and preach to the sultan, until at last he and Brother Illuminato slipped over to the Saracen camp on their own. They were brought before al-Malik al-Kamil, the Ayyubid sultan of Egypt and nephew of Saladin. The records on each side differ — the Christian sources speak of a debate from which Francis returned unharmed and the sultan thoughtful; an Arabic chronicler of the next generation reports the visit of "a poor monk" who spoke with the sultan and was given safe-conduct — but the encounter, the first documented direct meeting between a Christian preacher of the new movement and a Muslim ruler, became one of the founding episodes of Franciscan tradition.

The order he had founded was outgrowing the rule he had written for twelve men. In 1220 Francis resigned the governance to Peter Catanii, and at his death the next year to Brother Elias of Cortona, his most capable administrator. A revised rule of 1221, the so-called Regula non bullata, was thought too austere by many of the now-thousands of brothers; a shorter and tighter version, the Regula bullata, was approved by Pope Honorius III in November 1223 and remains the rule of the Friars Minor. At Christmas of that year, at the hilltop hermitage of Greccio above the Rieti valley, Francis arranged a living re-enactment of the Nativity with an ox and an ass and a manger filled with hay, in what the Franciscan tradition takes as the origin of the Christmas crèche.

In September 1224, in a retreat on Mount La Verna in the Apennines above the Tiber, Francis received what his immediate companions described as the marks of the wounds of Christ on his hands, his feet, and his right side. The phenomenon — the first attested case of stigmata in the Christian tradition — was concealed by him during his life and was attested after his death by Brother Leo, his confessor, and by Brother Rufino. His health had been declining for years; he was nearly blind from a chronic eye disease contracted in Egypt, his digestion was ruined, and he was in pain from the marks. In the summer of 1226, knowing he was dying, he asked to be carried back to the Portiuncula. There, on the evening of 3 October, lying on the bare earth as he had asked, he died, singing the last verses of the canticle he had composed two years earlier in praise of Brother Sun, Sister Moon, Brother Fire, Sister Water, and Sister Bodily Death.

He was canonised by Pope Gregory IX — his old friend Cardinal Hugolino dei Conti di Segni — at Assisi on 16 July 1228, less than two years after his death, the swiftest canonisation of the Middle Ages. The great basilica on the western flank of the town was begun the next day. Within a century the order he had founded numbered tens of thousands of brothers across Christendom and beyond; within seven centuries it had divided and reformed many times and produced popes, theologians, poets, and a long line of saints, of whom only Clare of Assisi, who outlived him by twenty-seven years at San Damiano, was a witness to the beginning.
