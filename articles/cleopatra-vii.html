Cleopatra VII

69 – 30 BC · last pharaoh of Egypt, the last of the Ptolemies

Cleopatra VII Philopator, the seventh of her name in the Macedonian Greek dynasty that had ruled Egypt for three centuries since the conquests of Alexander, was born in 69 BC at Alexandria and died there by her own hand in August 30 BC, a few weeks after the Roman armies of Octavian had taken her city and her treasure. She was, in her three closely linked decades, the last pharaoh of an independent Egypt, the consort in turn of Julius Caesar and Mark Antony, the mother of the only acknowledged son of Caesar and of three children by Antony, and one of the very few rulers of Egypt who had bothered to learn the Egyptian language.

The Ptolemaic court she inherited was a dying institution. Her ancestors had ruled from Alexandria since Ptolemy I, one of Alexander's generals, had taken the country and the embalmed body of Alexander himself as his share of the dismembered empire. Three centuries of palace warfare had thinned the dynasty to a knot of murderous siblings. Cleopatra's father, Ptolemy XII Auletes, the Flute-Player, had bought his throne and his Roman protection by ruinous bribes and had been driven from the country and restored; on his death in 51 BC, his will left the kingdom jointly to his eighteen-year-old daughter Cleopatra and her ten-year-old brother Ptolemy XIII, who were to marry in Ptolemaic fashion and to share the throne.

They quarrelled within a year. Cleopatra was driven out of Alexandria by her brother's court of eunuchs and tutors and was raising an army on the eastern frontier when Pompey the Great, on the run from Julius Caesar after his defeat at Pharsalus, landed at the mouth of the Nile and was murdered as he came ashore by the courtiers of the boy-king, who believed they were doing Caesar a favor. Caesar himself arrived within days, was received in Alexandria with the head and signet ring of his old rival, and took up residence in the royal palace to settle the succession. Cleopatra, blockaded outside the city, contrived the most famous arrival in ancient history: she was carried into the palace rolled inside a length of bedding by a single Sicilian merchant and presented herself to Caesar in his own quarters. She was twenty-one. Within months she was his lover, queen of Egypt under his protection, and pregnant with the son she would name Caesarion.

Caesar fought a difficult war that winter in the streets of Alexandria, in which the great Library is said to have suffered fire, and emerged in the spring with Cleopatra's brother drowned in the Nile and her younger sister Arsinoe arrested and shipped off to Rome as a prisoner. Cleopatra ruled with another, younger Ptolemy as nominal co-regent and bore Caesar's son in June of 47 BC. She came to Rome on Caesar's invitation in 46 and lived in a villa across the Tiber until his assassination two years later, then returned to Egypt and within months had her remaining brother quietly killed, securing the throne for herself and her child.

The second great relationship of her life began in 41 BC, when Mark Antony, master of the East after the defeat of Caesar's assassins, summoned her to Tarsus to account for support reportedly given to Cassius. She came up the river Cydnus on a barge with gilded stern, purple sails, and silver oars, dressed as Aphrodite, and the meeting fixed both their futures. They wintered together in Alexandria, and the next decade alternated political summits with the births of three children: the twins Alexander Helios and Cleopatra Selene, and the younger Ptolemy Philadelphus. Antony in 34 BC, at the so-called Donations of Alexandria, ceremonially distributed portions of the eastern Roman possessions among the children — Armenia and Parthia to Alexander, Cyrene to Selene, Phoenicia and Syria to the youngest — and proclaimed Caesarion the rightful heir of Julius Caesar. The act was, in Roman terms, his ruin.

Octavian, Caesar's grand-nephew and adopted heir at Rome, made of the Donations the casus belli he had been seeking. He read out in the Senate what he claimed to be Antony's will, in which Antony provided for burial alongside Cleopatra in Egypt; he secured a declaration of war not against Antony but against Cleopatra, on the grounds that Antony was a Roman possessed by a foreign queen. The decisive battle was fought at sea, off Actium on the western coast of Greece, on the second of September 31 BC. Cleopatra's squadron broke from the engagement under sail, and Antony followed her; the rest of his fleet surrendered. They reached Egypt with a thinned army and waited eleven months for the inevitable invasion.

Octavian arrived at Pelusium in the summer of 30 BC and marched on Alexandria. Antony's last troops deserted to the advancing Romans, and Antony, deceived by a false report of Cleopatra's death, fell on his sword and died in her arms in the mausoleum she had begun preparing for them. Cleopatra, captured alive, was held in honorable house arrest while Octavian, in correspondence and audience, sounded her out and concealed his intention to lead her in his triumph at Rome. She understood. On a day in August, dressed in royal robes, she was found dead on her couch in her mausoleum with her two maids; the tradition that the death came from the bite of an asp smuggled in with a basket of figs is the report of Plutarch, who also mentions the possibility of a self-administered poison. She was thirty-nine.

Octavian honored her wish to be buried beside Antony, took the kingdom of Egypt as his personal possession — it remained, until late antiquity, the private estate of the Roman emperor rather than an ordinary province — and put to death Cleopatra's son Caesarion, partly out of strategic necessity and partly to remove a possible rival heir to the name of Julius Caesar. The other three children, Antony's, were taken to Rome and raised by Antony's Roman widow Octavia, sister of Octavian, an act of complicated, careful magnanimity that allowed the surviving princess Cleopatra Selene to grow up in the imperial household and be married, in time, to a North African client king named Juba II.

The historical Cleopatra was a polyglot, an administrator who minted her own coinage and intervened personally in the appeals of priests and tax collectors, the only Ptolemaic ruler reported to have spoken Egyptian, and a politician who held the throne of Egypt through twenty-one years of Roman civil war by reading correctly which Roman to attach herself to and how. The legendary Cleopatra of the Roman poets, of Shakespeare, and of the European theater — the Eastern queen of inexhaustible artifice, the seductress who lost a world for love — is the invention of the man who defeated her and required, for the legitimacy of his own one-man rule at Rome, that the woman he had broken be remembered as a danger. The Macedonian queen who took her own life rather than be led in chains through the Roman Forum was, by both records, a sovereign to the end.
