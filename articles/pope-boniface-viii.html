Pope Boniface VIII

c. 1230 – 1303, Benedetto Caetani, the pope of Unam Sanctam, broken by the Outrage of Anagni

Benedetto Caetani was born about 1230 at Anagni in the Lazio, of a minor noble family of the small Roman aristocracy with extensive landholdings in the Roman Campagna. His uncle Pietro Caetani was bishop of Todi; the family channel into the church, the conventional pattern for the second son of a minor lord, brought him to canon law studies first at Spoleto and Todi, then at Bologna where he studied under the famed glossator Sinibaldo dei Fieschi who would later be Pope Innocent IV. He had been ordained subdeacon by 1252 and was canon of Anagni by 1255. He served as canon lawyer and curial bureaucrat through the long decades of Innocent IV and the Italian and French popes who succeeded him; he was made cardinal-deacon of San Niccolò in Carcere Tulliano by Pope Martin IV in 1281 and cardinal-priest of Sts Sylvester and Martin in 1291 by his predecessor Nicholas IV.

His political and diplomatic career through the 1280s and early 1290s was the work of an able canon lawyer at the centre of the papal court: he served on legations to France in 1290, to England in 1291, and to the kingdom of Sicily in 1294, and was widely regarded as the most experienced curial diplomat of his generation. The conclave that elected him pope in December 1294 was the longest in many years — over two years from the death of Nicholas IV in April 1292 to the eventual election of the hermit Pietro del Morrone in July 1294. Pietro, who took the name Celestine V, was an elderly hermit of unusual personal sanctity but no administrative experience; his five-month pontificate ended on 13 December 1294 when, on the advice (and, by his enemies' later claim, the elaborate manipulation) of Cardinal Caetani, he resigned the papacy — the only confirmed abdication of a pope before Benedict XVI in 2013. Eleven days later Caetani was elected to succeed him as Boniface VIII.

The unprecedented circumstances of his election shadowed his pontificate. Celestine V, who had been allowed to return to the hermitage, was forcibly retired by Boniface to the fortress at Fumone in the Roman Campagna, where he died in 1296 under conditions widely supposed at the time to amount to imprisonment. The Spirituals among the Franciscans, who had taken Celestine for their man, never forgave Boniface; the Colonna cardinals, an old and rival family of the Roman aristocracy, took up the question of Boniface's legitimacy as a means of pursuing their own quarrel with the Caetani family; Dante put both Celestine and Boniface in his Inferno in adjacent and savage passages.

The first crisis was with the Colonna. The two Colonna cardinals, James and Peter, in May 1297 issued a manifesto challenging the legitimacy of Boniface's election on the ground that no living pope could resign and that his election was therefore invalid. Boniface excommunicated them, stripped them of their cardinalates, and in 1298 launched a crusade against the Colonna lands in the Roman Campagna. The family fortresses at Zagarolo, Palestrina, and Colonna were systematically reduced; Palestrina was demolished stone by stone on Boniface's order, ploughed with salt, and the inhabitants dispersed. The Colonna survived, in exile in France, and would supply most of the personnel of the eventual counter-stroke at Anagni.

The second crisis was with Philip IV of France. The dispute was over the taxation of the French clergy. Boniface's bull Clericis Laicos of February 1296 prohibited the taxation of the clergy by lay authority without papal consent; Philip IV responded by banning the export of gold and silver from France, which cut off the papal revenues from the French church. Boniface backed down with the bull Etsi de Statu in 1297. The settlement held for five years.

The year 1300 was Boniface's great public triumph. He proclaimed the first papal Jubilee, the holy year that gave plenary indulgences to pilgrims who visited the basilicas of Rome; perhaps two hundred thousand pilgrims came over the year, the largest gathering of any kind in medieval Europe, and the papal treasury was filled by their offerings. Dante was among them, in March or April 1300; the imagined date of the visionary journey of the Commedia is Holy Week of the Jubilee.

The renewed quarrel with Philip IV opened in 1301 when Bernard Saisset, Bishop of Pamiers, was arrested by Philip's officers on charges of treason and tried before a royal court — a clear breach of ecclesiastical immunity. Boniface responded with the bull Ausculta Fili of December 1301, recalling Philip to a proper deference to ecclesiastical authority. Philip burned a forged version of the bull in public — the so-called Deum Time — and convened the first French Estates General in April 1302 to rally the kingdom against the pope. Boniface in turn promulgated, on 18 November 1302, the bull Unam Sanctam, the strongest single assertion of papal supremacy in medieval canon law: it declared that there was no salvation outside the Roman Church, that the two swords (spiritual and temporal) belonged equally to the church, and that "every human creature is subject to the Roman Pontiff" was a doctrinal necessity.

Philip IV's chief minister, Guillaume de Nogaret, was sent to Italy in the summer of 1303 with a small force and a pile of French gold. With the help of Sciarra Colonna, the most embittered of the exiled Colonna, he proposed to seize the pope at Anagni and bring him before a general council for trial on charges of simony, sodomy, and heresy. On 7 September 1303 Nogaret and Sciarra entered Anagni with about three hundred soldiers, found the pope still in his palace with a small retinue, and held him for three days. The townspeople rose in his support on 9 September and freed him; the French force withdrew. But Boniface, seventy-three years old and physically broken by the captivity, returned to Rome on 13 September, fell into a delirium of rage and despair, and died on 11 October 1303. The episode — the so-called Outrage of Anagni — was the symbolic end of the high medieval papacy.

His successor Benedict XI lived less than a year. The next conclave, of 1304 to 1305, elected the Frenchman Bertrand de Got as Clement V, who, under Philip IV's pressure, moved the papal court to Avignon in 1309. The seventy years of "Babylonian Captivity" of the papacy at Avignon followed; the Schism of 1378 followed that. Boniface VIII had occupied the apex of the medieval claim to papal universal sovereignty; his successors, broken by his fall, abandoned the universal claim in practice while retaining it in formula.

The historical reputation of Boniface VIII has been bitterly hostile from his own lifetime to the present. Dante's Commedia alone preserved him in the European imagination for centuries as the archetype of the worldly pope, of the ambitious churchman, of the simoniac. The bull Unam Sanctam, the high-water mark of medieval papal theory, was never withdrawn; it was bypassed in practice, by Boniface's immediate successors and by all who came after them, in deference to the secular states that had broken him. The Caetani family, which he had built up by his papal authority, remained one of the principal noble houses of the Roman aristocracy for the next six hundred years.
