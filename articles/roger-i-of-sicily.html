Roger I of Sicily

c. 1031 – 1101, Great Count of Sicily, conqueror of the island from the Arab emirates

Roger was born about 1031 in Normandy, the youngest of the twelve sons of the petty Norman knight Tancred of Hauteville, lord of a modest fief near Coutances. The Hauteville inheritance was nowhere near sufficient for so large a brood, and the older brothers had already gone south, in the wave of Norman adventurers who in the 1030s and 1040s were taking service with the Lombard princes of southern Italy and the Byzantine catepan of Bari, and who by mid-century had begun to seize lordships for themselves. By 1057 Roger followed them. His eldest surviving full brothers, William Iron-Arm, Drogo, and Humphrey, had already held in succession the title of count of Apulia under the half-Norman, half-Lombard republic of Melfi; by 1057 the dominant figure in the family was his half-brother Robert, called Guiscard or “the cunning,” whom Humphrey on his deathbed had named as guardian of his sons and who at the assembly of Melfi later that year was elected count of Apulia in their stead.

The relationship between Roger and Robert defined the next thirty years. Robert was about fifteen years the elder; he was the political head of the family and, from August 1059 when Pope Nicholas II invested him at the synod of Melfi, duke of Apulia, Calabria, and “future Sicily.” Roger, dispatched to Calabria, made his first independent campaigns there in 1058–1060 in conditions of extreme privation; the chronicler Geoffrey Malaterra, writing in Roger’s entourage a generation later, records the count and his sixty followers reduced to one cloak shared between them. The two brothers quarrelled bitterly over the division of the Calabrian conquest, were reconciled, quarrelled again, and finally in 1061 began together the expedition that the Melfi investiture had promised — the crossing of the Strait of Messina into Muslim Sicily.

The island was at that moment divided. The Kalbid emirate that had ruled Sicily for the Fatimids in the tenth century had fragmented after 1040 into three or four warring principalities; one of them, the emir Ibn al-Thumna of Catania and Syracuse, had been defeated by his rival Ibn al-Hawas of Castrogiovanni and Agrigento and in 1060 had appealed to the Normans for help. With this Sicilian Muslim invitation as cover, Roger landed at Messina in May 1061 with about three hundred knights, took the city by surprise, and held it against Ibn al-Hawas. Robert crossed with the main force; at Cerami in June 1063 the brothers, with perhaps a hundred and fifty knights, broke a far larger Muslim army from Agrigento and Palermo and confirmed the bridgehead. From that base Roger pushed inland through the early 1060s in raids and small sieges, defeating Ibn al-Hawas at the battle of Misilmeri in 1068. Palermo, the great Arab capital of the island, held until 1072, when Roger and Robert took it after a five-month siege; Robert in his ducal authority granted the city, with a small surrounding territory, to Roger as count, and returned to the mainland.

For the next twenty years Roger conducted the rest of the conquest alone. Trapani fell in 1077; Taormina in 1079; Syracuse, defended by the last serious naval forces of the Sicilian Muslims, in 1086 after a long siege in which the emir Ibn al-Wardi was killed at sea. The mountain fortress of Castrogiovanni, the centre of the interior, surrendered in 1087 after its emir Ibn al-Hammud accepted Christian baptism, married into the count’s following, and was granted lands in Calabria. The final pockets — Butera, Noto, and the small fortified towns of the southern coast — were taken in 1091. In the same year Roger’s fleet crossed to Malta, expelled the small Arab garrison, accepted the homage of the Christian population that had lived under Muslim rule for two centuries, and added the Maltese archipelago to the county.

What he built on the conquered island was unlike anything else in the Latin Christian world. Roger had no army of settlers behind him — the Norman knights and their followers numbered at most a few thousand — and to hold a population that was at his accession roughly half Greek Orthodox Christian and half Sunni Muslim he chose deliberate toleration. The qadis and Arabic-speaking officials of the previous regime were retained; the diwan and the daftar of Palermo continued to register the count’s lands and revenues in Arabic; Muslim soldiers served in his armies and Muslim peasants on his estates; new Latin bishoprics were created at Mileto, Squillace, Troina, Catania, Agrigento, and Mazara, but the existing Greek monasteries and parishes were preserved and in some cases endowed. The Latin clergy he installed answered directly to him: by the bull Quia propter prudentiam tuam of 5 July 1098 Pope Urban II, in return for the count’s loyalty in the crusading years, granted Roger and his heirs the apostolic legateship of the Sicilian Church, the right to act in place of a papal legate within the island, which his great-grandson Frederick II would still be invoking a century and a half later. Few medieval rulers entered into so favourable a settlement with Rome.

Roger’s domestic life was less serene. His first wife Judith of Évreux, a Norman cousin he had married in 1061 and brought to Sicily as a young woman, died about 1076 having borne him daughters but no surviving son. His second marriage, to Eremberga of Mortain, ended with her death about 1089, again with daughters but no son to inherit. In 1089, already in his late fifties, he married a third time: Adelaide del Vasto, the niece of the marquis Boniface of Savona, a member of the Aleramic family of Piedmont who was barely sixteen. She bore him three sons in the next decade — Simon, born about 1093, and Roger, the future king, born at Mileto on 22 December 1095. He died at Mileto on 22 June 1101, having seen one son grow to ten and the youngest still a child of five. The county he had built was governed for the next eleven years by Adelaide as regent for her sons, and on her departure for Jerusalem in 1113 passed entire and intact to Roger II, who in 1130 would gather it and the duchy of his cousins into the kingdom of Sicily.

The historical place of Roger I is therefore double. He was the last and most successful of the Hauteville brothers, the founder of the Norman state in the south whose institutional framework — bilingual chancery, tolerated minorities, Latin Church under princely control, mercenary cavalry on the iqta’ lands of the previous regime — the kingdom would preserve for two centuries. He was also the personal model that his son and his great-grandson explicitly invoked: when Roger II at Cefalù in 1145 declared the principles of the Sicilian monarchy he claimed only to be continuing what his father had begun, and when Frederick II in his Constitutions of Melfi in 1231 enacted the kingdom’s most ambitious legislation he referenced the count’s practice as precedent. The brothers had set out from a small fief in Normandy with little more than horses and the impatience of younger sons; one of them, by his death, had taken an island the size of a small kingdom from a civilisation older than his own and bequeathed it to his children.

The road continues to Robert Guiscard.
