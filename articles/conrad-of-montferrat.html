Conrad of Montferrat

mid-1140s – 28 April 1192, defender of Tyre, king-elect of Jerusalem

Conrad was born in the middle 1140s, the second son of William V of Montferrat, called William the Old, by Judith of Babenberg. He grew up at his father’s court in Piedmont and in the wars of the marquisate against the Lombard communes, served as a young man in the army of his uncle, the emperor Frederick Barbarossa, in the campaigns of the 1170s, and by his early thirties had a reputation as a fighting man and a diplomat with an unusual gift for picking up languages. The chronicler Ibn al-Athir, who never saw him, knew that he spoke Arabic, Greek, French, Latin, and his native Italian; the Greek chronicler Niketas Choniates, who saw him several times, called him the cleverest of the Latins of his generation. He fought at Legnano against his uncle’s communes in 1176, was briefly imprisoned at Camerino in a skirmish of his father’s wars in the late 1170s, and travelled in the early 1180s to Constantinople in connection with the eastern marriage policy that his father had begun with the betrothal of his eldest brother William Longsword to Sibylla of Jerusalem.

In the summer of 1187 he returned to the city to be married. The emperor Isaac II Angelos, then only just installed by the revolt of September 1185 and badly in need of a Western ally, gave him as a bride his sister Theodora Angelina, the title of Caesar, and command of an imperial corps of Latin and Greek troops. Within months of the wedding Conrad found himself fighting for his brother-in-law’s throne. Alexios Branas, the general who had just defeated the Normans of Sicily at Demetritzes, marched on the capital in the autumn of 1187 and proclaimed himself emperor; Isaac, who had no field army, asked Conrad to organise a defence. Conrad armed his Italian retinue and a body of the city militia, sallied out of the gate of Charisius on a foggy morning, and broke the besiegers in a single afternoon’s engagement on the plain outside the walls; in the mêlée he came on Branas personally and ran him through with his lance. Some weeks later, fearing for his own safety in a court whose intrigues he distrusted, he took ship for the Holy Land with a small Italian following. He was about forty.

He arrived off the coast of Palestine in July 1187 to find that Saladin had just destroyed the army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at the Horns of Hattin and was taking the cities of the coast one after another. The port of Acre had already fallen; the port of Tyre, the next on Saladin’s line of march, was on the point of negotiating its surrender when his galley dropped anchor in the harbour. He landed, tore down the white flag that the city had already hoisted on its walls, accepted the homage of the magistrates, and prepared the defence. Saladin appeared before the walls a few days later; Conrad held the city against two formal sieges, the second of which lasted from November 1187 to New Year 1188 and was conducted by Saladin in person with the full army of Egypt and a fleet of galleys from Alexandria. The arrival of an Italian relief squadron at the height of the second siege broke the Egyptian fleet; the army withdrew. Tyre alone of the great coastal cities had not fallen, and Conrad alone of the lords of the Latin East still held a city in his hand. From that moment he was the de facto leader of the remnant of the kingdom.

The political consequence was an inevitable collision with the surviving king. Guy of Lusignan, the husband of the late Queen Sibylla and the man whose generalship had lost the battle of Hattin, was released by Saladin from prison in the summer of 1188 on the surrender of Ascalon; he presented himself before the walls of Tyre and demanded to be admitted as king. Conrad refused. Guy, with no other recourse, marched south in 1189 and laid siege to Acre with the small force he had been able to gather; the Third Crusade, when it arrived in 1191, found a divided Latin command, with Guy at Acre under the protection of Richard the Lion-heart of England and Conrad at Tyre under the protection of Philip II Augustus of France and Leopold of Austria. In the autumn of 1190 Conrad regularised his position by marrying the heiress of the kingdom, Isabella, the eighteen-year-old half-sister of the late Queen Sibylla. Her existing marriage to the young Humphrey of Toron was dissolved by a panel of Latin bishops on the ground that she had been a minor at the time of the wedding; the legality was disputed in Rome but never overturned. The marriage made Conrad king-consort and the legitimate heir to the throne the moment Guy’s tenure ended.

The dispute occupied Richard and Philip through the whole year of the Third Crusade in Palestine. Acre fell to the crusaders in July 1191; Richard marched south and won the Battle of Arsuf and the campaigns of 1191–1192; Philip went home; the kingship remained undecided. In April 1192, with Richard about to depart for Europe and the political situation impossible to leave open any longer, the barons of the kingdom met at Acre and voted, almost unanimously and over Richard’s opposition, for Conrad. Richard accepted the verdict; Guy was compensated with the lordship of Cyprus, which Richard had taken in 1191 from the Komnenian usurper Isaac. Messengers rode north to Tyre with the news.

On 28 April 1192, days after the messengers reached him and before he had yet been crowned, Conrad went out to dine at the house of his friend Philip, bishop of Beauvais, in the Genoese quarter of Tyre. As he returned to his own house at dusk, two men in monastic dress who had been loitering at a corner approached him; one drew a dagger and stabbed him in the side, the other in the back. He was carried home and died within the hour. The killers were taken alive; under torture they declared themselves agents of Rashid ad-Din Sinan, the master of the Nizari Ismaili stronghold at Masyaf in the Syrian mountains, the chief whom contemporaries called the Old Man of the Mountain. Sinan’s motive was never fully clear; rival contemporary accounts attribute the contract variously to Saladin, to Richard, to a private grievance over a Genoese ship Conrad had seized, and to the assassins’ own political calculation. Modern scholars generally regard the Old Man’s own initiative, with or without Saladin’s knowledge, as the most likely answer.

Isabella was eighteen and seven months pregnant by him. She married Henry II of Champagne eight days later, on the encouragement of Richard and of the surviving barons; their daughter Maria, born after Conrad’s death, would be the heiress of the kingdom, marry John of Brienne in 1210, and through her own daughter pass the line to the emperor Frederick II. The defence of Tyre survived as the foundation event of the Third Crusade’s small recoveries; Conrad himself was remembered, in the chivalric literature of the next century, as the model of a clever and obstinate captain.
