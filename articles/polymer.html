A polymer is a molecule of immoderate size, assembled by joining many small molecules — monomers — into chains that may run to hundreds of thousands of repeating units. The principle is that of the pearl necklace or the railway train: a modest unit, repeated with patience, yields something of altogether different character. Nature hit upon the trick long before chemists did, building wood from cellulose, wool from protein, and heredity itself from nucleic acids; humanity’s own contributions — polyethylene, nylon, and their numerous kin — have furnished the modern world so thoroughly that the present age is sometimes called, not always as a compliment, the Age of Plastics.

Etymology and a long misunderstanding

The word was coined in 1833 by the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius, from the Greek polys (“many”) and meros (“part”), though he meant by it something rather different from the modern sense. For nearly a century thereafter, respectable opinion held that substances such as rubber and cellulose were mere aggregates — small molecules huddling together by some vague association. The German chemist Hermann Staudinger insisted, from 1920 onward, that they were true covalent giants: single molecules of enormous length. He was met with the advice, from a colleague, to abandon his “greasy chemistry” for tidier subjects. Staudinger persisted, was vindicated, and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953 for establishing the science of macromolecules.

Architecture

A polymer’s character is dictated less by what its monomer is than by how the chains are arranged. Chains may be linear, branched, or cross-linked into a single continuous network; they may pack into ordered crystalline regions or sprawl in amorphous tangles, and most real materials are a compromise between the two. A few consequences are worth setting down:

  • Thermoplastics — chains held together only by their mutual entanglement — soften on heating and may be melted and re-moulded indefinitely. Polyethylene and PET are of this kind.
  • Thermosets — chains cross-linked into one vast molecule — cannot be re-melted; heat them too far and they char rather than flow. Bakelite and cured epoxies belong here.
  • Elastomers — lightly cross-linked chains, coiled at rest — may be stretched to several times their length and will return, which is the whole secret of rubber.

The chemist’s vocabulary distinguishes homopolymers, built from one monomer repeated, from copolymers, which interleave two or more — an arrangement that allows properties to be blended much as an alloy blends metals.

Polymers in nature

Nature is the senior practitioner. Cellulose, a polymer of glucose, is the most abundant organic compound on Earth and the substance of cotton, paper, and timber. Proteins are polymers of amino acids, spun by silkworms into thread and folded by cells into machinery. DNA is a polymer whose sequence of units is the point of the exercise: a molecule employed as a document. Natural rubber, tapped from Hevea brasiliensis, was known to Mesoamerican peoples for centuries before European chemistry could say what it was, let alone improve upon it.

The synthetic century

The synthetic era opened in stages. Charles Goodyear’s vulcanisation of rubber (1839) cross-linked a natural polymer into usefulness. Celluloid (1860s) modified cellulose into the first commercial plastic, marketed, among other things, as a substitute for ivory billiard balls. The decisive step came in 1907, when Leo Baekeland condensed phenol with formaldehyde to produce Bakelite, the first fully synthetic polymer — a dark, glossy thermoset that soon housed radios, telephones, and electrical fittings across the world.

There followed, in remarkably quick succession, polystyrene and PVC, polyethylene (discovered at ICI in 1933 by an experiment that had, strictly speaking, gone wrong), and nylon, unveiled by Wallace Carothers’ team at DuPont and presented to the public in 1939 in the form of stockings, four million pairs of which sold in four days. The Second World War turned polymer chemistry into a strategic industry; the peace that followed turned it into a domestic one.

Virtues and debts

The virtues of synthetic polymers are the familiar ones: they are light, cheap, mouldable into any shape, indifferent to rust and rot, and excellent insulators. The debt is the same list read in a different light. A material indifferent to rot does not politely disappear when discarded; common plastics persist for centuries, fragmenting into the microplastics now found from the deep sea to the human bloodstream. Of all the plastic ever produced, only a small fraction has been recycled. The present efforts of the field — biodegradable polymers, chemical recycling that returns chains to their monomers, and polymers grown from plants rather than petroleum — amount to teaching the discipline’s masterpiece, durability, when to take its leave.

A deeper delve

How chains are built: the two great mechanisms

Every synthetic polymer is made by one of two broad strategies, and the distinction governs almost everything that follows — the speed of the reaction, the shape of the molecular-weight distribution, and the conditions under which the chemist must work. The older language, due to Carothers, called them condensation and addition polymerisation; the more exact modern language calls them step-growth and chain-growth, for it is the kinetics of growth, not the by-products, that truly separates them.

In step-growth polymerisation, any two molecules bearing the appropriate reactive ends may join — monomer to monomer, dimer to trimer, oligomer to oligomer — and the chains lengthen by stitching together whatever fragments happen to meet. The reaction is typically a familiar piece of organic chemistry, an esterification or an amidation, often expelling a small molecule such as water as it proceeds; hence the older name “condensation”. The consequence of this democratic joining is that high molecular weight arrives only at the very end. At 95 per cent conversion the average chain is still only about twenty units long; to reach the thousands of units that make a useful fibre, the reaction must be driven to better than 99 per cent completion, which demands scrupulously balanced reactants and the diligent removal of the condensation by-product. Polyesters, polyamides (nylons), polyurethanes, and the phenolic and epoxy thermosets are all step-growth products.

Chain-growth polymerisation works on an entirely different rhythm. Here a reactive centre — a free radical, a cation, an anion, or a metal–carbon bond — is created on one molecule, and monomers add to it one at a time, each addition regenerating the active centre at the chain end so that a single chain may grow to full length in a fraction of a second before it is extinguished. The classic instance is free-radical polymerisation, which proceeds in three acts: initiation, in which a peroxide or azo compound decomposes to give radicals; propagation, the rapid serial addition of monomer; and termination, in which two growing radicals meet and quench one another by combination or disproportionation. Because chains begin, grow, and die throughout the reaction, high polymer is present from the start, and unreacted monomer lingers to the end — the mirror image of the step-growth picture. The vinyl polymers — polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, poly(vinyl chloride), poly(methyl methacrylate) — are the great chain-growth family.

Living and controlled polymerisation

Ordinary radical polymerisation is a rowdy affair: chains are born and killed ceaselessly, and the chemist has little control over their length or their ends. In 1956 Michael Szwarc demonstrated living anionic polymerisation, in which termination and chain transfer are suppressed altogether. The carbanion at the chain end remains active once the monomer is exhausted, so the chains all grow in step, reach nearly identical lengths, and stand ready to resume growth if fresh monomer is added — whence the prized ability to build block copolymers by adding a second monomer after the first is consumed. Styrene–butadiene–styrene block copolymers, the thermoplastic elastomers that put the spring into shoe soles, are made in just this way.

The closing decades of the twentieth century extended this discipline to the radical mechanism, hitherto thought too unruly to tame. Controlled radical methods — nitroxide-mediated polymerisation, atom-transfer radical polymerisation (ATRP), and reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) — all work by the same gambit: the great majority of chains are kept reversibly “asleep” in a dormant form, with only a vanishing fraction awake and growing at any instant, so that the destructive collisions of two live radicals become rare. The chains therefore grow slowly but uniformly, and the chemist regains the architectural control of living systems while keeping the tolerance and convenience of radicals.

Catalysis: Ziegler–Natta and the metallocenes

The polyolefins, which dominate world production by tonnage, owe their existence to catalysis. Polyethylene made by the high-pressure radical route is heavily branched and of low density; ethylene shows little inclination to polymerise into anything better under mild conditions. In the early 1950s Karl Ziegler, in Mülheim, found that certain titanium halides activated by aluminium alkyls would polymerise ethylene at modest pressures into a linear, high-density material. Giulio Natta, in Milan, applied the same family of catalysts to propylene and discovered that they imposed stereoregularity: the methyl side-groups were all placed on the same side of the chain (the isotactic arrangement), yielding a crystalline, tough, commercially priceless polypropylene where the random (atactic) material is a useless grease. The two shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and the term tacticity — the geometry of successive stereocentres along a chain — entered the vocabulary.

A second revolution arrived in the 1980s with the single-site metallocene catalysts — sandwich complexes of zirconium or titanium activated by methylaluminoxane — whose every active centre is structurally identical. Where the classical Ziegler–Natta solid presents a heterogeneous crowd of sites of varying activity, the metallocene offers one well-defined site, and so produces chains of narrow length distribution and exquisitely controllable tacticity and comonomer placement. The chemist may now, in effect, design the chain from the catalyst outward, tuning the melting point and stiffness of a polyolefin to order.

Molecular weight and its distributions

A small molecule has a molecular weight; a polymer sample has a distribution of them, for the chains in any real batch are of many different lengths. This is among the deepest peculiarities of the subject, and a polymer’s properties depend not only on its average length but on the breadth of the spread. Two averages are in common use. The number-average molecular weight counts every chain equally, and so reflects the colligative behaviour — how the chains lower a melting point or raise an osmotic pressure. The weight-average weights each chain by its own mass, and so is biased toward the long chains that dominate such properties as melt viscosity and toughness. Their ratio, the dispersity (formerly the polydispersity index), measures the breadth of the distribution: it is exactly one for a perfectly uniform sample, runs to about two for an ideal step-growth product, and is kept near unity by living methods.

These quantities are measured by various means. Osmometry and end-group analysis yield the number-average; light scattering yields the weight-average directly, since long chains scatter more; and gel-permeation (size-exclusion) chromatography, which sieves chains by their size in solution, has become the routine workhorse, returning the whole distribution at a stroke. The practical importance is blunt: a polymer below some threshold length is a brittle wax of no use, while above it mechanical strength rises steeply with chain length before levelling off. There is, for every application, a molecular weight worth aiming at, and the art of the process chemist is largely the art of hitting it.

Polymer physics: why chains behave as they do

If polymer chemistry concerns how chains are built, polymer physics concerns what they then do en masse, and it is a science of tangles, motion, and compromise between order and disorder. Its central facts have no counterpart among small molecules.

The glass transition and melting

A small-molecule solid has a sharp melting point; a polymer has, more characteristically, a glass transition. As an amorphous polymer is cooled, it does not crystallise but stiffens gradually until, at a temperature denoted Tg, the long-range wriggling of chain segments freezes out and the soft, leathery material becomes a hard, brittle glass. Above Tg poly(methyl methacrylate) is a rubber; below it, the rigid “Perspex” of aircraft canopies. Natural rubber has a glass transition well below room temperature, which is why it is supple in the hand and why, plunged into liquid nitrogen, it shatters like crockery. Polymers that can crystallise possess in addition a true melting point, Tm, above which the ordered regions dissolve into the melt; but crystallisation is never complete, and such materials are best pictured as crystalline grains suspended in an amorphous sea.

Crystallinity

That a tangle of long chains can crystallise at all is a small marvel. The chains do not pack as neat rods but fold back upon themselves at intervals of some ten nanometres, building thin platelet crystals called lamellae; these in turn radiate from nucleation points into the spherical aggregates, spherulites, whose Maltese-cross figures under crossed polarisers are among the prettier sights in the microscope. Regular chains free of bulky side-groups — high-density polyethylene, isotactic polypropylene, the polyesters and polyamides — crystallise readily and turn translucent and tough; irregular or atactic chains cannot pack at all and remain glassy and clear. Crystallinity buys stiffness, strength, chemical resistance and opacity, all at the cost of transparency and a certain brittleness.

Viscoelasticity, entanglement and reptation

A polymer melt or solid is neither a simple liquid nor a simple solid but viscoelastic: it stores energy like a spring on short timescales and flows like a syrup on long ones, so that the same material may bounce when struck smartly and sag when left to its own devices — the behaviour every child has met in a hand of so-called silly putty. The governing fact is that long chains entangle, looping through one another like cooked spaghetti. Below a certain critical length the chains slide past freely; above it the entanglements act as temporary cross-links, and the melt viscosity climbs with roughly the 3.4 power of molecular weight — double the chain length and the melt becomes some tenfold more sluggish, which is why high polymers are so stubborn to process.

How an entangled chain moves at all was the great puzzle answered by the reptation model of Pierre-Gilles de Gennes (who took the 1991 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on ordering in complex matter, polymers among them) and elaborated by Doi and Edwards. A chain cannot pass through its neighbours; it is confined, as de Gennes pictured it, within a notional tube formed by the surrounding chains, and can escape only by slithering, snake-like, along its own length until it has worked free of the original tube. This serpentine creep — reptation, from the Latin for crawling — predicts the strong molecular-weight dependence of viscosity and remains the canonical picture of how molten polymers flow.

Rubber elasticity

The springiness of rubber is one of the most counter-intuitive phenomena in physics, for it is driven not by energy but by entropy. A lightly cross-linked elastomer above its glass transition consists of chains coiled into the random, high-entropy tangles they prefer. To stretch the rubber is to pull those coils toward the orderly, low-entropy extended state, and the chains resist precisely because they are thereby being deprived of their disorder; release them, and the relentless statistical preference for randomness snaps them back. The thermodynamic signature of this entropic origin is delightfully strange: a stretched rubber band warms as it is extended and cools as it relaxes (one may verify it against the lip), and a stretched band under fixed load contracts when heated, where any ordinary solid would expand. The light cross-links supplied by vulcanisation are what give the network a memory of its shape and prevent the chains from simply flowing apart.

A fuller history

The story of polymers is older than the science of them, for humanity worked these materials for millennia without the least idea of what they were.

Rubber and Goodyear. Natural rubber reached Europe as a curiosity — useful chiefly for rubbing out pencil marks, whence its English name — but it was hopeless as a material, sticky in summer heat and rigid in winter cold. Charles Goodyear, a self-taught and famously impoverished American, pursued the problem with monomaniacal devotion until, in 1839, he discovered (the legend says by accident, dropping a rubber-and-sulphur mixture on a hot stove) that heating rubber with sulphur transformed it into a stable, elastic solid serviceable across a wide range of temperatures. The process, later christened vulcanisation after the Roman god of fire, cross-links the rubber chains with short sulphur bridges. We now understand it as the conversion of a tangle of separate chains into a single elastic network; Goodyear understood only that it worked, died in debt, and gave his name to a tyre company he never owned.

Celluloid. The first commercial plastic was not synthetic but a chemically modified natural polymer. In the 1860s the Englishman Alexander Parkes and, with greater commercial success, the American John Wesley Hyatt treated cellulose with nitric acid and plasticised it with camphor to produce celluloid, a mouldable solid promoted, in response to a prize offered for a substitute for increasingly scarce ivory, as the material for billiard balls, combs, collars, and eventually photographic and cinema film. Celluloid had the alarming vice of flammability — nitrocellulose is a cousin of guncotton — but it established that a material might be manufactured rather than grown.

Bakelite. The decisive break came in 1907, when the Belgian-American chemist Leo Baekeland, already rich from inventing a photographic paper, condensed phenol with formaldehyde under heat and pressure to make Bakelite — the first wholly synthetic polymer, owing nothing to any natural starting material. A hard, dark, infusible thermoset, an excellent electrical insulator and impervious to heat and solvents, it was the perfect material for the electrifying world, and within a generation it had become the substance of telephones, radio cabinets, switches, and ten thousand small objects of the machine age. Baekeland is fairly called the father of the plastics industry.

Staudinger’s heresy. Yet no one knew what these materials were. The reigning view held that rubber, cellulose and proteins were colloidal aggregates — clusters of ordinary small molecules bound by weak forces. From 1920 Hermann Staudinger argued, against the weight of his profession, that they were genuine covalent molecules of gigantic size, true macromolecules with bonds no different from those in any other compound. The opposition was fierce; a distinguished colleague urged him to give up the notion, for “there are no molecules so large.” Staudinger marshalled the evidence — chiefly that the viscosity of polymer solutions tracked chain length in a lawful way, and that chemical reactions altered a polymer without breaking it into small pieces — and by the late 1930s the macromolecular hypothesis had carried the day. His Nobel Prize followed in 1953.

Carothers and nylon. While Staudinger argued the theory, Wallace Carothers built the proof. Hired by DuPont in 1928 to do fundamental research, Carothers set out to make macromolecules deliberately, by controlled step-growth chemistry, and in doing so laid down much of the conceptual framework of the field, including the distinction between condensation and addition polymers. His laboratory produced first neoprene, a synthetic rubber, and then, in 1935, the polyamide that would be called nylon — a fibre that could be drawn, cold, into a strong, lustrous, silk-like thread. DuPont unveiled it at the 1939 World’s Fair; the famous nylon stockings reached the shops in 1940, and four million pairs are said to have sold in their first days. Carothers, who suffered from severe depression, did not live to see the triumph: he took his own life in 1937.

Wartime rubber. The Second World War made polymers a matter of national survival. When Japanese conquests cut the Allies off from the rubber plantations of Southeast Asia, the United States mounted a crash programme to manufacture synthetic rubber, scaling up styrene–butadiene copolymer (then called GR-S, or government rubber–styrene) from a laboratory curiosity to hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year within a few seasons — one of the great feats of industrial chemistry, and a decisive contribution to the war effort. The same years saw poly(methyl methacrylate) glazing aircraft canopies, polyethylene insulating the cables of the new radar, and PTFE, freshly discovered, sealing the corrosive uranium chemistry of the atomic bomb.

The polyolefin boom. The post-war decades belonged to the polyolefins. The Ziegler–Natta catalysts of the early 1950s made linear high-density polyethylene and isotactic polypropylene available cheaply and in bulk, and the world filled with moulded chairs, squeeze bottles, food film, crates, pipes, and the disposable packaging that is at once the triumph and the curse of the trade. By the closing decades of the century, plastics had displaced wood, metal, glass and paper across vast swathes of manufacture, and annual production, a few hundred thousand tonnes before the war, had climbed past a hundred million.

The commodity polymers, one by one

A handful of cheap, high-volume thermoplastics — the “commodity” plastics — make up the great bulk of what is produced and discarded. They repay individual acquaintance.

Polyethylene (PE), the simplest and most produced of all, is nothing but long chains of –CH2–. It comes in several guises. Low-density polyethylene (LDPE), the original high-pressure radical product, is branched, soft and clingy — the stuff of squeeze bottles and the thin film of bags. High-density polyethylene (HDPE), the linear Ziegler–Natta product, is stiffer, stronger and more crystalline — milk jugs, pipes, chemical drums. Linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE), a copolymer with a controlled scatter of short branches, has largely captured the film market. And ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene, with chains millions of units long, is tough enough for artificial hip joints and bulletproof vests.

Polypropylene (PP), a close cousin bearing a methyl group on every other carbon, owes its usefulness to the isotactic regularity Natta learned to impose. Stiffer and more heat-resistant than polyethylene, it serves for car bumpers, food tubs that survive the dishwasher, the living hinges of flip-top caps (which it endures being folded a million times), and woven sacks and ropes.

Poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) is half chlorine by weight, which makes it inherently flame-retardant and unusually cheap, the chlorine coming free, as it were, from common salt. Rigid PVC builds window frames, drainpipes and the once ubiquitous gramophone record; plasticised with phthalate softeners it becomes the flexible material of flooring, cable sheathing, garden hose and the medical drip bag. Its chlorine content also makes it the most problematic of the commodity plastics to burn or recycle.

Polystyrene (PS) is the clear, hard, brittle plastic of cheap cutlery and CD cases; blown with gas into expanded polystyrene it becomes the white foam of coffee cups and protective packaging, some ninety-odd per cent air. Toughened by blending with rubber (high-impact polystyrene) it loses its brittleness for yoghurt pots and appliance housings.

Poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) is a polyester, and the only commodity plastic that is both a major fibre (sold as Dacron or Terylene) and a major packaging material (the clear, tough, gas-tight bottle of fizzy drinks). It is also, by a wide margin, the commodity plastic most successfully recycled, since the bottles are clean, identifiable and worth collecting.

Engineering and high-performance polymers

Above the commodity plastics sits a tier of costlier materials chosen for performance rather than price — the engineering polymers — and, above them again, a small aristocracy of high-performance polymers that survive conditions which would destroy the rest.

Polycarbonate is a tough, glass-clear engineering thermoplastic so resistant to impact that it glazes riot shields and the canopies of aircraft, and was, before the rise of streaming, the substance of compact discs. The polyamides (the various nylons) and the acetal resins (POM) serve as the self-lubricating gears, bearings and clips of countless mechanisms, cheaper and quieter than metal. ABS, a terpolymer of acrylonitrile, butadiene and styrene, combines stiffness with toughness and a fine finish, and is the plastic of telephone handsets, car trim, and the interlocking brick beloved of children.

The high-performance grades are stranger and dearer. Kevlar, an aromatic polyamide (aramid) devised by Stephanie Kwolek at DuPont in the 1960s, forms rigid rod-like molecules that align into liquid-crystalline order; spun into fibre it is, weight for weight, several times stronger than steel, and stops bullets. PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene, Teflon), discovered by accident at DuPont in 1938 when a cylinder of the gas polymerised to a waxy white solid, is all but chemically invincible, has the lowest friction of any solid known, and clads everything from frying pans to laboratory seals. PEEK (polyether ether ketone) keeps its strength at temperatures that would melt most plastics, resists nearly all chemicals, and is trusted for aerospace components and spinal implants. Such polymers are sold by the kilogram where commodity plastics are sold by the tonne.

Biological polymers at depth

The living world is built almost entirely of polymers, and three classes carry the burden of life: the proteins, the polysaccharides, and the nucleic acids. To these the rubber tree adds a fourth of more parochial interest.

Proteins are step-growth copolymers of twenty kinds of amino acid, linked by the amide (peptide) bond. What distinguishes them from any synthetic polymer is that their sequence is not statistical but exactly specified, written out by the cell’s machinery, so that each protein is a single defined molecule rather than a distribution. That precise sequence dictates a precise folding into a compact three-dimensional shape, and it is the shape that does the work — catalysing reactions as enzymes, bearing loads as the collagen of tendon and the keratin of hair and horn, or, drawn out into the β-sheets of silk, forming a fibre tougher for its weight than most that chemistry can spin.

Polysaccharides are polymers of sugar, and the difference a single bond can make is among the most instructive lessons in the subject. Cellulose and starch are both polymers of glucose; they differ only in the geometry of the linkage between successive units. In starch the linkage lets the chain coil, giving a digestible energy store; in cellulose it forces the chain straight, so that the chains lie side by side and lock together by hydrogen bonds into the rigid, water-insoluble, indigestible microfibrils that stiffen every plant cell wall — the most abundant organic material on the planet. Chitin, a related polymer bearing nitrogen, performs the same structural office in the shells of crabs and the exoskeletons of insects.

Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are polymers whose backbone of alternating sugar and phosphate is monotonous but whose pendant bases spell out, in a four-letter alphabet, the genetic text. Here the polymer is valued not for its bulk properties but purely for its sequence: it is a molecule used as writing, copied, read and translated. That a chain chemically so similar to the polyesters and polyamides of the factory should also be the archive of heredity is a reminder that the polymer principle is indifferent to its application.

Natural rubber, finally, is cis-polyisoprene, a chain-growth polymer the rubber tree assembles from isoprene units with a stereochemical precision that long embarrassed the chemists trying to copy it. The all-cis geometry keeps the chains from crystallising at rest, so the material is soft and springy; the rare trans isomer, gutta-percha, is by contrast a hard solid once used to insulate submarine cables. It was the eventual mastery of such stereochemistry, by the Ziegler–Natta route, that at last let industry make a synthetic rubber faithful to the natural one.

Turning resin into things: processing

A polymer leaves the reactor as pellets or powder; the shaping of it into useful objects is a craft in its own right, and the great economy of plastics lies in how cheaply and quickly that shaping can be done.

Extrusion is the workhorse: a rotating screw conveys, melts and pressurises the polymer and forces it continuously through a shaped die, producing anything of constant cross-section — pipe, sheet, film, fibre, cable sheathing, the seal around a car door. Injection moulding takes the same melt and shoots it under high pressure into a closed, cooled mould, where it solidifies to the mould’s shape in seconds before the mould opens and ejects it; it is the method behind the overwhelming majority of moulded plastic objects, from bottle caps to keyboard keys, and its moulds, costly to cut, are economic only across long production runs. Blow moulding inflates a hot tube of polymer against the wall of a mould with compressed air to make hollow articles — bottles above all. Spinning draws fibres by forcing the melt or a solution through the fine holes of a spinneret, the filaments then being stretched to align the chains and develop their strength.

To these shaping operations is added a pharmacopoeia of additives, for the bare polymer is rarely fit for use. Plasticisers — the phthalates added to PVC the most familiar, and the most contested on health grounds — are small molecules that wedge between the chains, lower the glass transition, and turn a rigid plastic supple. Stabilisers protect against the assaults of heat, oxygen and ultraviolet light that would otherwise break the chains and yellow the material. Antioxidants, flame retardants, lubricants, pigments and fillers each play their part, and reinforcing fibres of glass or carbon turn a humble thermoplastic into a structural composite stiff enough for aircraft. The finished “plastic” of commerce is thus a formulated mixture, of which the polymer is the principal but seldom the only ingredient.

Polymers that conduct: the 2000 Nobel

Polymers were prized for a century precisely because they did not conduct electricity; they insulated the cables and the switches of the electrical age. It was therefore a genuine surprise when, in the 1970s, Hideki Shirakawa, Alan MacDiarmid and Alan Heeger found that polyacetylene, doped with a little iodine, conducted electricity nearly as well as a metal — its conductivity rising through many orders of magnitude. The trick is a backbone of alternating single and double bonds (a conjugated chain) along which electrons may travel once the doping has created the charge carriers to do so. The discovery opened the field of conducting polymers, won its three authors the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2000, and underlies the flexible displays, organic light-emitting diodes, printed circuitry and plastic solar cells of the present day — the marriage of the cheapness and flexibility of plastic to the electronic functions once reserved for silicon and copper.

The environmental reckoning

The very durability that recommends synthetic polymers has become their gravest liability. Humanity has produced, since the middle of the twentieth century, something on the order of eight or nine billion tonnes of plastic, the great majority of it in the few decades since production accelerated; annual output now runs to around four hundred million tonnes. By the reckoning of Geyer, Jambeck and Law (2017), only a small fraction of all the plastic ever made — under a tenth — has been recycled; a similar fraction has been incinerated; and the great remainder has been buried in landfill or simply discarded into the environment, where, being indifferent to rot, it persists.

The familiar resin identification codes — the numbers one to seven within a chasing-arrows triangle, stamped on packaging since 1988 — are widely and wrongly read as a promise of recyclability. They are only an identification of the resin. In practice, only PET (1) and HDPE (2) are recycled at any scale; mechanical recycling, moreover, degrades the polymer a little each cycle, shortening the chains and clouding the colour, so that “recycling” is more often a one-step descent to a lower-grade use (downcycling) than a true closing of the loop.

Microplastics. Plastics in the environment do not vanish; they embrittle under sunlight and abrade into ever smaller fragments, the microplastics (below five millimetres) and nanoplastics now found everywhere that has been looked — in Arctic ice and ocean trenches, in tap water, table salt and bottled water, and, latterly, in human blood, lungs and placenta. Their long-term toll on health and ecosystems is the subject of urgent and still inconclusive study. The ocean plastics that gather in the great mid-ocean gyres — the so-called garbage patches, more a thin diffuse soup than an island one could stand upon — are the visible emblem of the problem, strangling and starving seabirds and turtles that mistake the fragments for food.

Biodegradable polymers. One response is to design the durability out. Polylactic acid (PLA), a polyester made from the lactic acid of fermented maize or sugar, is both bio-based and compostable — though only, as a rule, in the heat of an industrial composter, not the garden heap or the sea — and serves for disposable cups, cutlery and the filament of desktop three-dimensional printers. The polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA), polyesters synthesised and stored by bacteria as an energy reserve, biodegrade more readily, even in seawater, but remain costly to make. Such materials answer the disposal problem only where genuine composting follows; thrown into ordinary landfill or littered, a “biodegradable” plastic may persist nearly as stubbornly as any other.

Chemical recycling. The more ambitious response is to reverse the polymerisation — to break the chains chemically back into their monomers or into oils, which may then be repurified and polymerised afresh without the degradation that dogs mechanical recycling. Depolymerisation works cleanly for the step-growth polymers, whose linkages can be hydrolysed; pyrolysis cracks the polyolefins into a synthetic crude. Chemical recycling promises a true closing of the loop but remains, for now, energy-hungry and difficult to make pay, and its eventual scale is among the open questions of the field.

Current frontiers

The science continues to surprise. Self-healing polymers carry within them the means to repair their own damage — whether by embedded microcapsules of a liquid healing agent that burst and cure when a crack passes through them, or by reversible bonds that re-form across a fracture when warmed — in the hope of materials that mend a scratch as living tissue mends a wound.

Vitrimers, a class introduced in 2011, attempt to reconcile the old opposition between thermoplastic and thermoset. Their cross-links can quietly rearrange themselves at high temperature, swapping partners without altering the total number of bonds, so that the material flows and may be reshaped or repaired like a thermoplastic while retaining, when cool, the solvent resistance and strength of a thermoset — and, crucially, the recyclability that thermosets have always lacked.

Polymer electrolytes — solid or gel-like polymers that conduct ions rather than electrons — are sought as the safer, non-flammable replacement for the volatile liquid electrolytes of lithium batteries, a key piece of the long-promised solid-state cell. And the medical polymers have become a field unto themselves: the dissolving sutures that the body absorbs, the polymer scaffolds on which tissue is coaxed to grow, the hydrogels of soft contact lenses and drug-delivery depots that meter out their cargo over weeks. From the rubbing-out of pencil marks to the rebuilding of the body, the polymer has proved the most protean of materials — and the discipline’s present task is to keep its great gift of permanence from outlasting its welcome.
