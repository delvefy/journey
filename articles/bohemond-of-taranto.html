Bohemond of Taranto

c. 1054 – 1111, prince of Taranto and of Antioch, leader of the First Crusade

Bohemond was born about 1054 in Calabria, the elder son of Robert Guiscard, then count of Apulia, by his first wife Alberada of Buonalbergo. He was baptised Mark, but at his christening Guiscard, struck by the size of his infant boy, gave him the name of the legendary giant Buamundus of an old chanson, and the byname stuck for life. The marriage of his parents was annulled in 1058 on the convenient ground of consanguinity, when Guiscard wanted the Lombard alliance offered by Sichelgaita of Salerno; Bohemond was thereby relegated, in his father’s ducal scheme, behind his younger half-brothers by the second marriage. The principal share — the duchy of Apulia, Calabria and Sicily — was reserved for Roger Borsa, and the slight was felt by Bohemond and by his partisans for the next thirty years.

From his early twenties he served as the chief lieutenant of his father’s eastern policy. In May 1081 he crossed the Adriatic in the vanguard of Guiscard’s Balkan expedition and took part in the great battle outside Dyrrhachium on 18 October, where he commanded the Norman right and after the rout of the Byzantine left was decisive in breaking the imperial centre. When Guiscard was recalled to Italy by the imperial assault on Gregory VII, Bohemond was left in command of the army in Greece; in 1082 and 1083 he marched into Thessaly, defeated Alexios I Komnenos twice in pitched battle, and besieged Larissa for six months. The expedition collapsed only when Alexios bought up his Italian and Apulian troops by means of money raised on a sale of Byzantine church plate. Bohemond returned to Italy in 1083 with nothing to show for two years of victory.

The death of Robert Guiscard in July 1085 left him with no inheritance. He fought a private war with his half-brother Roger Borsa for two years in Apulia, and by the settlement of 1087 was given the principality of Taranto, the cities of Otranto, Bari and Brindisi, and other lordships of the Adriatic coast — less than he had claimed, but enough to make him the second power of the duchy. There he sat almost ten years, restless, while his cousins on the Sicilian and Calabrian side of the family extended their conquests against the Muslims. The opportunity came in 1095. The preaching of Pope Urban II at Clermont in November of that year, in response to Alexios I’s request for western mercenaries against the Seljuk Turks, set in motion the armed pilgrimage that the contemporaries called iter Hierosolymitanum and posterity the First Crusade. Bohemond, besieging the rebellious city of Amalfi alongside Roger Borsa in the summer of 1096, took the cross with theatrical publicity, tore his cloak into crosses for his followers, and in October crossed the Adriatic at the head of perhaps five hundred Norman knights and several thousand foot. He was the most experienced soldier of the western princes and, in the hostile estimate of Anna Komnene, the most dangerous.

The Byzantine reception was understandably wary. The army that had nearly broken her father’s reign was now coming through his territory as a pilgrim. Bohemond conducted his march from Avlona through Macedonia to Constantinople with disciplined restraint, presented himself at the Blachernai palace in April 1097, and there swore the oath of fealty Alexios required from each of the crusading princes: any city of the old Byzantine territory recovered by the crusade was to be returned to the empire. The oath was sworn with a mental reservation that everyone present understood. From Constantinople the crusade crossed to Asia, took Nicaea after a six-week siege in June 1097, and at Dorylaeum on 1 July annihilated the Seljuk sultan Kilij Arslan’s army — a victory in which Bohemond’s contingent, attacked first, held the line until the rest of the army came up. The march across Anatolia and into Cilicia brought the crusade to the walls of Antioch in October 1097.

The siege of Antioch was the central event of his career. It lasted eight months in worsening conditions of famine and desertion; many of the western leaders — including Stephen of Blois — abandoned the army; the city was taken at last on the night of 3 June 1098 by treachery, in which a tower-warden named Firouz, suborned by Bohemond, opened a section of the wall. Two days later a Turkish relief army under the atabeg Kerbogha of Mosul, twenty thousand strong, arrived and besieged the crusaders inside the city they had just stormed. On 28 June Bohemond, in tactical command of the sortie, led the starving army out of the gates and broke Kerbogha’s host in a single charge. The victory was widely attributed to the discovery a few days before of the Holy Lance by the visionary Peter Bartholomew, but the disposition of the battle was Bohemond’s.

After Kerbogha’s defeat Bohemond claimed Antioch as his own principality, refusing to deliver it to the Byzantine governor as the oath to Alexios required. He argued that the emperor had broken faith first by failing to march with the relief column the previous spring. Raymond IV of Toulouse, the other leading prince, contested the claim; for a year the leaders quarrelled at Antioch while the rank and file pressed on to Jerusalem under Godfrey of Bouillon and Raymond. Jerusalem fell in July 1099; Bohemond was not present, having been left as the new prince of Antioch in charge of a tiny Latin principality between Byzantine, Armenian, and Turkish lordships. He held the city for seven years against pressure on every side. In 1100 he was captured by the Danishmend emir Malik Ghazi during a raid into Cappadocia, held for ransom in the fortress of Niksar, and released only in 1103 after the payment of one hundred thousand bezants and his betrothal to a Danishmend princess in some abandoned scheme of dynastic alliance. In May 1104 his army, reinforced by his nephew Tancred and by Baldwin of Edessa, was destroyed by Ridwan of Aleppo and Soqman of Mardin at the battle of Harran; the Latin frontier in northern Syria contracted; Antioch lost Cilicia to the Byzantines and Edessa for a few months to the Turks.

Bohemond left the East at the end of 1104 to recruit in the West. He toured France through 1106 preaching a fresh crusade aimed openly against Constantinople, married Constance, daughter of King Philip I, in the cathedral of Chartres in March 1106, raised an army of perhaps thirty thousand and crossed to Avlona in October 1107. The campaign was a strategic and tactical disaster: Alexios refused battle and starved the crusader force in the hills outside Dyrrhachium through 1107 and 1108. By the Treaty of Devol of September 1108, in the most humiliating settlement of his life, Bohemond acknowledged that he held the principality of Antioch as a fief of the Byzantine emperor, was to hold no city forbidden by the emperor, and would expel from Antioch his nephew Tancred who had governed the principality during his absence. He never returned to the East. He died at his castle of Bari in March 1111 and was buried in a small octagonal mausoleum of his own building at the cathedral of Canosa di Puglia, where his tomb — a bronze door with the inscription Boamundus in Greek and Latin letters — still survives. The principality of Antioch he had won by treachery and held by force passed to his son Bohemond II at the age of three and lasted under his family until 1130; his great enemy Alexios outlived him by seven years.
