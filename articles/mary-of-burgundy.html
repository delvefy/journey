Mary of Burgundy

1457 – 1482, Duchess of Burgundy in her own right, heiress of Charles the Bold, the bride whose marriage carried the Low Countries into the House of Habsburg

Mary was born at the Coudenberg Palace in Brussels on 13 February 1457, the only surviving child of Charles, then count of Charolais and later duke of Burgundy, and his second wife Isabella of Bourbon. The duchy of Burgundy under her grandfather Philip the Good and her father Charles the Bold was already the most spectacular court in Christendom and one of the most coherent territorial states in Europe, a corridor of rich provinces running from the Somme to the Zuiderzee and including Flanders, Brabant, Hainaut, Holland, Zeeland, Luxembourg, Franche-Comté, and the duchy of Burgundy proper. Mary, called from infancy la riche héritière, the rich heiress, was the only descendant likely to inherit the whole.

Her mother died at Antwerp in 1465 when Mary was eight, and the household passed under the care of her grandmother Isabella of Portugal until her father remarried in 1468. The new duchess was Margaret of York, sister of Edward IV of England, twenty-two years old at the wedding, who became and remained Mary's affectionate stepmother. Mary's education was the standard of the Burgundian court at its highest point: French and Dutch from childhood, some Latin and Italian, music, dancing, the genealogies of all the noble houses of Europe, falconry, and riding. The court of the Low Countries had become the patron of Memling, of Hugo van der Goes, of the manuscript painters of Ghent and Bruges, of polyphonic music under Antoine Busnois; her father commissioned for her tutor the manuscript called the Hours of Mary of Burgundy with its famous frontispiece of a Burgundian lady reading, possibly Mary herself, in a window over the figure of the Virgin and Child in a Flemish church interior.

Her father's ambition was to make the Burgundian inheritance a kingdom and to obtain her hand for an emperor or an emperor's son. He negotiated betrothal after betrothal, through her childhood, with the heirs of France, England, Aragon, Savoy, and the Habsburg empire. In 1473 he agreed in principle at Trier with the Emperor Frederick III that Mary would marry Frederick's son Maximilian; Frederick wanted a royal crown for Charles in exchange, the negotiation broke down, but the betrothal remained on file. Charles meanwhile pursued his great project, the link-up of his northern and southern territories by the conquest of Lorraine, Alsace, and the lands of Switzerland. The Swiss cantons defeated him at Grandson and Murten in 1476, and on 5 January 1477 he was killed in the snow before the walls of Nancy fighting René II of Lorraine.

Mary was nineteen and alone. Her stepmother Margaret stood by her in Ghent. Louis XI of France, her father's lifelong enemy, at once invaded, claimed the duchy of Burgundy as a male fief reverting to the French crown, occupied Picardy and the Boulonnais, and pressed for her marriage to his eight-year-old Dauphin, the future Charles VIII. The Flemish towns, smelling weakness, rose against the centralised ducal administration. On 11 February 1477 Mary granted at Ghent the document called the Great Privilege, restoring to the estates their old powers of consent to taxation, to war, to the appointment of officials, and to the assembling of the States General without ducal summons. Two of her father's most loyal councillors, the chancellor Hugonet and the lord of Humbercourt, were arrested by the Ghent townspeople, tortured, and beheaded under her windows; she had pleaded in person, in mourning dress, for their lives, and could not save them. The episode marked her permanently; her later relations with her Flemish subjects were correct but never warm.

She refused the French Dauphin and accepted in his place the agreed Habsburg match. Maximilian crossed the Reich with what soldiers his father could spare and married her at Ghent on 19 August 1477 by proxy and on the same day in person. He was eighteen, a year younger than she; both were tall, blond, athletic, fond of the hunt. The marriage seems by the surviving evidence to have been an unusually happy one. She bore him a son, Philip the Handsome, on 22 July 1478, and a daughter Margaret on 10 January 1480; a third child, Francis, died in infancy.

While she was bearing his children, Maximilian was holding her inheritance together against the French. The Burgundian army, reformed and reinforced, beat off the French at Guinegate in August 1479. By the Treaty of Arras in December 1482, after her death, France kept Burgundy proper, Picardy, the Boulonnais, and the Somme towns, but Maximilian and his children retained Franche-Comté and the seventeen northern provinces — the territories that would be Habsburg for the next two centuries and become Belgium and the Netherlands.

She did not live to see the settlement. On 27 March 1482, on her way back to Bruges after a falconry party at Wijnendale near Torhout, her horse stumbled in a ditch and fell on her. The fall broke her back or her ribs; she lingered for three weeks, declined surgery on grounds of modesty, and died on 27 March 1482 at the Prinsenhof in Bruges, just over twenty-five years old. The Latin chroniclers gave her the epithet Maria Felix, Lucky Mary, with mournful irony.

She was buried in the Church of Our Lady at Bruges. The gilded bronze recumbent effigy on her tomb, commissioned by her son Philip the Handsome in 1490 and worked by Pieter de Beckere, with its long Burgundian gown and the slim hands holding a small dog, remains one of the great late-medieval funerary monuments of Europe. Beside it her son later placed the tomb of her father, repatriated from Nancy.

Her significance was that of an heiress who had married well at the right moment. She passed to her descendants the wealthiest territories in northern Europe at a time when their cities were the financial and commercial capitals of Christendom. From her son Philip the Handsome and his Castilian marriage came Charles V and the Spanish Habsburgs; from her daughter Margaret came generations of Habsburg regents in the Netherlands. The eighty years' war that her great-grandson Philip II would fight to keep the Netherlands Catholic and obedient, the Habsburg-Bourbon struggle for the Spanish succession two hundred years later, the Belgian and Dutch states that arose out of all this — all began with the hunting accident outside Bruges on a March afternoon in 1482.

The road continues to Charles the Bold.
