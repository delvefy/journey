Henry the Lion

c. 1130 – 1195, Welf duke of Saxony and Bavaria, founder of Lübeck and Munich, broken by Frederick Barbarossa in 1180

Henry was born about 1130, probably at Ravensburg in upper Swabia, the only surviving son of Henry the Proud, duke of Bavaria and of Saxony and head of the Welf family, and of Gertrude of Süpplingenburg, the only child and heiress of the Emperor Lothair III. The combination concentrated in him the inheritance of two of the three great dynasties of twelfth-century Germany: the Welf lands in Swabia and Saxony, the Billung inheritance of north Germany that had come through his maternal grandfather, and a claim on the duchy of Bavaria. He was nine when his father died at Quedlinburg in October 1139, in the middle of the civil war that had broken out the previous year when the German princes had chosen Conrad of Hohenstaufen as king over the Welf claim. King Conrad III declared the Welf duchies forfeit; Henry's mother fought a holding action against the Hohenstaufen forces and married, on the rebound, the Babenberg Henry Jasomirgott; the war ended only with the Peace of Frankfurt in 1142, which restored Saxony to the boy Henry but kept Bavaria for the Babenbergs.

He came of age in 1147, the year of the Second Crusade. Instead of accompanying his uncle Welf VI and his Hohenstaufen rivals to the Holy Land — an expedition whose disasters he could not have foreseen but which would have removed him from the German political scene at a crucial moment — he received papal permission to discharge his vow against the pagan Slavs of the lower Elbe. The Wendish Crusade of that summer and the following years, in which Henry took a leading part beside the Saxon margraves Albert the Bear and Adolph of Schauenburg, opened up the political settlement of the country east of the Elbe to the Mecklenburg and Pomeranian coasts. The chronicle of Helmold of Bosau, written in the next generation by a Saxon priest in close contact with the duke's policy, is the principal source for the systematic plantation of German peasants, Westphalian and Frisian, that followed in the conquered territories — the foundation of the German colonial settlement of the north-east that lasted into the twentieth century.

In 1152 his Hohenstaufen cousin Frederick Barbarossa succeeded Conrad III on the German throne. The new king was Henry's nearest political ally for the next twenty years. At the Diet of Regensburg in 1156, Frederick restored to him the duchy of Bavaria, which the Babenbergs surrendered in exchange for the elevation of their margraviate of Austria to a duchy with extraordinary privileges. Henry was now duke of two duchies, Saxony and Bavaria, that ran from the Baltic to the Alps, an extent of territory unmatched by any other prince of the empire. He used the dual position with notable consistency. In Bavaria, in 1158, he had the salt road from Reichenhall to Augsburg diverted across a bridge he had built over the Isar at the village of Munichen ("at the monks'"); the bishop of Freising complained but Frederick confirmed the diversion and the toll, and Munich, the small settlement at the bridge, took its place in the records of European urban history. In Saxony, in 1159, after a fire had destroyed the old trading port of Lübeck which a Holstein count had founded a decade earlier, Henry refounded the city under his direct rule, granted it the privileges that made it the chief port of the Baltic, and presided in person over the synod that organised its diocese. The bronze lion that he caused to be raised in front of his castle of Dankwarderode at Braunschweig in 1166, the first free-standing monumental sculpture cast in metal in northern Europe since antiquity, became the standard of the city and the symbol by which his contemporaries and his descendants called him.

His marriages reflected the scale of his standing. About 1148 he married Clementia of Zähringen, the daughter of the great Burgundian house, and held her for some fifteen years until political reasons in 1162 obliged him, with papal sanction on the grounds of consanguinity, to set her aside. In February 1168, at Minden cathedral, he married Matilda Plantagenet, the eleven-year-old daughter of Henry II of England and Eleanor of Aquitaine, who had been brought to Germany with a great cortege through Normandy and Flanders. The marriage attached him to the western political system as much as to the Saxon one. Through Matilda came the four children of the second marriage: Richenza, born in 1172, who married Geoffrey of Perche; Henry, born about 1173, who would become count palatine of the Rhine; Otto, born about 1175 at Argentan in Normandy, who would in the next century be elected king of the Romans and emperor; and William, born at Winchester in 1184, ancestor of the later Welf dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg and through them of the Hanoverian line of the British kings.

In the autumn of 1172 Henry and Matilda made a pilgrimage to the Holy Land, travelling through the Byzantine empire as the formal guests of Manuel I Komnenos and bringing with them at every stage of the journey the splendour appropriate to the duke of two duchies. He returned in the summer of 1173 with relics, manuscripts (including elements of what would become the famous Gospels of Henry the Lion, the great Romanesque codex now divided between Wolfenbüttel and the British Library), and a strengthened sense of his independent standing in Latin Christendom. His refusal, three years later, to come to Barbarossa's aid in the Italian campaign of 1176 followed from this growing autonomy. The interview at Chiavenna in north Italy in January 1176, where, according to the chroniclers of the next generation, Barbarossa knelt before Henry to plead for Saxon and Bavarian contingents and Henry refused, may be a literary heightening; the political fact was that Henry sent no troops, and that Barbarossa was defeated at Legnano on 29 May 1176 with an army for which his own cousin had refused to provide.

The reckoning came over the next four years. The Saxon nobility, alienated by Henry's growing aggressiveness in their own territories and in particular by his treatment of the archbishop of Magdeburg and the bishops of Halberstadt and Hildesheim, brought formal complaints against him before the king. Henry refused to appear at three successive imperial diets. On 13 January 1180, at Würzburg, he was condemned in his absence on the feudal procedure of contumacy and his ducal fiefs were declared forfeit. At the Diet of Gelnhausen in April the duchy of Saxony was divided: Westphalia was given to the archbishop of Cologne, eastern Saxony to Bernhard of Anhalt of the Ascanian line; Bavaria, in a separate sentence at Altenburg in September, was given to Otto of Wittelsbach, the founder of the Wittelsbach dynasty of Bavaria. A war of resistance, fought across the summer of 1180 and into 1181, was lost; on 25 November 1181 at the Diet of Erfurt, Henry submitted publicly to Barbarossa and accepted the forfeitures, retaining only his hereditary allodial lands in the Brunswick–Lüneburg country between the Harz and the Elbe.

He was sent into exile for three years. He spent it at the Norman court of his father-in-law Henry II of England, with periods at the English court and at Argentan, where his son Otto was raised in the household of his Plantagenet uncles. He returned to Brunswick in 1185 under safe-conduct; on the eve of Barbarossa's departure for the Third Crusade in 1188 he was sent into exile a second time, in part to remove the danger of a Welf restoration during the emperor's absence; he returned in 1189 on the news of his wife's death (Matilda had died at Brunswick on 28 June 1189, aged thirty-three) and threw himself into a brief renewed war that the new emperor Henry VI ended by treaty at Tilleda in 1190. He spent his last years at Brunswick on what was left of the family lands, completing the rebuilding of the church of St Blaise that he and Matilda had begun, where the cycle of frescos commissioned for the choir and the tomb effigy of the duke and the duchess survive. He died on 6 August 1195, aged about sixty-five, and was buried in the choir of St Blaise's beside Matilda. The Welf inheritance, broken down to the patrimonial estates around Brunswick and Lüneburg, survived the catastrophe of 1180; the family was not extinguished, and the next great chapter of its history began with his son Otto, who in 1209 entered Rome to be crowned emperor and who, until his ruin at Bouvines in 1214, came nearer than any other Welf to recovering what his father had lost.
