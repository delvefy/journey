Giovanni Boccaccio

1313 – 1375, Florentine merchant's son, author of the Decameron, the third of the three crowns of fourteenth-century Italian letters

Giovanni Boccaccio was born in 1313, the illegitimate son of Boccaccino di Chellino, an agent of the great Florentine banking house of the Bardi, by a woman whose identity has never been recovered. Florentine family law of the period was unusually liberal toward the legitimisation of merchant bastards, and the boy was raised in his father's household and given the education appropriate to a future banker: arithmetic, the rudiments of Latin, and from his teens an apprenticeship in the family business. He hated the apprenticeship. He hated the further preparation in canon law to which his father redirected him about 1330; he wanted from his teens to be a writer.

His formative years were spent in Naples, the second city of the Bardi network, where his father had been transferred about 1327 as the Bardi's resident agent at the court of King Robert I of Anjou, the Angevin king of Naples. The Neapolitan court was the most cultivated lay establishment in Italy: Robert had been the patron of Petrarch's first laureation, the librarian of Naples was the humanist Paolo da Perugia, and the city's university preserved an unbroken school of Roman law and classical letters running back into the twelfth century. Boccaccio acquired in Naples an education that no amount of merchants' grammar at Florence could have given him: he read Virgil with the philologist Cino da Pistoia, Statius with the Augustinian Dionigi da Borgo San Sepolcro who would later be Petrarch's confessor, and the Greek romances of late antiquity in the Latin translations available to him.

He wrote his first works in Naples in the late 1330s. The Filocolo, a long prose romance recasting the late antique love story of Floire and Blancheflor, was finished about 1338; the Filostrato, the verse romance of Troilus and Cressida from the Trojan War cycle, about 1340; the Teseida, the epic of Theseus, Palamon, and Arcite, about 1341. The Filostrato and the Teseida would, fifty years later, supply Geoffrey Chaucer with the materials for Troilus and Criseyde and the Knight's Tale; the form Boccaccio devised for the Teseida, the eight-line stanza of ottava rima, became the dominant stanza form of Italian narrative verse for the next three centuries.

About 1340 the Bardi bank, overextended in loans to Edward III of England, began the slide into bankruptcy that broke the Florentine banking system the following year. Boccaccino was ruined; his son returned to Florence about 1341 in straitened circumstances and lived for the next thirty years in modest economy. His writing continued unbroken. The Comedia delle Ninfe Fiorentine, the prose-and-verse pastoral, came about 1342; the Amorosa Visione, the long allegorical poem, about 1343; the Elegia di Madonna Fiammetta, an extraordinary first-person prose monologue in the voice of an abandoned woman, often considered the first psychological novel in any vernacular European language, about 1344; the Ninfale Fiesolano, a pastoral elegy in ottava rima, about 1345.

The plague of 1348 entered Florence in March; by August perhaps half the inhabitants of the city were dead, including his father and his stepmother. Out of his observation of the disaster he made the central work of his life. The Decameron, written from 1348 to 1351 and revised through the 1350s, is a frame-tale collection of one hundred prose stories, ten told each evening by seven ladies and three gentlemen who have withdrawn from plague-stricken Florence to a country villa for a fortnight. The introduction to the first day, with its slow and astonishingly precise description of the plague at Florence in the spring of 1348, is one of the great pieces of European descriptive prose. The stories that follow run the whole range of subject matter available to a vernacular prose writer of his century: tragic and comic, courtly and bawdy, devotional and bitterly anti-clerical. They were collected, on the manuscript evidence, by the women of the merchant Florentine households for whom Boccaccio wrote; the work travelled in Italian, Catalan, French, and German translation through the next century, supplied the Renaissance theatre with most of its plots, and made of vernacular Italian prose, in a single book, what Dante had made of vernacular Italian verse in the Commedia.

His mature life was largely passed in correspondence with his friend Petrarch, whom he had met at Florence in 1350 and who became his closest intellectual associate. The exchange, in over a hundred surviving letters between 1351 and Petrarch's death in 1374, was the formative dialogue of early Italian humanism. The two of them, together with the Greek scholar Leontius Pilatus whom they housed and supported in turn for several years at Boccaccio's expense, were responsible for the first complete Latin translation of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey — literally, Pilatus's hand on the Greek and Boccaccio's on the Latin — that brought Homer back into Western reading after centuries of obscurity. Petrarch persuaded Boccaccio, slowly and against his preference, to redirect his writing from the vernacular to learned Latin; the late works in Latin — the Genealogia Deorum Gentilium, a great encyclopedia of pagan mythology completed in 1360 and revised until his death, the De Mulieribus Claris of one hundred and six biographies of famous women, the De Casibus Virorum Illustrium of moral biographies of fallen great men — were the works of an old humanist who had decided to be remembered as a Latin scholar rather than as the author of the bawdy Decameron, which in his later years he half-disowned.

The last great public office of his life was the foundation in 1373 of the lectureship in Dante at the Florentine studio. Boccaccio had been the first major reader of Dante; he had transcribed copies of the Commedia in his youth and possessed the manuscript known as the Toledano which is the basis of much of the modern textual tradition. He delivered the inaugural Dante lectures from a pulpit at Santo Stefano di Badia in Florence in the autumn of 1373; his commentary on the first seventeen cantos of the Inferno, taken down by his students, was the first systematic exegesis of the poem. He fell ill in the early spring of 1374 and broke off the lectures before completing canto seventeen. He never resumed them.

Petrarch died in July 1374. Boccaccio, broken by the news, retired to his ancestral village of Certaldo south of Florence; he died there on 21 December 1375, sixty-two years old. He was buried in the parish church of Santi Jacopo e Filippo in Certaldo, where his tomb survives. The street that ran past his birthplace in the village was renamed for him in the sixteenth century and is still called Via Boccaccio.

His afterlife in European letters was immediate and enormous. The Decameron was the model of Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, of Marguerite de Navarre's Heptaméron, of Cervantes' Novelas Ejemplares, and of the entire short-story tradition of the European Renaissance. Within his own line of work, ottava rima carried him to Ariosto and Tasso. As humanist, the Genealogia remained the standard reference for classical mythology in Europe for two centuries. The "third crown of Florence" — the title beside Dante and Petrarch that the Tuscan canon gave him — understated the scale of his presence in the European vernacular tradition that he, in significant measure, had invented.
