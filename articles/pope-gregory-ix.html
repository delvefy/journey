Pope Gregory IX

c. 1170 – 1241, Ugolino dei Conti di Segni, cardinal-protector of the Franciscans, codifier of canon law, the implacable adversary of Frederick II

Ugolino dei Conti di Segni was born about 1170 at Anagni in the hill country south of Rome, of the same Conti family that had given the Church its current head, his uncle Lothar dei Conti di Segni, who in 1198 was elected Pope Innocent III. The family was an ancient one of the Latian baronage, half feudal and half Roman; it was to give the papacy four occupants between 1198 and the end of the thirteenth century. Ugolino studied at Paris and at Bologna, where the new science of canon law was being built on the foundation of Gratian's Decretum, and as a young cleric was attached to the household of his uncle. In December 1198, in the first months of his pontificate, Innocent III made him cardinal-deacon of Sant'Eustachio, and in 1206 promoted him cardinal-bishop of Ostia and Velletri, the senior of the suburbicarian sees and the office that traditionally consecrated the pope.

For the next twenty-one years, under Innocent III and then under Honorius III, Ugolino was the most active diplomat of the Roman curia. He was legate in northern Italy in 1207 and again in 1217, charged with making peace between the warring communes of Lombardy and Tuscany and with preaching the cross for the Fifth Crusade; in 1209 and 1221 he was sent to Germany; he was at the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 and a leading voice in its drafting committees. In the course of his Italian legations, probably about 1217, he encountered the small movement of mendicants that Francis of Assisi had founded only eight years before. He attached himself to Francis with a personal devotion which the lives of the saint, and his own surviving letters, record with unusual warmth: he visited Francis at the Portiuncula, lodged him in his own house at Florence, secured the papal approval of the rule of 1223, attended the chapter of the order, and protected the brothers and the sisters of San Damiano under Clare from the canonical objections of their more conventional critics. In 1220 Honorius III formally named him cardinal-protector of the order, and three years later of the Poor Ladies. The relation was not only patronal: Francis addressed letters to him as "the lord cardinal", but Ugolino's letters to Clare survive, signed "the most loving father", and after Francis's death in 1226 he kept up the protection of the order with the consistency of a personal obligation.

On 18 March 1227 Honorius III died at Rome; the next day, in a swift and unanimous conclave at the convent of Santa Sabina on the Aventine, Ugolino was elected, and took the name Gregory IX. He was about fifty-seven, on some reckonings already over seventy; in either case he had two decades of curial experience behind him, and a programme that he set in motion in the first months. The most pressing item was the unfinished business of the crusade of Frederick II. The young emperor had taken the cross in 1215, had renewed the vow at his imperial coronation in 1220, had repeatedly deferred his departure, and at the death of Honorius was still in Apulia preparing a fleet. Gregory gave him a final deadline of August 1227. Frederick sailed in September, returned to land within days with an outbreak of plague among the troops, and put off the expedition once more. On 29 September 1227 Gregory excommunicated him for breach of the crusading vow, an act for which there was canonical warrant but no recent precedent against a reigning emperor.

The struggle that followed was the central business of Gregory's pontificate and one of the formative episodes of the thirteenth-century papacy. Frederick, undeterred by the excommunication, sailed again in the summer of 1228 and proceeded to recover Jerusalem by negotiation: a ten-year treaty with the Ayyubid sultan al-Kamil ceded the city, Bethlehem, and Nazareth to the Christians, and on 18 March 1229 he crowned himself King of Jerusalem in the Holy Sepulchre. While he was in the East, Gregory authorised an invasion of his Italian kingdom by papal mercenaries under John of Brienne, who occupied much of Apulia until Frederick's return in June 1229 drove them out. The two were reconciled at the Treaty of San Germano in July 1230: Frederick was absolved, the territories restored, and the crusade declared retrospectively meritorious. The peace held, with strains, for nine years. In March 1239 Gregory excommunicated him a second time, on a longer list of charges that included the imperial occupation of Sardinia and a general accusation of heresy, and the long war for Italy began — a struggle of pamphlets, diplomacy, alliances, and intermittent campaigns that was to outlast both men and end only with the extinction of the Hohenstaufen at Tagliacozzo in 1268.

Within that frame Gregory carried through the rest of his pontificate. In 1228 he canonised Francis of Assisi at the high altar of the new basilica he was helping to build above the saint's tomb. In 1232 he canonised Anthony of Padua, in 1234 Dominic of Caleruega, in 1235 Elizabeth of Hungary — the founders and exemplars of the mendicant movement to which he had attached the prestige of the papacy. In September 1234, by the bull Rex Pacificus, he promulgated the great collection of papal decretals that his Dominican confessor Raymond of Penyafort had compiled at his command. The five books of the Decretales Gregorii IX (the Liber Extra) became, with Gratian's earlier Decretum, the foundation of medieval and early modern canon law and were taught in the law faculties for six centuries.

His bull Excommunicamus of February 1231 inaugurated, or formalised, the papal inquisition: the procedures for the trial of heretics were taken out of episcopal hands and entrusted to specially commissioned papal judges, mostly drawn from the Dominicans and Franciscans. Conrad of Marburg, a violent and ill-judged choice, was named inquisitor in Germany in 1232 and pursued his commission with such savagery that he was murdered the next year by relatives of his victims; the Dominicans Robert le Bougre in northern France and the brothers commissioned in Languedoc were more durable instruments. In 1239, on the denunciations of the convert Nicholas Donin, Gregory ordered the seizure and examination of the Hebrew Talmud, which after a trial at Paris in 1240 was condemned and partially burned in the place de Grève in June 1242, six months after his own death.

In the last year of his pontificate, with the Tuscan and Lombard wars going badly for the papacy, Gregory summoned a general council to meet at Rome at Easter 1241 to depose Frederick. The Genoese fleet bringing prelates from France, Spain, and northern Italy was intercepted by an imperial squadron under Frederick's son Enzio off the island of Giglio on 3 May 1241; some cardinals were drowned, the rest taken prisoner. The blow was decisive. Gregory, by then ninety or more, retired to his hot Roman summer and died on 22 August 1241 at the Lateran, while the imperial troops of Frederick were preparing to march on the city. He was buried in St Peter's. The conflict he had bequeathed went on, after a brief and pacific interlude under his successor Celestine IV (who reigned eighteen days), to fall on the next major pope, his great-nephew Sinibaldo Fieschi, who took the name Innocent IV and carried the war to its end.

The road continues to Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor.
