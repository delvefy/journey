Tiberius

42 BC – AD 37 · second emperor of Rome

Tiberius Caesar Augustus, born Tiberius Claudius Nero at Rome on 16 November 42 BC, was the second emperor of Rome from the death of his stepfather Augustus in AD 14 to his own death in his bed at Misenum on 16 March AD 37. He was an able and reluctant ruler, by background the senior surviving Claudian of the late Republic and by accident, after the deaths of three of Augustus's intended heirs in succession, the only viable candidate left in his stepfather's house. His reign of twenty-two and a half years, the longest of any emperor of the first century, ran from a careful and competent provincial administration in its first decade through the steady decay of his trust in Roman politics to his final retirement on the island of Capri and the long succession of treason trials under his prefect Sejanus and after Sejanus's fall. Suetonius's epitaph for him — "the gloomiest of all men" — has been answered in the modern historical literature with a complicated and partly sympathetic figure, but the contemporary verdict that the second emperor was a man broken in middle age by the things asked of him is the one that has lasted.

He was the elder son of Tiberius Claudius Nero, a senator of distinguished Claudian descent who had backed first Caesar, then Antony, then the Republic, then the wrong side in the Perusine war of 41 to 40 BC, and his young wife Livia Drusilla. When the boy was three, in late 39 BC, Octavian compelled the family to give up Livia for him; the father divorced her with full ceremony and gave the children to be raised in the new combined household, the elder boy Tiberius and his then-infant brother Drusus, together with Octavia's children and the daughter of Octavian's other marriage. Tiberius Claudius Nero the elder died in 32 BC; the boys passed permanently into the imperial house.

He grew up in the shadow of his more open and popular younger brother Drusus the Elder, on whom Augustus visibly doted, and his colder reserve was already a matter of comment in his teens. His public career began at twenty-two with the joint command with Drusus in the Alps in 15 BC, which subdued the Alpine peoples in a single summer; Horace memorialized the campaign in two of his most famous odes. He went on to Pannonia, then to Germany after Drusus's death in 9 BC, and finished the conquest of the lower Danube lands and the integration of the Rhine frontier. He was given the consulship for the second time in 7 BC and the tribunician power for five years in 6 BC.

In 11 BC Augustus required him, against his open and bitter resistance, to divorce his much-loved wife Vipsania Agrippina, the daughter of Agrippa, and marry the recently-widowed Julia, then about twenty-eight, in order to position himself as a possible regent for Julia's young sons by Agrippa. He did so. He never forgave the requirement. The famous anecdote of his accidental sight of Vipsania in the street after the divorce, after which he wept and asked to be sure he never saw her again, comes from Suetonius and stands, in the ancient picture of Tiberius, as the moment when something in him began to break.

The marriage to Julia was unhappy, produced one infant son who died, and ended for practical purposes in 6 BC when Tiberius, at the height of his career and apparently in despair, applied to Augustus for permission to retire from public life and travel privately in the East. Augustus refused; Tiberius left anyway, taking a single freedman with him, and lived at Rhodes for the next seven years, an exile in fact if not in name, while his career stopped dead. He was forty-three when he returned to Rome in AD 2, formally divorced from Julia by then, and forty-five when the deaths in quick succession of Augustus's grandsons Lucius and Gaius Caesar in AD 2 and AD 4 left him the only remaining adult male of the imperial house. Augustus adopted him in AD 4, requiring him in turn to adopt his nephew Germanicus as a condition.

He spent the next ten years in continuous military command on the northern frontier, putting down the great Pannonian revolt of AD 6 to 9 and stabilizing the Rhine after the loss of the three legions of Varus in the Teutoburg Forest in AD 9. He was given the tribunician power in AD 13 and the supreme imperium over the provinces jointly with Augustus, the constitutional preparation for the succession. Augustus died at Nola on 19 August AD 14. Tiberius, then fifty-six, became emperor.

The first decade of his reign was, by every account, an able administration. He kept the imperial finances solvent, refused divine honors of the kind that the Senate kept attempting to offer him, conducted the provinces carefully and with attention to good governors, and treated the Senate, at first, as the real partner of the new dispensation. He recalled Germanicus from Germany in AD 16, settled the eastern frontier through him, and quietly mourned him when he died at Antioch in AD 19. The trial of Piso for Germanicus's death — at which Tiberius's own role was indirectly questioned — was uncomfortable but quietly closed. His own son Drusus the Younger died in AD 23 at thirty-six, of what seemed an illness but was later revealed to have been poisoning by Sejanus and Livilla; Tiberius mourned him publicly and changed in private.

From AD 26 he withdrew from Rome to the island of Capri off the bay of Naples and did not return for the remaining eleven years of his life. The administration of the city he left to the praetorian prefect Sejanus, whose power over Rome and over access to the emperor became, by AD 30, the effective imperial authority. The treason trials began to multiply; the Senate became a court of denunciation. In October of AD 31 his sister-in-law Antonia the Younger sent the long letter to Capri exposing Sejanus's conspiracy with Livilla and his designs on the succession; Tiberius, through the new prefect Macro, removed Sejanus in a single Senate meeting and had him and his children put to death. The trials, far from stopping, accelerated, and the discovery from Sejanus's discarded wife Apicata's suicide note that Drusus the Younger had been poisoned eight years earlier sent the emperor into a still darker isolation.

He lived another five and a half years at Capri, in the company of the philosopher Thrasyllus and a small staff, refusing to return to Rome, refusing to receive most visitors, dying slowly. He died on 16 March AD 37 at Misenum, on a brief journey back toward the city, in his seventy-ninth year. Tacitus reports that Macro, finding him not quite dead, ordered him smothered with pillows. The Senate was preparing public thanksgivings before the news had reached the city. His grandnephew Caligula, the son of Germanicus and his last clear designated heir, was acclaimed in his place; his own grandson Tiberius Gemellus, named as joint heir in his will, was killed by Caligula within six months.

He had been, by every contemporary measure, the most capable military man of his generation and one of the most capable administrators of the Roman political class. He had been required to do, in succession, every thing he most disliked: divorce a beloved wife, marry an unsuited and notorious one, accept the principate against his preference, watch his natural son murdered by his most trusted subordinate, and govern in retirement from an island the city that he had stopped trusting. The Roman political class that remembered him after Caligula was less surprised by his withdrawal than by the fact that he had taken on the principate at all. The reign closed the period in which the principate could be conducted, in any sense, as a partnership with the Senate; it opened the period in which the personal character of the emperor, for good or ill, was the whole of the constitution.
