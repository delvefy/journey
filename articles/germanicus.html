Germanicus

15 BC – AD 19 · Roman general, prince of the Julio-Claudian house, lost heir of the empire

Germanicus Julius Caesar, the elder son of Drusus the Elder and Antonia the Younger, was the most popular Roman of his generation and the heir-presumptive to the principate of his uncle Tiberius when he died at Antioch in the autumn of AD 19, in circumstances that left half the Roman world convinced he had been poisoned. He was thirty-three years old. His memory survived in the Roman political imagination for the next century as the great might-have-been of the Julio-Claudian house — the bright, brave, popular prince who would have been emperor; whose death gave the empire instead to his strange, secretive uncle and, in time, to his own brilliantly disturbed eldest son Caligula and to his nephew Nero.

He was born in 15 BC, his father's first son, six years before Drusus the Elder died at twenty-nine on a road in Germany. He was raised in his mother Antonia's household at Rome, with his younger brother Claudius and his sister Livilla, under the eye of his grandmother Livia and his great-uncle Augustus. Augustus, in AD 4, settling the succession after the deaths of the young Gaius and Lucius Caesar, adopted Tiberius and required Tiberius in turn to adopt Germanicus, his elder brother's son, as a condition. The arrangement gave the principate, in two generations' time, to the boy from his beloved Drusus's house, leapfrogging Tiberius's own son.

His public career began in his teens. He was sent to put down a Pannonian and Dalmatian rising in AD 7 to 9, served beside Tiberius on the Rhine after the Varus disaster, and in AD 13, at the age of twenty-eight, was given supreme command of the eight legions of the German frontier — the largest field army in the empire. The next two years were spent on a series of campaigns east of the Rhine that recovered, in part, the standards lost with Varus in the Teutoburg Forest, visited the site of the disaster to bury the bones still lying where the legions had fallen, defeated the Cherusci chieftain Arminius in pitched battles at Idistaviso and the Angrivarian wall, and threatened to push the frontier back toward the Elbe. Tiberius recalled him in AD 16 over his protests; the recall ended the German offensive permanently, and the Rhine remained the frontier for the rest of the empire's history.

He was given a triumph at Rome in May of AD 17 — the first full triumph for an imperial prince in many years — and rode through the streets with his five children and his wife Agrippina the Elder in the chariot beside him. The crowds, by Tacitus's account, found in him the picture of the young Roman that Augustus had carefully cultivated as a public image: brave, mild, fluent in Greek as well as Latin, a writer of comedies, a popular orator who pleaded cases in the Forum, an open and accessible commander, a faithful husband. The contrast with the dark, withdrawn Tiberius was, for the Roman political class, painful and unavoidable.

In the same year Tiberius sent him east, with extraordinary powers covering all the eastern provinces, to settle a series of crises around Cappadocia, Armenia, and the Parthian frontier. He installed a Roman client on the throne of Armenia, reorganized Cappadocia and Commagene as Roman provinces, and was on the brink of resolving the Parthian question peacefully when he quarrelled violently with the new governor of Syria, Gnaeus Calpurnius Piso, whom Tiberius had appointed alongside him on what looked very much like a watching brief. The quarrel turned ugly; Germanicus formally renounced his friendship with Piso and ordered him out of the province; Piso left, but only as far as the islands, and the two were still corresponding in mutual hostility when Germanicus fell ill at Antioch.

The illness was sudden and went on for weeks. Germanicus believed he was being poisoned by Piso and his wife Plancina; the household searched the floors and walls of the residence and found, by Tacitus's report, bones of corpses, tablets of curses, leaden plates with the name of Germanicus on them, and the marks of a magician's work. Germanicus died on the tenth of October AD 19, after sending his friends and family the dying message that they were to avenge him on Piso. He was thirty-three.

His body was burned at Antioch and his ashes carried home by his wife Agrippina the Elder, in a long, mournful winter journey across Asia Minor and through Italy that drew enormous crowds wherever the cortege passed. They were laid in the mausoleum of Augustus at Rome. Piso was put on trial before the Senate the following year on the charge of murder by poisoning; he died before the verdict, of a cut throat made to look like suicide, and Tiberius let the trial be closed without further pursuit of the question. The Roman public concluded that Tiberius had ordered the killing; modern historians are divided, and the case can no longer be settled.

He left six surviving children: the eldest sons Nero Julius Caesar and Drusus Caesar, who would be destroyed in the political purges of Tiberius's late reign at the hands of the praetorian prefect Sejanus; the youngest, Gaius, who had been raised in his father's camp by the Rhine soldiers, who had nicknamed him Caligula, "little boots," from the miniature soldiers' sandals his mother dressed him in, and who would survive to become emperor; and three daughters, Agrippina the Younger, Drusilla, and Julia Livilla, of whom Agrippina the Younger would, in her own turn, be the mother of Nero, the wife and poisoner of the emperor Claudius, and the most powerful woman of her age.

His memory was carried, by his wife in her exile and her granddaughters in their courts and his son in his army, through the generation that followed. The first act of his son Caligula on becoming emperor in AD 37 was a public retrieval of the ashes of his mother and brothers from their islands of exile and a great public funeral procession to the mausoleum; the second was a calculated political appeal to the memory of Germanicus, which the soldiers and the urban crowds of Rome had not forgotten. Germanicus himself never reached the throne. His brother Claudius, his son Caligula, and his grandson Nero each in turn did.
