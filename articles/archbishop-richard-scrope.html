Archbishop Richard Scrope

c. 1350 – 1405, Archbishop of York, executed by Henry IV, the first English archbishop killed by royal warrant since Becket

Richard Scrope was born about 1350, the third son of Henry, first Baron Scrope of Masham, and Joan, daughter of Sir William Roos of Helmsley. The Scropes were a Yorkshire family of recent prominence: Richard's father had served Edward III as Steward of the Household and as Treasurer of England, and his elder cousin Richard, first Baron Scrope of Bolton, served the same king as Chancellor. The family had risen in the king's service over two generations and was, by the time of Richard's birth, one of the wealthier of the secondary northern baronages. As a third son he was placed in the church; he matriculated at Cambridge in the early 1370s, took a degree in canon law at Oxford about 1375, and a doctorate at Cambridge in 1387.

His ecclesiastical career advanced with his family's standing. He was an official of Ely diocese at twenty-five, dean of Chichester at twenty-six, an auditor of the Rota at Avignon during the long Western Schism. Pope Urban VI consecrated him Bishop of Coventry and Lichfield in 1386. He was translated to York — the second see of England — in 1398, on the death of Robert Waldby, in a translation procured at the Roman court of Boniface IX with the personal support of Richard II. He was consecrated on 15 August 1398 and entered York in the autumn.

His political position in the revolution of 1399 was that of a churchman who, like most of his ecclesiastical contemporaries, accepted the de facto power of the moment. He took part in the embassy that received Richard II's deposition at the Tower on 29 September; he sat on the parliamentary commission that drew up the thirty-three articles of deposition; he crowned Henry IV at Westminster on 13 October. The crowning was, on its face, a clear endorsement of the new regime. His later quarrel with Henry IV grew slowly out of issues that, in 1399, no one foresaw.

The first phase of the quarrel was clerical: Henry IV's parliament of 1404, in an attempt to address the chronic shortage of revenue, threatened the temporal possessions of the English church on what was called the "Lollard knight" programme — the formal dispute had been theological, between the orthodox bishops and the followers of Wycliffe, but the immediate political question was the same as the one that had nearly broken the previous reign, whether the wealth of the church could be confiscated to fund the wars. Scrope, with Archbishop Thomas Arundel of Canterbury, mounted vigorous defence of ecclesiastical property; the parliament eventually backed down. The episode left him on record as a defender of the rights of the church against the crown.

The second phase was political and personal. The Percys, the great northern family that had brought Henry IV to power and had since broken with him, were Scrope's territorial neighbours; the archbishop's diocese, with its rich revenues and its substantial palatine privileges, lay in the same country as the Percy lordships. After Hotspur's death at Shrewsbury in 1403, the Percys' remaining patriarch — Henry Percy, first Earl of Northumberland — had been pardoned but not restored to office. By the spring of 1405 he had taken up again with the exiled Edmund Mortimer of Pilleth, with Owain Glyndŵr in Wales, and with Thomas Mowbray, the young earl Marshal whose father had been the king's quarrel-partner Bolingbroke at Coventry in 1398. The plan was a tripartite division of the kingdom — Glyndŵr to take Wales and the western shires, Mortimer to take the south, the earl to take the north — that came to be called the Tripartite Indenture.

Scrope's part in the conspiracy is one of the most disputed in English ecclesiastical history. By his own later defence, his programme was political reform, not regime change: a manifesto in fifteen articles, posted on the doors of York Minster on 28 May 1405, called for the relief of taxation, for the reform of justice, for the restoration of the rights of parliament, and made no mention of deposing the king. The manifesto is the earliest formal articulation of an English political reform programme by an archbishop; the substance, in retrospect, was constitutional rather than treasonable. His enemies, however — and his judges — treated the manifesto as the cover for the Tripartite scheme, in which they had identified the archbishop as a principal mover. The truth, in all likelihood, lies between: Scrope is unlikely to have known of the full Mortimer-Glyndŵr indenture, but he was certainly in contact with Northumberland, and the timing of his rising and Northumberland's was unmistakably co-ordinated.

The rising itself was a fiasco. Scrope and Mowbray gathered perhaps eight thousand citizens of York and of the Yorkshire wapentakes at Shipton Moor north of the city on 27 May 1405. The earl of Northumberland's army from the north never appeared. A royal force under Ralph Neville, first Earl of Westmorland and brother-in-law of Northumberland, approached, opened parley with the two leaders, and persuaded them, on what the rebels took to be a promise of grievances redressed, to dismiss their army. The army dismissed, Westmorland arrested them.

Henry IV arrived at York on 3 June. He had Scrope and Mowbray tried by a commission whose presiding judge, Sir William Gascoigne, refused to pass a sentence of death on a churchman; the king accordingly procured Sir William Fulthorpe as a substitute. On 8 June 1405 Scrope and Mowbray were brought out to a field of barley outside the walls of Bishopthorpe Palace. The archbishop was in his pontificals. He turned to the crowd, said the seven psalms, and asked the executioner to give him five strokes for the five wounds of Christ. He was beheaded. He was the first English archbishop executed by royal warrant since the murder of Thomas Becket at Canterbury in 1170.

The reaction was immediate. The Pope Innocent VII placed Henry IV and his judges under excommunication. The king himself, from that summer onward, contracted the leprous or eczematous skin disease that disfigured him for the rest of his life, and on which contemporary opinion settled with general satisfaction as the just punishment of the crime. A cult of Scrope at his tomb in York Minster grew rapidly and was reported, in formal petitions to successive archbishops over the next century, to have produced miracles; the cult was never officially recognised by Rome but was tacitly tolerated by the English crown. The cult survived the Reformation in modified form; Scrope's tomb in the minster, much restored, is still shown.

His role in the English political imagination has been that of the saintly resister of royal tyranny — the figure who, with Thomas Becket and Thomas More, gave the English church its three great executed martyrs at the hands of the English crown. Of the three he is the least famous because Henry IV's reign is the least famous; but in 1405 his death rang harder through the country than either of the others. The conscience of Henry IV, which had survived the deposition and murder of Richard II without much trouble, did not survive the killing of Archbishop Scrope; it is on Henry IV's record that the dynasty he founded ruled, in its turn, for sixty-two years, and that for the rest of his life he believed his disease to be the punishment for what he had done that morning outside Bishopthorpe.
