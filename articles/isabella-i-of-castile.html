Isabella I of Castile

1451 – 1504, Queen of Castile and León, conqueror of Granada, patroness of Columbus, founder of Catholic Spain

Isabella was born on 22 April 1451 at Madrigal de las Altas Torres in old Castile, the daughter of John II of Castile by his second wife, Isabella of Portugal. Her father died when she was three, and the throne passed to her elder half-brother Henry IV. Her childhood was spent in the small dower town of Arévalo with her widowed and mentally fragile mother and her younger brother Alfonso, in the genteel poverty of a forgotten infanta. At about ten she was summoned to court at Segovia, partly as an ornament, partly as a hostage against rebellion. Her education there was unsystematic but pious; she was taught to read and write in Castilian by Beatriz Galindo, learned some Latin only as an adult, and absorbed from her confessors a hard, ascetic devotion that never left her.

The reign of Henry IV, called the Impotent, was a collapse of royal authority. The grandees in 1465 deposed him in effigy at Ávila and proclaimed her brother Alfonso king in his place; when Alfonso died in 1468, possibly of plague, the rebels turned to Isabella. She refused the crown while her brother lived but accepted recognition as Princess of Asturias and heir presumptive by the Treaty of the Bulls of Guisando in September 1468. The condition was that she should marry where the king wished. The king wished her to marry Afonso V of Portugal or the Duke of Berry; she had decided on Ferdinand, the heir to the crown of Aragon. With the help of the archbishop of Toledo, Ferdinand crossed Castile in disguise, met her at Valladolid, and married her there on 19 October 1469. The dispensation said to authorise the marriage between cousins was a forgery; a real one was obtained later.

Henry IV repudiated her in fury and named instead his disputed daughter Juana, called by Isabella's partisans la Beltraneja after her supposed natural father. When Henry died in December 1474, Isabella had herself proclaimed queen in Segovia the next day. The War of the Castilian Succession followed: Afonso V invaded in support of Juana, supported by France; Ferdinand defeated him at the Battle of Toro on 1 March 1476, a confused field which she insisted on calling a victory and which broke the rebel cause. By the Treaty of Alcáçovas of 1479 Portugal acknowledged her as queen; the same year Ferdinand inherited Aragon, and the two crowns — though not yet a single state — were joined in their persons.

What followed was a quarter-century of furious work. Castile and Aragon had been ungovernable; she set out to govern them. She and Ferdinand toured the country incessantly, holding court personally in town after town, restoring royal justice, breaking the great nobility's private wars and pulling down their illegal castles. They expanded the brotherhoods of cities (the Hermandad) into a kind of national police; they took back the masterships of the three military orders into the crown; they professionalised the royal council and the secretariat. The cost of this energy was paid by everyone. In 1478 they obtained from Pope Sixtus IV a bull authorising the Spanish Inquisition, distinct from and harsher than the medieval papal one and answerable to the crown; in 1480 the first tribunal sat at Seville under Tomás de Torquemada, and over the next twenty years thousands of converted Jews and Muslims were tried, hundreds burned, and a regime of religious surveillance laid over Spanish society that would last for centuries.

From 1482 to 1492 they prosecuted the conquest of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in the peninsula. The war was a series of sieges — Alhama, Ronda, Loja, Málaga, Baza, and at last Granada itself — conducted in person by Ferdinand in the field while Isabella organised supplies, artillery, finance, and the propaganda of a war preached as a crusade. On 2 January 1492 the Emir Muhammad XII handed over the keys of the Alhambra. The Catholic Monarchs entered the city in triumph; the conquest was greeted in Christendom as the recovery of the lost lands of the Visigoths.

The year 1492 was the hinge of her reign. On 31 March she signed at Granada the Alhambra Decree, ordering the expulsion of all unconverted Jews from Castile and Aragon within four months. Perhaps eighty thousand, perhaps more, were driven out, the longest-established Jewish community in Western Europe destroyed. In April she signed the capitulations with the Genoese mariner Christopher Columbus, after years of refusing him, for an Atlantic voyage west to the Indies. He sailed from Palos on 3 August and returned in March 1493 with parrots, gold ornaments, captured Taíno, and the news that islands had been found. The papal bulls of Alexander VI in 1493 and the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 with Portugal divided the new world between the two crowns. Isabella took the Indies into the patrimony of Castile, ordering that the inhabitants be treated as her subjects and forbidding their enslavement — an instruction often violated and the source of the long arguments in the next century, by Las Casas and others, over what Spanish rule in America could legitimately be.

She and Ferdinand placed their children at the great courts of Europe. Their eldest, Isabella, married first the heir and then the king of Portugal and died in childbirth in 1498. Their only son John, the long-awaited Prince of Asturias, died at nineteen in 1497. Their second daughter Joanna was married to Philip the Handsome of Burgundy, heir to the Habsburg lands, and through her the crowns of Spain would in time pass to Charles V. Maria married the king of Portugal after her sister's death. The youngest, Catherine, was sent to England in 1501.

Her last years were heavy. The succession was uncertain: with John and the elder Isabella dead, Joanna was heiress, but Joanna was unstable and her husband foreign. Isabella drew up an austere testament in October 1504, leaving Castile to Joanna with Ferdinand as regent in case of necessity, and forbidding the harsh treatment of her Indian subjects. She died at Medina del Campo on 26 November 1504, in her own words wishing to be remembered as la Católica, the Catholic. Pope Alexander VI had granted her and Ferdinand that title in 1496. She was buried at the Alhambra of Granada, beneath the city she had conquered.

Her legacy was Spain itself. The united monarchy, the Inquisition, the empire in the Indies, the expulsion of the Jews and later of the Moriscos, the Counter-Reformation devotion that would carry Spanish arms from Naples to the Netherlands — all of it descended from her reign. Modern historians have judged her by every available measure, with results that swing between hagiography and indictment. What is not in dispute is that she remade her kingdom and that no European monarch of the late fifteenth century left behind a more durable structure of state.
