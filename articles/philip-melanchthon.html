Philip Melanchthon

1497 – 1560, German Reformer, humanist, and architect of Protestant education

Philip Melanchthon was born on 16 February 1497 at Bretten in the Lower Palatinate, the son of a master armourer at the electoral court. His given name was Philipp Schwarzerdt — “black earth” in Swabian German — and he kept it until his great-uncle Johann Reuchlin, the foremost Christian Hebraist of the age and one of the principal humanists in Germany, took an interest in his education and offered him, on the model of Reuchlin's own Greek pseudonym, the calque “Melanchthon” from the Greek for the same earth-colour. The new name stuck. Reuchlin shaped his early studies, sent him in 1509 to Heidelberg and in 1512 to Tübingen, and made him in effect his protégé during the years of the great Reuchlin Affair, the protracted scholarly and judicial quarrel between Reuchlin and the Cologne Dominican Johannes Pfefferkorn over the proposed suppression of Jewish books, which polarised German humanism around 1510–1520.

In August 1518 Melanchthon was appointed professor of Greek at the new University of Wittenberg in Saxony at the age of twenty-one, on Reuchlin's strong recommendation. His inaugural lecture, De corrigendis adolescentiae studiis (“On Reforming the Studies of Youth”), set out a humanist programme of classical and biblical languages that he would pursue for the rest of his life. Within weeks he had been drawn into the orbit of Martin Luther, who was fourteen years his senior and at the height of his early Reformation activity. The friendship between the older monk and the younger humanist was immediate, profound, and lifelong. They lived in the same town, lectured in the same university, and worked in such close intellectual partnership that the German Reformation can scarcely be told as the work of either one alone.

Melanchthon's first major contribution was the Loci Communes of 1521, the first systematic Protestant theology, written while Luther was confined at the Wartburg. The book organised the new evangelical doctrine under common-place headings — sin, law, grace, justification, the sacraments — in a way that became the model for Protestant systematic theology for the next two centuries. Luther was so taken with it that he repeatedly said it ought to be reckoned among the canonical books. Melanchthon revised and expanded it through the rest of his life; the late editions reflect not only the consolidation of Lutheran doctrine but also Melanchthon's own gradual softening on certain hard Lutheran positions, particularly on the freedom of the will in the act of conversion, a softening that would later cause him much grief from his Gnesio-Lutheran critics.

His public role grew steadily. He represented Luther — who as an outlaw could not safely attend imperial assemblies — at the Diet of Augsburg in 1530, and there drafted the Augsburg Confession, the foundational confessional document of the Lutheran churches. The Confession is Melanchthonian in temper as well as authorship: irenic where possible, precise where necessary, deliberately framed in a way that closed as few doors to reunion with the Catholic Church as Lutheran principle could allow. His Apology of the Augsburg Confession the following year defended it against the Catholic Confutation.

He took part in nearly every theological colloquy of the next two decades — Marburg in 1529, where he failed with Luther to reach agreement with Zwingli on the eucharist; Worms and Regensburg in 1540–1541, where he came closer than any Lutheran of his generation to a compromise with Catholic moderates such as Cardinal Contarini and Johann Eck before the talks collapsed; Worms again in 1557. In every encounter he was the spokesman the Protestant side put forward when conciliation was wanted and Luther's bluntness was not.

He became, in parallel, the great organiser of Protestant German education. By the end of his life he was called the Praeceptor Germaniae, the “preceptor of Germany,” for the curricular reforms he had imposed on universities — Wittenberg above all, but also Tübingen, Leipzig, Heidelberg, Rostock, Königsberg, and others — and for the network of Latin gymnasia he had helped establish across Protestant Germany. The university curriculum he designed — Greek and Hebrew, the trivium and quadrivium, philosophy on Aristotelian lines, theology built on the biblical languages — became the standard of Protestant higher education and shaped the formation of German intellectual life through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. He wrote textbooks himself for Greek and Latin grammar, dialectic, rhetoric, physics, and ethics, most of which remained in use for generations.

The death of Luther in February 1546 left him the unwilling leader of the Lutheran movement at the worst possible moment. The Schmalkaldic War of 1546–1547 broke the political alliance of the Protestant princes; the emperor Charles V imposed the Augsburg Interim in 1548, requiring Protestant territories to readmit certain Catholic practices pending a general council. Melanchthon, after long agonised consultation, supported the modified Leipzig Interim that the Saxon elector Maurice negotiated, on the grounds that the practices in question were adiaphora — things indifferent — that could be tolerated for the sake of preserving the Reformation's institutional survival. The Gnesio-Lutheran party — “genuine Lutherans,” led by Matthias Flacius Illyricus — denounced him as a traitor to the cause and to Luther's memory. The quarrel embittered the last twelve years of his life and the next generation of German Lutheranism.

In private life he was a temperate, scholarly, and depressive man. He married Katharina Krapp, daughter of a Wittenberg burgomaster, in 1520 at Luther's urging — he had not wanted to marry — and the marriage was warm; he was a devoted father, and the deaths of two of his children in their first years marked him deeply. He kept up an enormous learned correspondence with humanists across Europe, including, until the break of 1525, Erasmus of Rotterdam, whose Latin he admired and whose views on the will he ended up closer to than Luther ever was.

His last years were melancholy. The Lutheran movement he had helped to define was splintering between his Philippist followers and the Gnesio-Lutherans; the political settlement of Augsburg in 1555 had given Lutheran territories legal recognition in the Empire but left the doctrinal quarrels open; the Calvinist current, increasingly distinct from Lutheran Saxony, was pulling reformed Christianity in directions he did not entirely follow. He died at Wittenberg on 19 April 1560, his final note, found on his desk, listing reasons not to fear death — among them that he would be “delivered from the rabies of the theologians.” He was buried in the castle church beside Luther.

The verdict on him in Lutheran tradition has fluctuated. The Formula of Concord of 1577 rejected many of his late doctrinal accommodations; nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship has steadily restored his stature as the architect of the institutions in which the Reformation actually lived. He was, in the words of one modern historian, the man without whom Luther would have remained a German prophet rather than the founder of a church.
