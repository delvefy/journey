Sigismund Báthory

1572 – 1613, Prince of Transylvania

Sigismund Báthory was born in 1572 at Várad (modern Oradea), into the most powerful family in Transylvania and, by the time of his birth, the most consequential in east-central Europe. His father Christopher Báthory was voivode of Transylvania; his uncle Stephen Báthory was prince of Transylvania and would, four years later, be elected king of Poland. When Christopher died in 1581 the nine-year-old Sigismund was made prince in his stead, with a regency council that included his formidable uncle's chancery and, after Stephen's death in 1586, was reconstituted under local magnates. He took personal rule in 1588 at the age of about sixteen, having been schooled by Jesuits and shaped, decisively, by the cosmopolitan court his uncle had built.

The Transylvania he inherited was an Ottoman tributary, formally subject to Istanbul since the disaster of Mohács in 1526, but with considerable internal autonomy. The Báthory princes had used that autonomy to make Alba Iulia a Catholic and humanist centre in a country whose nobility and townspeople were largely Lutheran, Calvinist, or Unitarian. Sigismund inherited the religious tensions of that policy along with the political ones. His own court was Italianate in taste — full of musicians, mathematicians, and Jesuit confessors — and he gave his patronage early and lavishly to composers in the Italian school. From the first he was also drawn westward in his politics, toward the Habsburgs and Rome, and away from the Porte; the Long Turkish War, beginning in 1593, gave him his opportunity to act on that inclination.

In 1594 he committed Transylvania to the Holy League being knit together by Pope Clement VIII and Emperor Rudolf II. The decision was not popular at home; many of his councillors preferred the cheap submission of tribute to the certain ruin of war, and Sigismund had a number of them executed in late 1594 — the so-called "bloody Diet" at Cluj — eliminating his principal opposition by the simple expedient of beheading it. From that point the war was inseparable from his throne. He concluded a treaty with Rudolf in 1595 and married the emperor's niece, Maria Christina of Habsburg, that same year; the marriage was, by all accounts, never consummated and would become a long political and personal embarrassment to both courts. He took the field in person, won a victory at Giurgiu in October 1595 in concert with the Wallachian voivode Michael the Brave and the Moldavian Ştefan Răzvan, and seemed for a moment to be the rising star of Christian arms in the east.

What followed was a sequence of abdications and returns that has fascinated and bewildered historians ever since. Sigismund renounced his throne for the first time in early 1597, handed it back to Rudolf in exchange for the duchy of Oppeln-Ratibor in Silesia, then changed his mind and resumed it; abdicated again in 1598; came back in 1599 only to abdicate a third time, this time in favour of his cousin Andrew Báthory, a Cardinal of the Catholic Church who promptly sought to make peace with the Ottomans. Each turn of the cycle compounded the resentment of the Transylvanian Diet, the irritation of the Habsburgs, and the bewilderment of the Porte. The cycle ended only when Michael the Brave, by then ostensibly Sigismund's vassal, invaded Transylvania, killed Andrew at Șelimbăr, and made himself master of the country in Rudolf's name. The Báthory dynasty's three-quarter century in Transylvania was effectively over.

Sigismund tried once more, with Ottoman backing this time, to recover what he had given up. After Michael was assassinated in 1601 and a confused interlude of Habsburg, Ottoman, and local rivalry followed, Sigismund returned briefly in 1601–1602 and was acknowledged again as prince by parts of the country. The Habsburg general Giorgio Basta defeated his forces; Sigismund signed a final renunciation at Lipova in July 1602 and withdrew into Bohemia, where Rudolf II granted him a pension, restored to him the Silesian duchy of Oppeln-Ratibor that he had earlier surrendered, and gave him an honourable exile.

He lived eleven more years in Prague and on his Silesian estate, an avid musician (he composed himself, indifferently, and patronised Italian composers more successfully) and a celibate ascetic, having his marriage to Maria Christina formally annulled in 1599 on the still-disputed grounds of his impotence or, as some at the time believed, his vow of chastity. She returned to the Habsburg court and lived out her life in a convent. He died at Prague on 27 March 1613, the last male of his branch of the Báthory line, and was buried there.

The verdict on his reign was hard at the time and has remained hard since. He had carried Transylvania into a war it could not afford; he had handed it serially to the Habsburgs and then watched the Habsburgs' allies kill his own cousin to take it; he had broken the dynasty his uncle Stephen had built. Yet the alternative — quiet vassalage to a declining Porte — would, in its own way, have been a kind of submission, and Sigismund's contemporaries who saw him at Giurgiu in 1595 did not see only a future failure. He was a man of impressive education and indifferent nerve, formed by an extraordinary family for a role he could not finally inhabit, and his life remains one of the most studied case-studies of early modern princely psychology in eastern Europe.
