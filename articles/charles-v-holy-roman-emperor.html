Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor

1500 – 1558, ruler of the Holy Roman Empire, Spain, the Netherlands, Naples, and the new world in the Americas

Charles was born at Ghent on 24 February 1500, the eldest son of Philip the Handsome of Habsburg and Joanna of Castile. Through his four grandparents he gathered into one body the inheritances of four dynasties: Burgundy from his grandmother Mary, the Habsburg lands from his grandfather Maximilian I, Castile and the Indies from his grandmother Isabella the Catholic, Aragon and its Italian kingdoms from his grandfather Ferdinand. His father died suddenly in 1506; his mother withdrew, in grief and increasing instability, to confinement at Tordesillas for the rest of her long life. Charles was brought up in the Netherlands by his aunt Margaret of Austria, with the dour Burgundian nobleman William of Croÿ as his governor and the future Pope Adrian VI as his tutor. He grew up speaking French and Flemish, learned Spanish only as an adult, and was never quite fluent in German.

At fifteen he was declared of age and took over the government of the Netherlands. In 1516, on Ferdinand's death, he became king of Castile and Aragon and ruler of the Indies; his arrival in Spain in 1517 with a Flemish entourage who took the great offices for themselves was so resented that the Castilian towns rose in 1520 in the revolt of the Comuneros, beaten down by his loyal nobility at Villalar in April 1521. In 1519 his grandfather Maximilian died, and Charles, at the age of nineteen, was elected Holy Roman Emperor in competition with Francis I of France and Henry VIII of England. The election cost him an immense bribe to the seven electors, financed by the Fugger bank of Augsburg, and committed him to a German policy he had not asked for.

His reign almost at once met its central problem in the form of an Augustinian friar. Charles arrived in Germany in 1521 and summoned Martin Luther to appear before the Diet of Worms. The young emperor presided in person, listened to the friar's refusal to recant, and a few weeks later issued the Edict of Worms outlawing him. The German princes refused to enforce it. The next thirty years of imperial politics in Germany would be a working out of what could not be settled at Worms. Charles believed to his bones in the unity of Latin Christendom under emperor and pope and could never quite credit that it was over. He attempted three times to call a general council that would reform the church and reunite it — he succeeded only in 1545, with the Council of Trent, and Trent then defined Catholicism in a way that closed off reunion forever.

Outside Germany he fought four wars with Francis I of France, the rival who in 1519 had wanted the Empire for himself and who from then on contested with him for control of Italy. At Pavia, on 24 February 1525 — Charles's twenty-fifth birthday — the imperial army under the marquis of Pescara destroyed the French army and took Francis prisoner. Francis was held in Madrid for a year, swore to the harsh Treaty of Madrid, and on release at once repudiated it, forming with Pope Clement VII the League of Cognac against the emperor. The unpaid imperial army marched on Rome and, in May 1527, took and sacked the city for eight days, an event that shocked Christendom and broke the political confidence of the Renaissance papacy. The pope, in the end, crowned him emperor at Bologna in 1530, the last imperial coronation by a pope; he refused, however, to grant Henry VIII the annulment for which Henry then broke with Rome.

The eastern frontier was held against Suleiman the Magnificent. The Ottomans took Belgrade in 1521 and Buda in 1526, killing Charles's brother-in-law Louis II of Hungary at Mohács and beginning a century and a half of Habsburg-Ottoman rivalry over the Hungarian throne. In 1529 Suleiman besieged Vienna and was repulsed by the city's hastily reinforced garrison; in 1535 Charles personally led a fleet to Tunis and stormed the corsair stronghold of Khair-ed-Din Barbarossa; an attack on Algiers in 1541 ended in disaster.

Across the Atlantic his reign coincided with the conquest of the great American empires he could not have planned. Hernán Cortés took Tenochtitlan in 1521; Francisco Pizarro took Cuzco in 1533. The silver of Potosí, discovered in 1545, began to flow into his treasury at the moment his European debts were becoming unmanageable. He convened in 1542 the Junta de Valladolid which produced the New Laws restricting the encomienda, and in 1550 the famous debate between Las Casas and Sepúlveda over the rights of the Indians — signs of a conscience attempting to keep up with an empire it had not asked for and could not entirely control.

In the late 1540s, with Francis I dead and the Council of Trent in session, he made his last great effort to settle Germany. The Schmalkaldic League of Protestant princes resisted; he defeated them at Mühlberg on 24 April 1547, capturing the Elector of Saxony, in a battle painted in his triumph by Titian. The victory was followed by the Augsburg Interim of 1548, a compromise theology imposed on the empire; it satisfied neither side and within five years a renewed Protestant alliance, with French help, drove him almost from Germany. Exhausted and ill, he authorised his brother Ferdinand to make the Peace of Augsburg in 1555, granting each German prince the right to determine the religion of his territory, cuius regio, eius religio. The settlement, which Charles himself refused to sign, was the formal admission that western Christendom would not be reunified.

In 1554–1556 he abdicated in stages, dividing his inheritance: the Spanish kingdoms, the Italian dominions, the Netherlands, and the empire in the Indies went to his son Philip II; the German Habsburg lands and the imperial crown went to his brother Ferdinand. He retired in October 1556 to the monastery of Yuste in Extremadura, where he kept a small court, his clocks, his Titians, his correspondence, and his confessors, and where he died on 21 September 1558. He had ruled, by one count, sixty-two distinct titles and a fifth of the human race.

The empire he had governed was the largest ever held by a European before Napoleon, and it could not survive in one pair of hands. Its division at his abdication, dynastic rather than ideological, set the lines along which Habsburg power would run for the next two centuries: the Spanish branch in Madrid, the Austrian branch in Vienna, the perpetual contest with France in the west and with the Ottomans in the east, the impossible task of paying for it all with the silver of the Indies. He had wanted to be a medieval Christian emperor in a world that was no longer medieval and would not stay Christian on his terms. The greatness of the failure is the measure of how seriously he had taken the task.
