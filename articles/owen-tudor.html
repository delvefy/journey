Owen Tudor

c. 1400 – 1461, Welsh squire of the household of Catherine of Valois, secret husband of a dowager queen of England, grandfather of Henry VII

Owen ap Maredudd ap Tudur, called in the English form of his name Owen Tudor, was born about 1400 in Anglesey, the son of Maredudd ap Tudur ap Goronwy and his wife Margaret of Plas-y-Dee. The family was a gentry house of the second rank in north Wales whose great period had been the thirteenth century, when his great-great-grandfather Ednyfed Fychan had served as steward to Llywelyn the Great and his great-grandfather to Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native prince of Wales. The Edwardian conquest of 1282 had stripped the family of its old offices, leaving it as substantial freeholders on Anglesey rather than the principal advisors of a Welsh court. His father and uncles had been deeply involved in Owain Glyndŵr's revolt against Henry IV from 1400 to 1409, and after its failure the family was attainted in 1406; lands were forfeited, several relatives executed or driven into exile, the political position broken. Owen would have grown up in straitened circumstances on what was left of Penmynydd in Anglesey, speaking Welsh as his first language and learning English and French in his teens for any career he might have outside Wales.

The means by which he entered Catherine of Valois's household are not documented, and the surviving evidence is mostly later hostile gossip. He appears to have served in some military capacity in Henry V's French wars, perhaps in the household of Sir Walter Hungerford, who was steward of Henry V's household and a connection of the queen's. By the mid-1420s, after the deaths of Henry V in August 1422 and Charles VI in October, the dowager queen Catherine, then twenty-one, was living in the English royal household as the mother of the infant Henry VI. By some date in the late 1420s, Owen, by then a man in his late twenties, was in her household, possibly as her keeper of the wardrobe, with access to her private apartments. The later chronicles reported with varying details — that Catherine had seen him swimming in the river by Windsor and been struck, that he had stumbled drunk into her lap at a dance — the moment of the romance; none rests on first-hand evidence.

What is reliable is that they were married, at some point probably about 1430, secretly. The marriage was canonically valid — both were free, the celebration before witnesses including, by some accounts, a priest, was sufficient — but was contracted in defiance of the parliamentary statute of 1428 forbidding the queen dowager's remarriage without the king's consent. The Council, which was dominated by John, Duke of Bedford, and Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, did not learn of it for some years. In the interval Catherine bore Owen at least four children: Edmund Tudor, born about 1430 at Much Hadham in Hertfordshire; Jasper Tudor, born about 1431 at Hatfield; a third son, also Owen, who became a Benedictine monk at Westminster; and a daughter, name unknown, who died young.

The marriage was discovered around 1436. Catherine, in poor health, retired to Bermondsey Abbey south of the Thames late that year; she died there on 3 January 1437 in her thirty-sixth year. Owen was at once summoned before the king's council in London. He came under guarantee of safe conduct and refused to acknowledge what he had done; he was then arrested and imprisoned in Newgate. He escaped, was retaken, was held for a time at Wallingford Castle. The growing affection of the young king Henry VI for his half-brothers, however, gradually changed his political position. By 1439 he had been pardoned, granted an annuity of forty pounds a year, and restored to his Welsh estates; by 1442 he was again at court. The two young Tudor boys, Edmund and Jasper, were brought into Henry VI's household and given the education appropriate to royal half-brothers.

Henry VI, when he came of age, treated them with affection. In 1452 he created Edmund Tudor Earl of Richmond and Jasper Earl of Pembroke and raised them to the peerage. In 1455 he arranged Edmund's marriage to Margaret Beaufort, the twelve-year-old heiress of the Beaufort line and a great-granddaughter, through her grandfather John Beaufort the legitimised son of John of Gaunt, of Edward III. Edmund did not live to see the consequences of the match: he was captured by Yorkist troops at Carmarthen in the autumn of 1456, fell ill of plague in their custody, and died on 3 November. His widow, then thirteen, was sent into Wales to her brother-in-law Jasper, and at Pembroke Castle on 28 January 1457 gave birth to a son who was christened Henry. The future Henry VII was Owen Tudor's grandson; the boy would, twenty-eight years later, win the throne at Bosworth and found the Tudor dynasty.

Owen himself, in late middle age, threw in his lot with his Lancastrian half-grandson and stepson. When the Wars of the Roses broke out in 1455, he raised troops in north Wales for the king's cause. The political position of the Lancastrian government deteriorated steadily through 1460; in February 1461 Edward, Earl of March, the son of Richard Plantagenet of York and the future Edward IV, marched into the Welsh Marches at the head of an army. Owen Tudor and Jasper, with a Lancastrian-Welsh-Irish force, attempted to bring up reinforcements through Hereford. They met Edward's army on 2 February 1461 at Mortimer's Cross, a few miles north of Leominster. The battle was decided by the Yorkist artillery and the famous appearance of three suns in the morning sky — a parhelion that Edward took to be a divine sign and a propaganda gift. The Lancastrian line was broken and the army was destroyed.

Owen Tudor was captured in the rout, escorted to Hereford, and beheaded on 4 February in the market square. The contemporary chronicler Gregory wrote that he had not believed he would actually be killed until he was on the block; he then is supposed to have said, looking at the executioner, "this head shall lie on the stock that was wont to lie on Queen Catherine's lap." A local woman afterwards washed his face, combed his hair, and set candles around the head. He was buried at the Greyfriars at Hereford.

His significance was wholly genealogical. He had been a Welsh squire of obscure family who happened to marry, secretly, a French princess of the house of Valois. Their grandson, by way of an heiress of the Beaufort line, would in 1485 take the throne of England and found a dynasty that produced Henry VIII, Mary I, and Elizabeth I; through his daughter Margaret it produced James VI of Scotland and so the union of the English and Scottish crowns. The line that ran from a defeated north Welsh house attainted in 1406 to the throne at Westminster in 1485 ran by way of the secret marriage at Much Hadham in about 1430 of a household squire and a young French widow.

The road continues to Henry VII of England.
