Elizabeth of York

1466 – 1503, daughter of Edward IV, wife of Henry VII, the Yorkist princess whose marriage closed the Wars of the Roses

Elizabeth was born at the Palace of Westminster on 11 February 1466, the eldest surviving child of Edward IV and Elizabeth Woodville. Her birth, after a string of stillbirths and one girl who had died in infancy, was the first political signal that the Yorkist marriage of 1464 — controversial because the queen had been a private gentlewoman, a widow with two sons, and four years older than the king — might in time produce an heir. She was baptised in Westminster Abbey with her godmothers Cecily Neville, the king's mother, and Jacquetta of Luxembourg, her grandmother. The court she grew up in was the rebuilt, deliberately luxurious court of Edward IV's restoration after Tewkesbury in 1471: tournaments at Westminster, lavish Easter ceremonies, a household run on the Burgundian model her father had picked up during his brief exile at Bruges.

From an early age she was an article of high diplomacy. At three she was betrothed to George Neville, Duke of Bedford, the heir of the Earl of Warwick; the betrothal lapsed when Warwick rebelled in 1469. From 1475 she was promised to the Dauphin Charles, the son of Louis XI of France, under the terms of the Treaty of Picquigny that had bought her father off from an English invasion of France. She was for a decade considered the future queen of France. Louis XI repudiated the betrothal in late 1482, marrying his son instead to Margaret of Austria, a humiliation her father felt deeply and that may have contributed to his sudden decline. Edward IV died on 9 April 1483 at the age of forty.

What followed was the central trauma of her life. Her uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester, summoned to escort his nephew Edward V from Ludlow to London for the coronation, instead arrested his nephew's escort, took the boy into custody, and lodged him in the Tower with his younger brother Richard of Shrewsbury. The queen's family was attacked, her uncle Anthony Woodville and her half-brother Richard Grey arrested and beheaded at Pontefract. By the end of June Richard had been declared king on the strength of an obscure parliamentary act, the Titulus Regius, that pronounced Edward IV's marriage to Elizabeth Woodville void on the ground of a pre-contract with one Eleanor Butler, and bastardised Edward IV's ten surviving legitimate children, Elizabeth among them. The two princes in the Tower disappeared during the late summer of 1483 and have not been certainly accounted for since. The dowager queen and her daughters took sanctuary at Westminster, where they remained, in increasingly straitened circumstances, for the rest of the year.

It was during this sanctuary that her mother and Margaret Beaufort, working through the physician Lewis Caerleon, agreed in principle to her marriage with Henry Tudor, then in exile in Brittany; Henry swore to the match at Rennes on Christmas Day 1483. The Buckingham revolt of October 1483, intended to bring him in, failed. In early 1484 Richard III negotiated her return from sanctuary with her mother in exchange for an oath of safe-conduct and provision for her daughters. The dowager queen accepted; Elizabeth went to Richard's court, where she was watched closely. The death of Richard's own queen Anne Neville on 16 March 1485 produced a flurry of rumour that Richard intended to marry his niece himself. The rumour appears to have had some substance; he was eventually forced by his own council to issue a public denial. Elizabeth's exact wishes in the matter are unrecoverable; a much later letter, attributed to her by a sixteenth-century antiquary, that suggests enthusiasm for the marriage, has been rejected by serious scholars as a probable fabrication.

On 22 August 1485 her future husband killed her uncle at Bosworth. She was nineteen. Henry VII postponed her marriage to himself for almost five months — until he had been crowned in his own right and parliament had repealed Titulus Regius, restoring her legitimacy — in order to make clear that he held the crown by conquest and inheritance rather than by his wife. She was married to him at Westminster on 18 January 1486 by Cardinal Bourchier. By September she had borne him his eldest son, Prince Arthur, at Winchester (the deliberate location was meant to evoke Arthurian legitimacy). Her own coronation, however, was deferred until 25 November 1487, a year and a half after the marriage and three months after the suppression of Lambert Simnel's rebellion at Stoke Field; the delay was felt by some observers to be ungenerous, the resulting ceremony was unusually splendid.

She bore eight children. Arthur was born in 1486 and died at Ludlow in 1502; Margaret in 1489, who would marry James IV of Scotland and become great-grandmother of James VI of Scotland and I of England; Henry in 1491, who would succeed his father; Elizabeth in 1492, who died at three; Mary in 1496, who would marry Louis XII of France and afterward Charles Brandon; Edmund in 1499, who died at fifteen months; Edward, who did not survive infancy; and her last child Katherine, born on her thirty-seventh birthday, 2 February 1503, who lived eight days. Elizabeth died of puerperal fever on 11 February 1503, her thirty-seventh birthday, at the Tower of London.

The reign she had quietly shared with her husband was the closest thing to a fully working consortship medieval England produced. She was, on every account, gentle, pious, generous to her relatives, careful with her finances, devoted to her children's education; she was painted twice with her family by Flemish-trained artists at court; she was popular with the Londoners in a way her formidable mother-in-law was not. The Tudor settlement rested on her body and on the union of the white and red roses that her marriage symbolised. The "Tudor rose" of double colour, derived from her family's white rose and her husband's red, remains the central element of the English royal arms. The folksong rhyme attributed to her in later tradition, "Elizabeth, our queen, was good, our king he was a fool" — was directed not at her husband but at the queens of cards in a children's nursery rhyme; the historical Henry VII, by contrast, was widely thought to have loved her and not to have remarried after her death partly out of a real grief.

She was buried at Westminster Abbey beside the still-vacant tomb space prepared for her husband. The double tomb by Pietro Torrigiano, with the gilded recumbent effigies in Italian Renaissance bronze, was completed about 1519 and stands behind the high altar of the Lady Chapel her husband had founded. Her death broke Henry VII; her son Henry VIII would, by some of his contemporaries' accounts, never quite recover from the loss of his mother at the age of eleven.

Her historical place is as the heiress through whose womb the Yorkist line was carried into the Tudor dynasty. The two princes in the Tower, presumed murdered, left her as the senior representative of Edward IV's blood. The marriage to Henry VII was the political reconciliation of the Wars of the Roses; the descent of every English monarch since 1485 runs in unbroken line through her. The Plantagenet inheritance that had begun with Geoffrey of Anjou in 1128 did not, in fact, end at Bosworth: it passed by way of Elizabeth of York into the Tudor century and from there, through her great-granddaughter Mary Queen of Scots, into every later European royal house. Her brief life had set the genealogy that the next four hundred years of English monarchy would follow.
