Joscelin II of Edessa

c. 1113 – 1159, last effective count of Edessa, 1131–1150

Joscelin was born about 1113, the only son of Joscelin I of Courtenay, lord of Tell Bashir and from 1118 count of Edessa, by Beatrice, the daughter of an Armenian magnate of the mountains north of Antioch and herself a Christian of the eastern church. He grew up in the bilingual frontier society of his father’s county: a few thousand Latin French and Norman soldiers and settlers concentrated in Edessa and the river fortresses, set down among a much larger population of Armenian Christians, Syriac Jacobites, Greek Orthodox, and Muslim Arabs of the surrounding villages. He spoke Armenian and Syriac as well as the French of his father’s court, married a Frankish lady, Beatrice of Saone, and was considered by the Frankish nobility of the south, who never visited the county, more an oriental than a westerner. The chroniclers from the kingdom of Jerusalem, especially William of Tyre, who knew him personally in his last years, describe him as dark, fleshy, easy-tempered, fond of long dinners, careless in his administration, and a competent if intermittent soldier when forced to take the field.

He inherited the county on the death of his father in September 1131. Joscelin I, accompanying the army of his neighbour the prince of Antioch in a small siege near Aleppo, had been mortally injured by the collapse of a sap that he was inspecting; he died at Tell Bashir, refusing to lie down, with his son at his side. The new count was perhaps eighteen and had served already with his father in the small engagements of the frontier, but the county he received was in a worse position than the public reputation of the dynasty admitted. The territorial recovery his father had made after the disasters of the 1120s had been thin; the great frontier fortresses had been let drift into disrepair; the financial base of the county lay in the lands east of the Euphrates, which were also the most exposed to the Turkic raids out of the Jazira. Worst of all, the political alliance that had given the county its security — the close military partnership with King Baldwin II of Jerusalem and with the principality of Antioch — was breaking up under the new generation. Joscelin II, in the first ten years of his rule, quarrelled with Fulk of Anjou, who succeeded Baldwin II as king of Jerusalem in 1131, with Raymond of Poitiers, who became prince of Antioch in 1136, and with the queen Melisende, the only one of the southern royal house who continued to take an interest in his welfare. The estrangement was the more dangerous because the Latin establishment of the Levant was at the same moment confronted, for the first time, by a Muslim power capable of fielding an army of fifteen thousand men.

That power was the new Zengid empire of Mosul and Aleppo built by Imad ad-Din Zengi from 1127. Through the 1130s and early 1140s Zengi took, one after another, the small Muslim principalities of the upper Jazira that had previously played as makeweights between the Christians and the Seljuks; by 1144 his frontier ran along the whole eastern edge of the county of Edessa. The decisive moment came in the autumn of 1144. Joscelin had marched east with the field army of the county, perhaps two thousand men, to support an Artuqid chieftain of Mardin who had broken with Zengi. While he was three days’ ride from Edessa, Zengi turned aside from his own march on the Artuqids, swung north-east, and appeared with twenty thousand men under the walls of the city. The garrison Joscelin had left behind was small — his standing army was at Tell Bashir — and the civilian population of Armenians and Syrians had no confidence in either the count or the Latin clergy of the city. After a siege of nearly four weeks Zengi’s sappers mined the wall by the Gate of the Hours; on the night of 23–24 December 1144, while the Latin canons were attending a Christmas Eve service in the cathedral, the sap was fired, the wall came down, and the army stormed in. The Latin inhabitants were massacred or sold into slavery; the archbishop Hugh II was killed in the entry of the cathedral; the Armenian quarter, less politically dangerous in Zengi’s estimation, was spared. Joscelin, riding hard from Tell Bashir at the news, arrived too late and turned back to his own fortress. Edessa, the oldest Crusader capital, was gone.

He had two years to try to retrieve the disaster. The Second Crusade was preached in Europe in the spring of 1146 in direct response to the fall; Joscelin himself, in November of the same year, made an attempt to recover the city by stealth. The Armenian inhabitants of Edessa, dismayed by the new Muslim regime, sent secret messengers offering to open the gates to him; Joscelin re-entered the city by night at the head of a small column. The citadel, held by a Zengid garrison, did not fall. Zengi was at that moment dead — murdered in his tent at Qalat Jabar in September 1146 — but his son Nur ad-Din arrived with the army of Aleppo within forty-eight hours. The walls Joscelin had hoped to defend were partly destroyed and partly unrepaired; the assault carried them in a single day; the Armenian population was massacred along with the Latin column. Joscelin himself escaped through a postern with a few horsemen. The catastrophe was the end of the county as a political fact; the western lordships of Tell Bashir, Aintab, and Marash continued to fly his banner, but the eastern half was lost beyond recovery.

The Second Crusade, when at last it arrived in Syria in the summer of 1148, ignored the case for the recovery of Edessa altogether and chose to attack Damascus, which was at that moment the only friendly Muslim power on the Christian frontier. The siege collapsed in four days. Joscelin, who had hoped to be the principal beneficiary of the Crusade, was now without western allies; the kingdom of Jerusalem, after the failure, washed its hands of the surviving fragments of his county. In May 1150, riding from Tell Bashir to consult with the new prince of Antioch, he was ambushed by a small Turcoman raiding party detached from the army of Nur ad-Din, taken prisoner, and brought in chains to Aleppo. Nur ad-Din had him publicly blinded in the maydan of the citadel and confined in the prison cell that adjoined it. His widow Beatrice, with no realistic means of defending the western lordships, sold them in the same year to the emperor Manuel I Komnenos, who garrisoned them for a generation before they too were lost. Joscelin died in his cell at Aleppo in 1159, blind, after nine years of captivity; he was forty-six. He was the last count to govern Edessa.
