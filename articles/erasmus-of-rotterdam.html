Erasmus of Rotterdam

c. 1466 – 1536, Dutch humanist, Christian philologist, and prince of the Republic of Letters

Erasmus of Rotterdam was born in the Netherlands probably on 28 October 1466 — the date is disputed within a range of a few years — the illegitimate son of a priest named Gerard and a physician's daughter named Margaretha. He grew up in Gouda and was sent for his early schooling first to Deventer, where the Brethren of the Common Life ran the most progressive Latin school of the late medieval Netherlands, and then to 's-Hertogenbosch. Both parents died of plague when he was a young teenager. His guardians, anxious to be rid of him, pushed him in 1487 into the Augustinian canons of Steyn near Gouda, where he was professed in 1488 and ordained priest in 1492. The monastery was the unhappiest period of his life. He was bookish, fastidious, in poor health, and contemptuous of the level of learning around him; for the rest of his life he sought, with eventual papal sanction, exemptions from the rule he had taken under family pressure.

In 1495 he was sent as Latin secretary to the bishop of Cambrai and from there to study theology at the College of Montaigu in Paris, then under the rigorous discipline of Jan Standonck. The Montaigu years were miserable — bad food, intellectual narrowness, constant illness — and he soon began to earn his living as a private tutor of wealthy young men, including, in 1499, the English nobleman William Blount, Lord Mountjoy, who took him on his first visit to England. There he met Thomas More, John Colet, William Grocyn, and Thomas Linacre — the Oxford and London humanist circle that would change his sense of what scholarship could be. Colet's lectures on Paul's epistles, given without the medieval scholastic apparatus and drawing directly on the Greek and the historical context, gave Erasmus a model that he would carry into his own biblical work.

He published the first edition of his Adages in 1500, a collection of Greek and Latin proverbs with humanist commentary that grew across the next thirty years into one of the great books of the Renaissance — a portable encyclopaedia of classical wisdom for the educated layman, and the work that established his European fame. He travelled, in the years that followed, more than any other scholar of his generation: to Paris, to England again, to Italy in 1506–1509, where he took a doctorate at Turin and worked at the press of Aldus Manutius in Venice, to England a third time and a fourth, to Brabant, eventually to Basel, where he attached himself to the press of Johann Froben and made it the foremost humanist publishing house in Europe.

In 1509, on the return journey from Italy to England, he composed in a single week, while staying at Thomas More's house in London, the Praise of Folly — Moriae Encomium, a Latin pun on More's name — a satirical declamation in the voice of Folly herself against the corruption of the clergy, the pedantry of theologians, the pretensions of princes, and the self-deceptions of every estate of Christendom. It was published in 1511 and went through dozens of editions in his lifetime. It made him, at a stroke, the most famous writer in Latin Europe.

The work he himself regarded as his most important was the Novum Instrumentum of 1516, the first published Greek New Testament — printed by Froben in Basel, with his own fresh Latin translation alongside the Greek and a large body of philological annotations. The Greek text was, by later standards, hastily compiled from a small number of manuscripts, and Erasmus would issue four further editions correcting it across his lifetime; but the existence of a printed Greek New Testament transformed biblical scholarship. Luther translated from it; Tyndale translated from it; every subsequent vernacular New Testament in Protestant Europe rested on Erasmus's text. He himself had intended the work as an instrument of Catholic reform — a return to the sources, philologically purified, in the service of what he called the philosophia Christi, the simple ethical teaching of Christ as the heart of Christianity. His enormous edition of Saint Jerome's letters, published the same year, was meant to do the same for the patristic tradition.

He returned to Basel in 1521 and made it his base for the rest of his life. By then the Reformation had broken out around him, and his position became increasingly difficult. Luther and his allies wanted Erasmus's prestige; the Catholic hierarchy demanded his denunciation of Luther. He temporised. He admired Luther's courage in attacking abuses but rejected what he saw as the violence of his temperament, his dogmatic certainty, his contempt for the consensus of the church. In 1524 he was finally forced to take a public position and wrote De Libero Arbitrio — On the Freedom of the Will — defending against Luther the proposition that the human will retained some power, however small, to cooperate with grace. Luther replied the next year with De Servo Arbitrio, On the Bondage of the Will, a far longer and more brutal text that broke their relationship and, with it, the hope that the humanist programme and the Reformation could remain part of a single movement.

For the rest of his life Erasmus stood between the parties, denounced by both. The Catholic side increasingly suspected him of having “laid the egg that Luther hatched” — the phrase circulated in his lifetime — while the Lutherans regarded him as a coward who had refused to follow his own logic. When Basel adopted the Reformation in 1529 he moved to the Catholic university town of Freiburg im Breisgau in order to be left alone; when his health failed in 1535 he returned to Basel and to the house of his old friend Hieronymus Froben, the son of his publisher.

He died there on 12 July 1536, in his late sixties or perhaps seventy, without the sacraments, surrounded by Protestant friends, whispering at the end in Dutch. He was buried with honour in the Lutheran cathedral of Basel, an arrangement that itself testifies to the ambiguity of his last years. The Council of Trent in the years after his death listed several of his works on the Index of Forbidden Books; Catholic critics continued to denounce him into the seventeenth century. Protestant scholarship, conversely, recovered him steadily as the founder of biblical philology and the patron saint of Christian humanism.

The legacy was vast and resistant to capture by either confession. He had been the most widely read author of his time. His Adages had taught classical learning to several generations of European schoolboys. His New Testament had reset the entire textual basis of Reformation biblical work. His correspondence — preserved in over three thousand letters, the largest surviving epistolary archive of any sixteenth-century European — opened a window onto the humanist republic of letters at its height. His irenic and historical Christianity, dismissed by Reformation and Counter-Reformation alike, would be rediscovered by Enlightenment writers and by liberal Christians in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as one of the founding voices of a more tolerant, more sceptical, more grounded Christian intellectual life.

The road continues to Thomas More.
