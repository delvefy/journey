Peter the Apostle

fl. c. AD 30 – c. 64 · fisherman of Bethsaida, first of the apostles

Simon, son of Jonah — called Cephas in Aramaic, Peter in Greek, both meaning rock — is named first in every list of Jesus's twelve disciples preserved in the New Testament and has been remembered ever since as the chief of the apostles. He was a fisherman from Bethsaida on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee, working out of nearby Capernaum with his brother Andrew and his partners James and John, the sons of Zebedee, when Jesus called him. He died, on the unanimous testimony of the earliest church, by crucifixion in Rome during the persecution under the emperor Nero, about thirty years later.

The gospels portray him as an impulsive, devoted, and chronically failing man. He volunteers and falters, professes and denies, leaps from the boat to walk on the water and sinks within steps. He is the first of the apostles to recognize Jesus as the Christ, the son of the living God, and the only one to receive an immediate rebuke for trying to talk Jesus out of suffering. He cuts off the ear of the high priest's servant in Gethsemane and is told to put up his sword. He follows Jesus into the courtyard of the high priest's house on the night of the trial, only to swear three times to a serving girl and a few bystanders that he does not know the man. The cock crows; he goes out and weeps bitterly. The gospel of Mark, which the early church believed to derive from Peter's own preaching, preserves these failures with what reads like firsthand memory.

The resurrection accounts make him their first male witness. Paul's letter to the Corinthians, written within twenty-five years of the events, lists the appearances of the risen Christ and places Peter, called by his Aramaic name Cephas, before all the rest. The gospel of John records a final scene by the sea of Galilee in which the risen Jesus three times asks Peter whether he loves him and three times tells him to feed his sheep — a public undoing of the threefold denial. From the day of Pentecost onward the book of Acts puts him at the head of the apostolic preaching in Jerusalem: he speaks for the Twelve to the festival crowds, heals a crippled man at the Beautiful gate of the Temple, twice answers to the Sanhedrin under Caiaphas, presides at the choosing of a replacement for Judas, and convenes the first community in the city.

Two decisions shaped the early church and have his fingerprints on them. The first was the admission of Gentiles. After a vision on a rooftop in Joppa, Peter baptized the Roman centurion Cornelius and his household at Caesarea without first requiring them to keep the Jewish law, and defended the act to the Jerusalem community when challenged. The principle was confirmed at a council in Jerusalem around AD 49, at which Peter argued openly for the admission of Gentiles on faith alone. The second was his collision with Paul over the same question. At Antioch, in the years that followed the council, Peter ate freely with Gentile believers until a delegation arrived from James the brother of Jesus, after which he withdrew. Paul, in the letter to the Galatians, says he confronted Peter to his face because he stood condemned — the only surviving glimpse of a sharp public disagreement between the two pillars of the early church.

Peter's later travels can only be sketched. He left Jerusalem under Herod Agrippa I in AD 44, after a night escape from a prison guarded by four squads of four soldiers. He worked among the Jewish communities of the eastern Mediterranean — the audience addressed by the letters that bear his name is the Christian diaspora of Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia — and by the early sixties he was in Rome. The first letter of Peter signs off from a place called Babylon, an early Christian code for Rome. Clement, bishop of the city within a generation of Peter's death, names him as a martyr alongside Paul; the historian Eusebius, three centuries later, records that he was crucified upside down at his own request, deeming himself unworthy to die in the same manner as his master.

He is buried, the same Roman tradition insists, on the Vatican hill, where a small monument over his grave was visible in the second century and where the first great church of Christendom was raised over the site by the emperor Constantine in the 320s. Excavations under the present basilica of Saint Peter, conducted between 1939 and 1949, located beneath the high altar a first-century necropolis and a niche grave that the early shrine appears to have been built to mark; the bones removed from it have been identified as those of an elderly man, though no certain proof can ever be had.

The letters attributed to him in the New Testament are two: the first a circular epistle of pastoral encouragement, written in highly literate Greek and addressed to scattered Christians under pressure; the second a sterner warning against false teachers, whose authenticity has been more disputed since antiquity. The figure of Peter himself was thereafter overlaid in legend with the keys of the kingdom — promised to him by Jesus in Matthew's gospel — with the founding of the see of Rome, with the long line of bishops who would claim his succession, and with a luxuriant apocryphal literature of his journeys, miracles, and confrontation with Simon Magus.

But the rock that Jesus built upon was a fisherman who walked on water and then sank, who professed and denied, who cried at a courtyard fire and went out alone before dawn. That his sense of his own failure became the substrate of an authority extended to every successor and every century is, by any measure, an improbable construction in the history of religion. The fisherman from Bethsaida, after a half-century of preaching that took him from the Galilean lakeshore to the heart of the empire that had crucified his teacher, was crucified on the same hill where the basilica that bears his name now stands.

The road continues to John the Apostle.
