Cardinal Henry Beaufort

c. 1375 – 1447, Bishop of Winchester, Cardinal of England, the richest churchman of the late medieval English crown

Henry Beaufort was born about 1375, the second of the four children of John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, by his mistress and later third wife Katherine Swynford. He and his three siblings were born out of wedlock during the lifetime of Gaunt's second wife Constance of Castile and were legitimated only in February 1397, when Gaunt married Katherine after Constance's death and procured an act of legitimation from Richard II and a bull from Pope Boniface IX. The surname Beaufort was taken from a castle of Gaunt's in Champagne. The children entered the upper aristocracy as adults already grown; in Henry IV's first parliament after his usurpation in 1399 the act of legitimation was reissued with an inserted clause barring them from inheriting the crown — a clause whose legal force was, and would remain, disputed.

Henry was educated for the church — the conventional placement of a second son — first at Aachen, then at Oxford and Cambridge, where he was for a time chancellor of the older university. His ecclesiastical career advanced with his family's political fortunes. He was dean of Wells at twenty-two, bishop of Lincoln at twenty-three, and bishop of Winchester — the wealthiest see in England — from 1404 until his death forty-three years later. Winchester gave him an income that contemporaries estimated at twenty thousand pounds a year, far above any other ecclesiastical revenue in the country and exceeding most lay magnates' annual income. The wealth of the see, deployed as ready cash, was the basis of everything he afterwards did in politics.

He served his half-brother Henry IV as Chancellor of England from 1403 to 1405; he resigned the office after a dispute with the king and went into a long quarrel that was patched up only when his nephew Henry V succeeded in 1413. Under his nephew, with whom he was in temperament and policy aligned, he served as Chancellor again from 1413 to 1417 and was the chief financial agent of the French war. He lent the crown over thirty-five thousand pounds in those years from his own revenues, more than any other single subject, and the loans, regularly repaid, were the model for the system of crown borrowing that sustained the long campaign that culminated in Agincourt and the Treaty of Troyes.

In 1417 Pope Martin V, newly elected at the Council of Constance after the long Western Schism — an election in which Beaufort had been a leading English negotiator — offered him a cardinal's hat. Henry V, jealous of foreign loyalties in his most senior ecclesiastic, forbade him to accept it. Beaufort resigned the chancellorship and went on pilgrimage instead. He took the hat in December 1426, three years after his nephew's death, when the political situation in England had changed and his great rival Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, no longer had the influence to prevent it. From that date he was Cardinal of England, and was frequently called by the contemporary nickname "the Cardinal of England" or, sometimes, "the rich Cardinal".

From 1422 the minority of the infant Henry VI made him, with his nephew John, Duke of Bedford, the regent of France, and his nephew Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, the protector in England, one of the three principal figures of the regency. Bedford he supported because their policies in France coincided; Gloucester he opposed without remission for two decades, in a feud that several times nearly turned to violence in the streets of London and in the council chamber. The feud was substantive as well as personal: Gloucester wanted aggressive war and the protection of the Burgundian alliance at any cost; Beaufort, with the cooler view of a man who counted the money, preferred negotiation and was prepared to do business with Pope Martin V and with the dauphinist French if it would protect the financial structure of the English position.

In 1429 he led a crusading army — raised under papal authority against the Hussites of Bohemia — into France instead, after Bedford diverted it to meet the emergency raised by Joan of Arc's relief of Orléans. The diversion was technically a misuse of crusade funds and earned him a long quarrel with Rome, but it stiffened the English line in Normandy at the critical moment. He attended Henry VI's French coronation at Notre-Dame de Paris on 16 December 1431, the high point of the dual monarchy, and presided over the Council of Arras in 1435 at which the Burgundian alliance broke down and the English position in France began its long collapse.

The death of Bedford at Rouen in September 1435 left him the senior surviving Beaufort and, in effect, the head of the council of England. For a further twelve years he governed the regime's finances and increasingly its foreign policy. He arranged the release of Charles d'Orléans, the long-imprisoned royal duke captured at Agincourt, in 1440, against Gloucester's furious opposition. He brokered, with his great-nephew William de la Pole, Earl of Suffolk, the truce of Tours in 1444 and the marriage of Henry VI to Margaret of Anjou the following year. By the time of his retirement from active politics in 1446 the cause of his nephew's French inheritance was visibly broken and the loss of Maine had been secretly conceded as the price of the marriage.

His personal wealth at his death was prodigious. He lent the crown a total, in the course of his career, of over two hundred thousand pounds, most of it secured on the customs and substantially repaid. He founded almshouses, completed the chapel and nave of Winchester Cathedral, rebuilt the great hospital of St Cross outside the city, and left bequests of plate, vestments, and books that filled three pages of his will. He died at the bishop's palace of Wolvesey at Winchester on 11 April 1447, aged about seventy-two, and was buried in a high tomb behind the high altar of his cathedral, which survives. Shakespeare's Henry VI, Part Two, taking up a hostile Tudor tradition, ends with Beaufort writhing on his deathbed in fear of damnation; the contemporary record gives no such picture, only that of an old man dying composed in the chair in which he had governed England for thirty years.

His family long outlived him. The legitimated Beaufort descendants of his father supplied the Earls and later Dukes of Somerset of the fifteenth century, and through his great-niece Margaret Beaufort, who married Edmund Tudor and bore Henry VII, they supplied the Tudor royal house. The "Beaufort clause" barring them from the crown had been, in the end, no bar at all.
