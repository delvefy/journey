Ferdinand II of Aragon

1452 – 1516, King of Aragon, Sicily, Naples, and Castile by right of his wife, co-founder of Catholic Spain, the original of Machiavelli's prince

Ferdinand was born on 10 March 1452 at Sos del Rey Católico in northern Aragon, the son of John II of Aragon by his second wife, Juana Enríquez, daughter of the Admiral of Castile. His father's first marriage had produced an heir, Carlos, Prince of Viana, and Ferdinand grew up amid a long, bitter civil war between his father and his elder half-brother over the inheritance of Navarre and the loyalty of the Catalans. Carlos died in 1461, possibly poisoned by Juana Enríquez; the war with Catalonia continued for another decade. At sixteen Ferdinand was made King of Sicily by his father, partly to give him standing in the marriage market.

That marriage was the decisive event of his life. In October 1469 he crossed Castile in disguise, a muleteer in the train of merchants, to reach Valladolid and marry Isabella, the eighteen-year-old heiress presumptive of Castile. The match was opposed by her half-brother Henry IV and supported by the archbishop of Toledo; the papal dispensation produced for the wedding had been forged. From it sprang one of the great working partnerships of European monarchy. They were equals in title, devotion, and ambition. Their motto, Tanto monta, monta tanto, Isabel como Fernando, declared their interchangeability, and although in practice Isabella was the more determined administrator and he the better soldier and negotiator, the surviving correspondence shows them in close, daily political collaboration for thirty-five years.

When Henry IV of Castile died in December 1474, Ferdinand and Isabella claimed Castile against the partisans of Juana, called la Beltraneja. The war that followed was both a civil war and a war against Portugal, whose king Afonso V had married Juana and invaded in her name. Ferdinand commanded at the field of Toro on 1 March 1476, a confused engagement which both sides claimed but which broke the rebel cause. By 1479, after the Treaty of Alcáçovas, Castile was secure. That same year his father died and he inherited Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, and the Balearic Islands, with Sicily and Sardinia. The two crowns now lay in his and her persons, with their separate parliaments, laws, currencies, and tax systems untouched, but the foreign policy of both was set in common.

Internally, Ferdinand spent the 1480s and 1490s pacifying his own kingdoms. In Aragon he conciliated the entrenched fueros, the traditional privileges of the towns and the nobility; in Catalonia he ended the long peasant revolt of the remences by the Sentence of Guadalupe in 1486, abolishing the harshest seigneurial dues in exchange for peace. In Castile he and Isabella jointly extended royal justice into the countryside and broke the old feudal aristocracy as a political class. The Inquisition, established under their authority from 1478 and headed by Tomás de Torquemada, was an instrument they shared. The mass expulsion of unconverted Jews in 1492, against the strong advice of his Jewish treasurer Isaac Abravanel, was approved by them both.

The Granada war, beginning in 1482, was Ferdinand's military masterpiece. Over ten campaigning seasons he reduced the last Muslim state in Iberia by methodical siege rather than open battle: artillery trains, garrisoned border fortresses, the destruction of the agricultural plain or vega outside each target city, generous terms of surrender to those who yielded and exemplary punishment to those who did not. On 2 January 1492 Boabdil, the last Nasrid emir, gave him the keys of the Alhambra. The fall of Granada was greeted in Christendom as a final reversal of the Muslim conquests of seven centuries earlier, and the papal title Reyes Católicos, the Catholic Monarchs, granted by Alexander VI in 1496, set the seal on it. The same months saw the capitulations with Columbus and the dispatch of the first Atlantic voyage; the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494, dividing the unknown world with Portugal, was negotiated by Ferdinand's lawyers.

Within months of Granada he turned the army he had built on Italy. Charles VIII of France invaded Italy in 1494 and seized Naples, which Ferdinand regarded as a possession of his house. The Italian Wars that followed would dominate the next decade and beyond. He sent to Italy the soldier who became his greatest commander, Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, el Gran Capitán, who at Cerignola and the Garigliano in 1503 destroyed two French armies and made Naples a Spanish kingdom. Ferdinand entered Naples in 1506 to receive its homage. The army Gonzalo led there was the prototype of the Spanish tercios, the disciplined pike-and-shot infantry that would dominate European battlefields for the next hundred and fifty years.

Isabella died in November 1504. The Castilian succession passed to their daughter Joanna, married to Philip the Handsome of Burgundy, but Joanna's mental state was fragile and Philip wanted the regency for himself. Ferdinand was at first pushed out, retreating to Aragon and Naples; in 1505 he married, more from policy than passion, the young Germaine de Foix, niece of the king of France, in a bid for a second heir who never lived. Philip's sudden death in September 1506 brought him back. From 1507 to his own death he ruled Castile as regent for the increasingly secluded Joanna and built up her son Charles, growing up in the Netherlands, as the future king. In 1512 he occupied Spanish Navarre, ending the independent Pyrenean kingdom and rounding off the peninsula.

He died at the village of Madrigalejo in Extremadura on 23 January 1516, on his way south, in his sixty-fourth year, having outlived nearly all his children. He left to his grandson Charles a united Spain with footholds in North Africa, the empires of Naples, Sicily, and Sardinia, the new world in the west, the most formidable army in Europe, and the diplomatic habits of a state that for the next century would be the central power of Christendom.

His reputation in his own time was high and ambivalent. He was famously patient, dissembling, and willing to break treaties he had sworn to. Machiavelli, looking out from Florence at the new powers of Europe, took him as the type of the successful new prince, the man who used religion to bind his subjects, war to extend his frontiers, and the appearance of good faith to gain advantage over it. The judgement was hostile and not unfounded. The structure he left, however, was the most durable political construction of the Renaissance, and the long history of Spanish power begins in his lifetime and his marriage.
