Philip of Swabia

1177 – 1208, youngest son of Frederick Barbarossa, contested king of the Romans, murdered at Bamberg

Philip was born in February or August 1177 at Pavia, the youngest of the surviving sons of the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa and his Burgundian empress Beatrice. He was the fifth of the boys to reach adulthood — his elder brothers Henry, Frederick, Otto, and Conrad were intended in turn for the German kingdom, the duchy of Swabia, the county of Burgundy, and the duchy of Rothenburg — and his birth came late enough that there was no remaining temporal inheritance to settle on him. Barbarossa accordingly directed him into the Church. The boy was placed at the cathedral school of Adelberg, then sent in adolescence to the great Premonstratensian house of Marbach, and in 1189, at twelve, was nominated provost of the cathedral chapter of Aachen, an office of substantial prestige in the German church and one that traditionally went to members of the imperial family destined for an episcopal career. By 1190 or 1191 he had been elected bishop of Würzburg, though never consecrated; whether he received the diaconate, the only canonical step toward priesthood that he certainly took, is implied by some sources and denied by others.

The series of family deaths that followed undid the plan. In May 1191, his elder brother Frederick of Swabia died at Acre on the Third Crusade. In August 1196 his brother Conrad of Rothenburg, then duke of Swabia, was murdered in a private quarrel at Durlach. With both elder Swabian heirs dead, Philip was the only surviving brother of the new emperor Henry VI, who needed reliable kinsmen for the administration of an empire that now stretched from the North Sea to Palermo. Henry recalled him from his ecclesiastical career, secured a papal dispensation for his return to lay status, and in the autumn of 1195 invested him with the duchy of Tuscany, the imperial march in central Italy; the next year, on Conrad's death, he received the duchy of Swabia and the seal of the German family lands. By 1197 he had become his brother's principal lieutenant on both sides of the Alps.

In the spring of 1197 Henry sent him to Constantinople to escort home the bride who had been chosen for the dead Frederick of Swabia: Irene Angelina, daughter of the deposed Byzantine emperor Isaac II Angelos, and the widow of Roger III of Sicily, whom Henry's conquest of the Norman kingdom three years earlier had placed in his hands. By the time the embassy reached Augsburg the marriage had been redirected to Philip himself, who married Irene there on 25 May 1197. She was about seventeen, fluent in three languages, formed by the court ceremonial of both Constantinople and Palermo, and brought to the German marriage the personal influence that her household and her correspondence with her brothers in the East would retain throughout the rest of Philip's life. The marriage was contracted in haste because Henry was preparing his crusade and intended Philip to govern the German half of the empire in his absence. Philip set out for Sicily in the summer to fetch the infant son of Henry and Constance — the future Frederick II, then about three — whom Henry had had elected king of the Romans the year before and who was now to be brought north as the secured heir.

Philip got as far as central Italy when the news reached him that his brother had died at Messina on 28 September 1197 of dysentery, contracted on a hunting trip in Apulia. The succession that Henry VI had spent the last years of his reign trying to make hereditary collapsed at once. Constance of Sicily, the widow, refused to have the infant Frederick taken to Germany and made what shift she could to secure him in Palermo under the wardship of Pope Innocent III, whose election in January 1198 followed within months. The German princes were divided. Many regarded the election of the three-year-old in Frederick's name as void; the southern and central German nobility, the great ministerial families of the Hohenstaufen demesne, and the prelates dependent on the imperial church looked for an adult Hohenstaufen, and turned to Philip. The Welf faction in the Rhineland and north Germany, with the Plantagenet money of Richard I and the support of the archbishop of Cologne, looked for an alternative and found him in Otto, son of Henry the Lion and Matilda Plantagenet.

Philip was elected at Erfurt on 6 March 1198 by a majority of the southern princes and crowned at Mainz on 8 September 1198 in the cathedral by the archbishop of Tarentaise, with the genuine imperial regalia — the crown of Conrad II, the holy lance, the sword — that the Hohenstaufen had kept in their possession. Otto's election at Cologne in June and his coronation at Aachen on 12 July 1198, in the place that custom required but with substituted regalia and by an irregularly enthroned archbishop, gave him the opposite formal advantage: he had been crowned at Aachen on the throne of Charlemagne by what was constitutionally the right consecrator. The double election produced ten years of civil war, settled at the level of armies and at the level of canon law.

For the first four years of the war, Innocent III stood aloof and weighed the candidates with the doctrine that the pope had the right of arbitration on disputed elections; in his great Deliberatio super facto imperii, circulated in 1200–1202, he came down for Otto, on the calculation that the Welf claim posed less danger to the papal lands in central Italy than the consolidated Hohenstaufen one. The decision did not produce a military result. Philip held the field. His armies recovered Saxony for a time, threatened Cologne in 1205, drove Otto back to his Welf strongholds, and by the spring of 1207 had reduced him to negotiating an honourable abdication. In the summer of 1207 Innocent III revised his position. He absolved Philip from the excommunication imposed during the worst of the imperial-papal dispute, dispatched the cardinals Hugolino dei Conti di Segni and Leo Brancaleone as legates to make peace, and accepted the political reality that Philip would be the emperor.

The terms agreed in March 1208 were to be sealed by a marriage. Otto would surrender the kingship in exchange for the hand of Philip's eldest daughter Beatrice and a substantial portion of the imperial revenues; Philip's other daughters were to be married out in a series of alliances designed to repair the broken political map. Cunigunde was assigned to Wenceslas of Bohemia; Maria to Henry of Brabant; the youngest, also Beatrice, to a southern prince. In May 1208 Philip travelled to Bamberg to celebrate at the cathedral the marriage of his niece, the daughter of his murdered brother Conrad of Rothenburg, to Otto of Andechs-Meranian. The court was lodged in the bishop's palace on the Domberg. On the afternoon of 21 June, the third day of the festivities, after the wedding mass and the public meal, Philip retired to a private chamber to rest. Count Otto of Wittelsbach, Count Palatine of Bavaria, a member of the imperial household with whom Philip had broken a betrothal to his elder daughter Cunigunde the year before in favour of the Bohemian match, entered the chamber, drew his sword, and cut the king's throat with a single stroke. He was let out of the palace by attendants who did not understand what had happened, escaped from the city before alarm was raised, and rode for the Bavarian forests.

The murder was the work of a private revenge of a kind common in the medieval German nobility; it had no political backing of any consequence. The reaction was immediate and savage. Philip's nephew Henry of Calden was put in charge of the pursuit; Otto of Wittelsbach was hunted down across Bavaria into Lower Saxony and killed at the small Saxon town of Oberndorf on the Weser in March 1209, his body left unburied on the road; his castles were demolished; his lands declared forfeit. Irene Angelina, who was at Hohenstaufen on the Swabian Alb when the news of her husband's death reached her in the seventh month of pregnancy, miscarried and died of the consequences on 27 August 1208. Philip's body was carried south for burial first at the Cathedral of Bamberg and, three decades later under the reign of his grand-nephew Frederick II, was translated to Speyer Cathedral, the Hohenstaufen necropolis, where it lies among his father, his brother, and the line of German kings before and after them.

The political effect of the assassination ran in the opposite direction from its motive. All the princes recognised Otto IV at the next Diet of Frankfurt later that summer; the betrothal of Beatrice was confirmed; the imperial crown that had been promised to Philip passed in October 1209 to the man he had been bargaining with. The Hohenstaufen succession resumed only with Philip's nephew Frederick II, whose coming of age in 1212 reopened the question and whose 1215 coronation at Aachen completed the recovery of the inheritance that Philip's death had so unexpectedly suspended.

The road continues to Henry VI.
