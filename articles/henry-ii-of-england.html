Henry II of England

1133 – 1189, first of the Angevin kings of England, founder of the common law, ruler from the Tweed to the Pyrenees

Henry was born at Le Mans on 5 March 1133, the eldest son of Geoffrey Plantagenet, count of Anjou and Maine and through his marriage duke of Normandy, and of the Empress Matilda, the only surviving legitimate child of Henry I of England and widow of the German emperor Henry V. The byname Plantagenet, taken by his father from the sprig of broom he wore in his hat, would attach to the dynasty of which Henry was the first generation; through his mother he had a claim to England as the grandson of the Conqueror's son, a claim that had been settled on her by Henry I's English nobility in oath of 1127 and on her son after her by a further oath ten years later. Henry I's death in December 1135 nevertheless brought to the throne not Matilda but her cousin Stephen of Blois, who reached London first. Eighteen years of inconclusive civil war followed, in which Matilda came near to victory in 1141, was driven back, and at last in the late 1140s left the cause of the English crown to her son.

The young Henry was at Bristol with the Empress's partisans for parts of his boyhood, was tutored by William of Conches and others among the leading masters of the Anglo-Norman world, and crossed to and from Normandy in three increasingly serious expeditions: a small adventure of 1147 at the age of fourteen, an armed visit of 1149 in which he was knighted at Carlisle by his great-uncle David I of Scotland, and the decisive invasion of 1153. In the intervening years his political position had transformed. In 1150 his father invested him with the duchy of Normandy; in September 1151 his father died and he became count of Anjou and Maine; in May 1152, eight weeks after the annulment of her marriage to Louis VII, he married Eleanor of Aquitaine, eleven years his senior, who brought him the duchy of Aquitaine that ran from Poitou to Gascony. The young man who landed in England in January 1153 controlled more of France than the king of France. After a brisk summer campaign in which neither side risked a major battle, the Treaty of Winchester of 6 November 1153 settled the war: Stephen would reign for life, Henry would be his heir, and the surviving son of Stephen, William, would receive only his father's hereditary estates. Stephen died on 25 October 1154; Henry was crowned at Westminster on 19 December.

The first years of his reign were given to dismantling the fortifications of the Anarchy, recalling the alienated royal demesne, restoring the exchequer, and re-establishing the principle that justice was the king's. He worked through a small inner council and through chosen servants of the second rank rather than the great barons: the most consequential of these was Thomas Becket, a Londoner of merchant origin in the service of Archbishop Theobald of Canterbury, whom Henry made chancellor at the beginning of 1155. For the next seven years Becket was the king's instrument across the Angevin lands — in administration, in war, in diplomacy — and his closest personal companion. In June 1162, on Theobald's death, Henry caused Becket to be elected archbishop of Canterbury, expecting an ally on the senior ecclesiastical seat. The miscalculation was the most expensive of his reign. Becket resigned the chancellorship within months, took the obligations of his new office with unexpected seriousness, and opposed the king's programme of reasserting royal jurisdiction over criminous clerks. The Constitutions of Clarendon of January 1164, which Henry had drafted to formalise his side of the dispute, were repudiated by Becket; in November 1164 Becket fled into exile in France and the quarrel was set on its long course.

While the dispute with Becket dragged on, the king's other work continued. He resumed the inquest into landholding begun by his grandfather, reorganised the exchequer under the treasurer Nigel of Ely and his son Richard FitzNigel (whose Dialogue of the Exchequer, written in the 1170s, is the founding text of English administrative history), and produced through repeated assizes — Clarendon in 1166, Northampton in 1176, the assizes of arms and of the forest — the substance of what would later be called the English common law. The grand jury, the standardised writ of right, the systematic use of itinerant royal justices visiting the counties on circuit, the assize of novel disseisin to recover seisin of land lost by force, all date from this reign or were brought into general practice by it. By 1180 the king's courts had displaced the older shire and hundred courts as the working centre of civil justice in England.

In 1170 the dispute with Becket reached its conclusion. A first attempt at reconciliation at Fréteval in July allowed Becket to return to Canterbury in December. He at once excommunicated the bishops who had taken part in the recent coronation of Henry's eldest son as "young king" while he, the archbishop of Canterbury, was in exile. The news reached Henry in Normandy at Christmas. The king's outburst — "What miserable drones and traitors have I nourished and promoted in my household, that they let their lord be treated with such shameful contempt by a low-born clerk!" — was reported in various forms; in some it became "Who will rid me of this turbulent priest?" Four of Henry's household knights, Reginald FitzUrse, Hugh de Morville, William de Tracy, and Richard le Bret, crossed to England and on 29 December 1170 cut down Becket in the north transept of Canterbury Cathedral. The murder shocked Latin Christendom. Becket was canonised within three years; the king did public penance at the tomb in July 1174, walked barefoot through Canterbury, and submitted to a token whipping by the cathedral's monks; the political settlement, the Compromise of Avranches of 1172, conceded the spiritual jurisdiction over criminous clerks but quietly kept much of the practical effect of the Constitutions of Clarendon.

In the year of the murder Henry had also crowned his eldest surviving son, Henry the Young King, as co-king in his own lifetime, on the French model, intending to settle the succession and to associate the young man in the administration. The "Young King" found that his father intended him to administer nothing and resented the dignity without the substance. In March 1173 he fled to the French court; his brothers Richard and Geoffrey joined him; their mother Eleanor, riding to join them in male disguise, was caught by Henry's men. The Great Revolt of 1173–1174, in which Louis VII of France, the king of Scots William the Lion, and a large faction of the Anglo-Norman baronage supported the sons, was defeated by Henry's hard riding and his good luck: William the Lion was captured at Alnwick in July 1174 on the day Henry was doing penance at Canterbury, and contemporaries took the coincidence as the sign of Saint Thomas's pardon. The settlement was generous to the sons in form and harsh in substance. Eleanor was imprisoned in England for the next sixteen years.

The middle years brought further extensions of the empire. In October 1171, on the invitation of the Irish king of Leinster Diarmait MacMurchada and to forestall the Anglo-Norman adventurers who had already crossed under Richard "Strongbow" de Clare, Henry himself landed at Waterford with a substantial army, received the submission of most of the Irish kings, and made the lordship of Ireland a Plantagenet possession. He never returned. The Treaty of Falaise of 1174 made William the Lion his vassal for Scotland, a position later commuted. In 1183 a fresh revolt in Aquitaine by the Young King, in alliance with the southern barons against his brother Richard, was ended only by the Young King's death of dysentery at Martel in June. Geoffrey of Brittany died at a Paris tournament in 1186. Richard and John remained; the question of which would inherit which lands was the dispute of Henry's last three years.

The closing campaign was against his son Richard and the new king of France, Philip Augustus, in alliance. They drove him out of his strongholds in the Loire valley in the summer of 1189. At Le Mans, his birthplace, the city was taken by storm on 12 June; Henry escaped to Chinon. On 4 July, at the conference of Ballan, he submitted to terms that included recognition of Richard as heir; on the next day he learned that John, the last of his sons whose loyalty he had still credited, was among the rebels. He returned to Chinon in a fever and died there on 6 July 1189, aged fifty-six. His body was carried to the abbey of Fontevraud, where Richard, who had been the cause of his last defeat, came to view it. The body, the chroniclers say, bled afresh as Richard entered the chamber. He was buried in the choir of the abbey, the place where Eleanor and Richard would in time be laid beside him.
