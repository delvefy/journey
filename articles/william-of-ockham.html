William of Ockham

c. 1287 – 1347, English Franciscan philosopher, the Venerable Inceptor, master of the razor and of the dissident friars at Munich

William of Ockham was born about 1287 in the village of Ockham in Surrey, a few miles southwest of London. Almost nothing is known of his family. He entered the Order of Friars Minor as a boy, perhaps at the London convent, and was sent to Oxford for his higher studies in the second decade of the fourteenth century. There he encountered the still-fresh teaching of John Duns Scotus, who had lectured at Oxford a generation earlier and whose intricate and brilliant Franciscan metaphysics dominated the schools of the order; he lectured himself on the four books of the Sentences of Peter Lombard between 1317 and 1319, the standard exercise of an advanced theology student. He never proceeded to the doctorate — he completed all the requirements and incepted in name, hence the title Venerabilis Inceptor by which he was known to later schoolmen, the Venerable Beginner — because his examination for the doctorate was interrupted by the proceedings against him at Avignon. He was thus formally only a bachelor of theology, the highest degree he ever held.

His Oxford lectures and the works composed in the few years afterwards — the Ordinatio revision of his Sentences commentary, the Summa Logicae, the seven Quodlibetal Questions, the treatises on physics, predestination, and the Eucharist — constituted the most radical reordering of late scholastic philosophy of his generation. The famous principle entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem, "entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity", was not stated by him in those exact words, but the methodological economy that came to be called Ockham's Razor is genuinely his and pervades his thought. He used it most consequentially against the Scotist apparatus of formal distinctions, common natures, and real universals: there are, on Ockham's view, no universals outside the mind, only individual things and the signs (mental concepts, spoken words, written marks) by which we refer to them. This nominalism, more uncompromising than that of any prior medieval philosopher, restructured the theory of knowledge, the philosophy of language, and (less obviously) the theory of God, on whose ordained but absolutely free power Ockham's entire metaphysical economy depended.

The Oxford chancellor John Lutterell, a hostile Thomist, denounced him at the papal court at Avignon in 1323 for fifty-six propositions he claimed to find in Ockham's Oxford lectures, principally on the Eucharist and on the Trinity. Ockham was summoned to Avignon in 1324 to defend himself. A commission of theologians, mostly Dominicans, examined his works for four years; in 1326 they reported fifty-one of the propositions either heretical or dangerous, but no formal condemnation followed. He remained in the convent of the Friars Minor at Avignon under what was effectively a comfortable house arrest while the cardinals delayed.

While he waited, the great quarrel of his life was prepared for him. The minister general of the Franciscans, Michael of Cesena, had been summoned to Avignon in 1327 to answer for his order's defence of the Spiritual Franciscan doctrine of evangelical poverty. Cesena and the senior Franciscan canonist Bonagratia of Bergamo asked Ockham, who was at Avignon for a different reason, to study the relevant papal bulls and the Franciscan record on poverty. Ockham did so over the winter of 1327–1328 and reported to Cesena that, in his judgement, John XXII's recent bulls (chiefly Ad Conditorem Canonum and Cum Inter Nonnullos) on Franciscan property and on the absolute poverty of Christ were heretical, and that John was therefore a heretic. The three friars resolved to flee.

On the night of 26 May 1328 Cesena, Bonagratia, and Ockham slipped out of Avignon, rode through Provence to the Mediterranean coast, and took ship for Pisa, where Ludwig of Bavaria, having recently been crowned emperor at Rome by Sciarra Colonna, was wintering with his army. The legend that on first presentation to Ludwig Ockham offered the bargain "Imperator, defende me gladio; ego te defendam calamo" — "Emperor, defend me with the sword; I will defend you with the pen" — is recorded only in much later sources, but the substance of the arrangement was as the line suggests. Ockham travelled with Ludwig's court back to Munich the following year and remained there for the rest of his life, in the Franciscan convent of the city, under imperial protection, excommunicated by every successive Avignon pope, refused absolution by all of them.

His writings from Munich are entirely polemical and political. The vast unfinished Dialogus, in three parts and seven books, surveys the question of heresy and the limits of papal authority in scholastic disputation form; the Opus Nonaginta Dierum (Work of Ninety Days), an enormous treatise composed in three months in answer to John XXII's bull Quia Vir Reprobus, defends the Franciscan view of evangelical poverty in line-by-line analysis; De Imperatorum et Pontificum Potestate argues that the imperial dignity is held immediately from God and not through the church; the Eight Questions on the Power of the Pope develops a comprehensive theory of limited papal sovereignty subject to general councils. The political treatises set out a working theory of conciliarism, of the rights of individual Christians within the church, and of secular government as an order of natural law independent of ecclesiastical authority — positions which had immense influence on the later conciliar movement at Constance and Basel and indirectly on the political theology of the Reformation.

The death of John XXII in 1334 did not bring the rehabilitation that Ockham, Cesena, and Bonagratia had hoped for. Benedict XII and Clement VI maintained the excommunications. Michael of Cesena died at Munich in 1342, having drafted his last polemic in articulo mortis. Bonagratia followed in 1347. Ockham survived as the last of the three, kept the great seal of the Franciscans which Cesena had refused to return to Avignon, and in his last years drafted a letter of submission to Clement VI in which he asked for absolution; the letter survives but the church's response, if any, does not. He died at Munich, almost certainly on 9 or 10 April 1347, six months before Ludwig the Bavarian, perhaps already during the early waves of the Black Death which reached Bavaria the following year; he was buried in the choir of the Franciscan church at Munich, where his tombstone was still legible in the seventeenth century.

The intellectual legacy of William of Ockham is among the largest of any medieval thinker. His logic, set out in the Summa Logicae, became the standard logic of the late medieval universities under the name of the via moderna; his nominalism shaped the metaphysics of fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Paris and remained an alternative tradition to Thomism and Scotism into the modern period. His political writings supplied the doctrinal vocabulary of the conciliarists of Constance and Basel and, by way of them, the early Protestant theorists; Luther's claim to have studied as a young man "in the Ockhamist school" was the more literal because Luther's Erfurt teachers were nominalists in his lineage. The Razor — in fact a methodological habit rather than a single thesis — passed through the scientific revolution and remains one of the very few medieval principles still in active use under its medieval name.
