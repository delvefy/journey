Anna Komnene

1083 – c. 1153, Byzantine princess and historian, author of the Alexiad

Anna was born on 1 or 2 December 1083 in the Porphyra chamber of the Great Palace at Constantinople, the first child of the emperor Alexios I Komnenos and Eirene Doukaina. The dignity of porphyrogenita, “born in the purple,” was conferred on imperial children born after their father’s accession, and Anna would invoke it for the rest of her life as proof of a claim to the throne that the events of her father’s reign and her brother’s succession progressively denied her. At her birth she was the only child of the emperor; in the next year, in a gesture of dynastic reconciliation between the Komnenian and Doukas houses, she was betrothed in infancy to Constantine Doukas, the son of the deposed Michael VII Doukas, a boy about ten years her senior who at that moment was Alexios’s designated heir. Constantine’s name appeared on the imperial coinage of the 1080s alongside her father’s; Anna in her own writing called him “wholly beautiful, a boy of golden hair and grey eyes,” and she remained attached to his memory long after his death.

The succession arrangement was overturned in 1087 with the birth of her brother John II Komnenos, who at four was elevated as co-emperor and displaced Constantine in the imperial titulature. Constantine died, probably in the early 1090s; Anna was left an heiress in name with no claim. Her education from infancy was the best the empire could give: tutors taught her the Greek grammarians and rhetoricians; she read, secretly at first against her mother’s reluctance, the philosophical works of Aristotle, Plato, Plotinus; she studied medicine and astronomy. Her later writing makes constant unforced allusion to Homer, Thucydides, Polybius, Aristophanes, and the Septuagint, in the high classicising Greek of the Komnenian renaissance.

About 1097, when she was fourteen, Anna was married to Nikephoros Bryennios the Younger, a member of the great Macedonian military family of Adrianople whose grandfather Bryennios the Elder had been blinded by Anna’s own father at the rebellion of 1078. The marriage was conceived by Alexios as a reconciliation between two houses with reason to hate each other; in fact the two young people were intellectually well-matched and personally devoted. Bryennios served Alexios as a soldier and a diplomat through the 1100s and 1110s, accompanied him on most of the campaigns of those years, and would write at his wife’s urging the unfinished Material for a History, an account of the imperial succession crisis of the 1070s and the rise of the Komnenoi which broke off about 1079. Anna bore him four children — Alexios, John, Eirene, and Maria — and lived with him in the imperial palace.

The crisis of her life was the death of her father in August 1118. Anna and her mother had, in the testimony of Anna’s own narrative and that of the rhetor John Zonaras, attempted in the final days to obtain from the dying emperor a confirmation of Bryennios as his successor in place of John. Alexios refused. John II, alerted by the silver ring his father had pressed into his hand, slipped out of the palace, secured the acclamation of the army camped outside the city, and entered Constantinople as emperor. Anna and Eirene retired to the Kecharitomene monastery, the foundation of Eirene’s building. A year later, in 1119, the mother and daughter were involved in a fresh plot to overthrow John on the occasion of his march into Bithynia; the plot was uncovered, partly through Bryennios’s refusal to play the part of usurper assigned to him by his women. Anna is reported to have declared in fury that “nature had erred and given the male organs to the wrong person.” John forgave her, in the relaxed manner of his temperament: her property was confiscated for a time and returned, her access to the court was forfeited but never her household, her husband remained on the imperial council and accompanied John on his Cilician campaigns through the 1130s.

Bryennios died in 1137, on his return from one such campaign. Anna was fifty-four, childless of further plans, and confined in spirit if not in fact to her mother’s monastery. She turned to her husband’s unfinished history and to the project that would become her own work, the Alexiad. The book is in fifteen books of formal classicising Greek prose, more than four hundred pages in modern edition, and recounts the life of her father from his early generalship under the Doukai through his thirty-seven-year reign to his death — with a digressive openness about Anna’s own loves, hatreds, regrets, and judgments which is unique in Byzantine literature and is the chief reason the book continues to be read. She wrote it through the 1140s into the next decade, completing the last book about 1148. The work draws on her father’s state archives, to which she had recovered access by her age and her brother’s forgiveness; on her husband’s notes; on the recollections of veterans of the Norman and Crusading wars whom she interviewed in her old age; and on her own girlhood memory of the court. The portrait of Bohemond — whom she met at Constantinople in April 1097 when she was thirteen — is famously vivid: a man taller and broader than any other of the western princes, with cropped hair and a bluish eye, “not stern as that of the savage Achilles but with a touch of the comical in it.” The narrative of the First Crusade from the Byzantine side is preserved nowhere else in such detail and from no other angle.

The Alexiad is the longest history written by a woman in the medieval world. Its conventions are entirely those of the male Greek historians on whom Anna had been raised, Thucydides above all; its tone is high, its judgments often peremptory, its theology orthodox without devotion. Anna’s opinions are everywhere visible: the Latins are by nature treacherous and avaricious; Bohemond is admirable and detestable; the heretic Bogomils, whose leader her father burned in the Hippodrome, are charlatans; her brother is barely named — he is “the present emperor” or “the imperial brother,” and the dynastic struggle of 1118 is told in such roundabout language that the reader is told what to think only by what Anna refuses to say.

She died about 1153, at seventy. Her contemporary George Tornikes preserved the funeral oration that gives most of the chronology of her later life: he describes a woman of unbroken intellectual life into old age, surrounded by scholars and physicians in her monastic apartments, still discussing Aristotle and Galen, the last porphyrogenita of her father’s house to live into the reigns of her grandnephew Manuel I. The Alexiad circulated in a small number of manuscripts and was not edited from Greek into a printed text until the seventeenth century, when scholars of the early Byzantine renaissance rediscovered it. It has remained since the principal record of the Komnenian recovery, and is read now both as a source for the events her father shaped and as the personal voice of a woman whose own claim to those events history had denied.
