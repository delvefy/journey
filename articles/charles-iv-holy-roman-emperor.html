Charles IV, Holy Roman Emperor

1316 – 1378, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor, founder of the University of Prague, author of the Golden Bull of 1356

Charles was born at Prague on 14 May 1316, the eldest son of John of Luxembourg, King of Bohemia, and Elizabeth of the Přemyslid royal house of Bohemia. He was christened Wenceslaus, after his mother's saintly ancestor; the name Charles he took at confirmation in Paris seven years later in honour of his godfather and great-uncle, Charles IV of France. The choice of the French name signalled, in advance, the cultural orientation of his life: he was raised in France from the age of seven until sixteen, was educated by Pierre Roger, the future Pope Clement VI, at the court of Charles IV of France, married at fourteen the French princess Blanche of Valois, and emerged from his French upbringing a polyglot of an unusual sort — fluent in French, German, Latin, Italian, and the Czech of his mother, which he had to relearn after his return.

His political life began in 1331 when his father sent him into Italy at fifteen to administer the Luxembourg territories there; he had a six-year apprenticeship in the running of a difficult Italian Ghibelline coalition, lost most of it, and returned to Bohemia in 1333 to take up the margraviate of Moravia, in effect the regency of his father's Bohemian kingdom. John of Luxembourg, by then blind, was in Bohemia rarely; Charles, working from Prague, applied himself to the rebuilding of the royal demesne, the restoration of order in the country, and the patient cultivation of the diocese of Prague, which he sought to detach from its dependence on Mainz. He succeeded in 1344: Pope Clement VI — his old teacher in Paris — raised Prague to the status of a metropolitan see in its own right, with the new bishoprics of Olomouc and Litomyšl as suffragans. The independent Bohemian church was the work of one paragraph in a bull, and was as definitive a step in the formation of the Czech kingdom as any other single act of the century.

In July 1346 the imperial electors, dissatisfied with the Emperor Louis IV of Bavaria, elected Charles King of the Romans in opposition. Two months later, on 26 August 1346, his father John was killed at the battle of Crécy fighting in the French line against the army of Edward III, despite being completely blind; his ostrich-plume crest was taken by the Black Prince and adopted as the badge of the Prince of Wales. Charles, who had also been at Crécy and was lightly wounded, succeeded his father as King of Bohemia the same day. Louis IV's death the following year left him uncontested King of the Romans; he was crowned at Aachen in 1349, in Milan with the Iron Crown of Lombardy in 1355, and emperor at Rome by Pope Innocent VI on Easter Sunday 1355.

His reign in the Empire is associated above all with the Golden Bull of 1356, the constitutional document that fixed the procedure for electing the King of the Romans by seven prince-electors — three ecclesiastical (the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier) and four lay (the King of Bohemia, the Count Palatine of the Rhine, the Duke of Saxony, and the Margrave of Brandenburg) — and that defined the rights, privileges, and order of precedence of those electorates. The Bull regularised what had been customary, granted to the electors near-sovereign rights in their own lands, and stood as the basic constitutional law of the Empire for nearly four hundred and fifty years, until it was overtaken by Napoleon's dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806.

His central concern, however, was always Bohemia. He made of Prague a capital of imperial dignity, founding in 1348 the University of Prague — the first university north of the Alps and east of the Rhine, the third oldest in central Europe after Paris and Bologna — with the first arts faculty staffed largely by Paris-trained masters. He began the construction of St Vitus Cathedral within the Hradčany castle that same year, employing the French architect Matthias of Arras and after his death the south German Peter Parler, whose triforium gallery of busts is among the great surviving works of late Gothic sculpture; the cathedral was completed only in 1929 but its essential structure is fourteenth-century. He laid out the New Town of Prague in 1348, the largest planned urban extension in late medieval Europe, doubling the area of the city; he rebuilt the Charles Bridge over the Vltava after the destruction of its Romanesque predecessor in 1342, with the foundation stone laid at 5:31 on 9 July 1357 in a moment chosen for its numerological symmetry. He founded the Karlštejn Castle outside Prague in 1348 as the repository of the imperial regalia and of his own collection of relics, the largest of any reigning prince of the fourteenth century.

His Italian policy was conducted with deliberate restraint after the bruising lesson of his youth. He visited Italy twice as emperor, in 1354 to 1355 and in 1368, taking what was offered him in homage and revenue and avoiding the entanglement in factional Italian politics that had wrecked his predecessors. He extracted from the visits enough money to fund the works in Prague but pursued no Italian programme of his own; the cities understood him as a more careful ruler than they were used to seeing from the north and rewarded the carefulness by allowing it.

He married four times. His first wife Blanche of Valois died in 1348. His second was Anne of the Palatinate; his third Anna of Schweidnitz, by whom he had Wenceslaus, his eventual successor in Bohemia and in the Empire; his fourth Elizabeth of Pomerania, by whom he had Sigismund, the future Emperor. He fathered eleven children; he negotiated marriages for them across the European map — his daughter Anne of Bohemia to Richard II of England in 1382; another daughter Margaret to Louis I of Hungary's son; sons-in-law for the others among the great German, Polish, and Austrian houses. The pattern was the unobtrusive consolidation of a network of dynastic relationships favourable to the Luxembourg house and to the Czech crown which was its base.

The Black Death, which entered the Empire in 1348 to 1350, took perhaps a third of the population of his lands but spared, by a peculiar pattern of the epidemic's path, the city of Prague almost entirely. Charles took the deliverance as providential and as an occasion for public foundation: the Marian column on the Old Town Square, the new churches of Týn and Our Lady of the Snows, the great Carmelite foundation of Mariazell on the Austrian frontier, were among the architectural fruits of those plague years.

He died at Prague on 29 November 1378, sixty-two years old. He was buried in St Vitus Cathedral in a tomb of his own design. His Latin autobiography, the Vita Caroli, written in his sixties and one of the earliest surviving works of medieval royal autobiography, presented his life as a moral example to his children; the text was widely read in central Europe through the fifteenth century and circulated in Czech, German, and Latin manuscripts.

The Czech historiographical tradition has called him "the father of his country" (otec vlasti) since the chronicler Beneš Krabice of Weitmile in his lifetime. The judgement is contemporary as well as long retrospective: of the Holy Roman Emperors of the late Middle Ages he was the only one who made of his hereditary realm a great kingdom, with a working university, a metropolitan church, a planned capital, a coherent administration, and a cultural identity that survived the eclipse of his dynasty.
