Otto IV

c. 1175 – 1218, Welf king of the Romans, Holy Roman Emperor, the loser of Bouvines

Otto was born about 1175, in all likelihood at Argentan in Normandy, the second surviving son of Henry the Lion, then in exile from the empire at the Plantagenet court, and of Matilda Plantagenet, the daughter of Henry II of England and Eleanor of Aquitaine. He grew up between Argentan, Caen, Westminster, and Winchester — the residence shifted with his parents' movements during the years of his father's first exile from 1182 to 1185, and again after the second exile of 1188 he remained in England with his Plantagenet uncles when his elder brother and parents returned to Brunswick. The first language of the household was the Norman French of his mother and her court; he learned German only in adolescence; his Latin was passable but never that of a man trained for a chancery. His maternal uncle Richard the Lionheart, who took the English crown in 1189 when Otto was about fourteen, brought him into a close affection that is recorded in the chronicles of both kingdoms. In 1196 Richard formally invested him with the county of Poitou, a great fief of the Angevin lands in the south of France, where Otto, still in his early twenties, governed for the next two years between campaigns against Philip Augustus on the Norman frontier.

The course of his life was abruptly diverted in the autumn of 1197. The Emperor Henry VI, the elder son of Frederick Barbarossa, who had united the German empire with the Norman kingdom of Sicily through his marriage to Constance of Hauteville and had been preparing a hereditary monarchy on a new model, died at Messina on 28 September 1197 at the age of thirty-two, leaving an infant son Frederick in Sicily and no political settlement in Germany. The Hohenstaufen princes turned to Henry VI's younger brother Philip, duke of Swabia, who was elected and crowned king of the Romans at Mainz on 8 March 1198 with the assent of the southern majority. The Welf faction of the north German princes, led by the archbishop of Cologne Adolf of Altena, summoned the Plantagenet party in Otto's person to oppose him. The anti-Hohenstaufen group elected Otto at Cologne on 9 June 1198 and crowned him at Aachen on 12 July, in the cathedral where he was born to be raised on the Carolingian throne but with the wrong regalia (Philip held the original imperial crown and sword), so that the legality of the election remained contested.

The double election produced a German civil war of ten years. Philip, with the support of most of the Swabian, Bavarian, and Saxon nobility and of the great ministerial families of the empire, held the field in the south and centre; Otto, sustained by English subsidies from his uncle Richard and after Richard's death in 1199 from his other uncle King John, held the Rhineland and parts of north Germany. Pope Innocent III, presented with the dispute as arbiter from 1200, weighed the candidates and in his great deliberation Venerabilem of 1202 decided in Otto's favour, on the grounds, formally, of his closer attention to papal interests in central Italy and, less formally, of his more limited prospects of effective imperial rule in Germany. The papal endorsement did not produce a military decision. By 1206, after a series of campaigns in which Philip steadily gained the upper hand, Otto was confined to the Welf lands around Brunswick and reduced to negotiating his abdication. The peace conference that was to seal it was set for the summer of 1208 at Bamberg.

On the afternoon of 21 June 1208, in the bishop's palace at Bamberg, Philip of Swabia was sitting after a long meal in the company of a few of his household when Count Palatine Otto of Wittelsbach, a Bavarian noble whose betrothal to one of Philip's daughters Philip had recently broken off, walked in unannounced and cut his throat with a single stroke. The murder, the work of personal vengeance and not of political conspiracy, removed at one blow the only effective Hohenstaufen claimant to the German throne. The princes met at Frankfurt that autumn and recognised Otto IV as the unopposed king of the Romans; he completed the gesture of reconciliation by betrothing himself to Beatrice, the elder daughter of the murdered Philip. Innocent III invited him to Rome for the imperial coronation, and on 4 October 1209, in St Peter's basilica, the pope set the imperial crown on his head — the first imperial coronation in nearly thirty years.

The break with Innocent followed almost at once. Otto, having received the empire on the papal terms, set about applying the imperial title to lands the papacy regarded as its own. He claimed the central Italian territories Innocent had recovered from the Hohenstaufen during the long imperial schism; he demanded the homage of the Tuscan cities; in November 1210 he crossed the lower Garigliano with an army into the kingdom of Sicily, intending to depose his ward and predecessor Frederick II, and overran most of the mainland portion of the Norman kingdom over the winter. Innocent excommunicated him at the Lateran on 18 November 1210 and began to look about for an alternative. The Hohenstaufen candidate — Frederick II, the orphan ward of Innocent's own first years as pope, then in Palermo — was now sixteen, married, and willing.

The German princes most opposed to Otto's southern programme met at Nuremberg in September 1211 and elected Frederick king of the Romans in absentia. Frederick crossed the Alps in March 1212, evaded Otto's patrols in Lombardy, reached Constance and the Rhineland by way of Chur, and was crowned at Mainz on 9 December 1212. Otto, recalled from southern Italy by the news, turned to defend himself. His own marriage to Beatrice of Swabia was at last celebrated at Nordhausen on 22 July 1212; she died of a sudden illness at Brunswick on 11 August, three weeks later. In May 1214 he married Maria of Brabant, a marriage of political alliance rather than affection. The civil war continued into the summer of 1214, with Otto's hold on the empire steadily eroding everywhere except in his old Welf base.

The strategy he and his English uncle John conceived for the recovery of his position was a coordinated invasion of France from north and south. John, denied his last hope of regaining the lost Plantagenet lands by any other means, supplied the money and led an army into Poitou in the spring of 1214; Otto, with Flemish and Brabantine and German contingents, was to invade from the north-east and join him after the conquest of Paris. The plan failed at Bouvines on Sunday 27 July 1214, on a flat plain between Lille and Tournai, where Philip Augustus of France met the imperial-Flemish army with a rapidly assembled royal levy. The battle was ferocious and short. Philip himself was unhorsed in the press; the count of Flanders and the count of Boulogne were captured; Otto, his standard cut from its pole and his horse killed under him, was rescued by his Saxon household knights and got away with only a few attendants north along the Schelde. The imperial banner with the dragon and the eagle was sent to Philip Augustus's tent as a trophy and on to Paris. John's southern army, hearing the news, retreated to England to face the political collapse that would produce Magna Carta the following summer.

Bouvines settled the empire. Otto withdrew to his Welf inheritance, where his brother Henry V, the count palatine, and his half-Plantagenet household sheltered him. At the Fourth Lateran Council in November 1215 he was formally deposed; on 25 July 1215 Frederick II, twenty-one years old, had been re-crowned in due form at Aachen. Otto attempted a final campaign in 1216 around Bremen, retired to the castle of Harzburg in the Brunswick country, and lived out his last years in increasing illness and the practice of pronounced personal austerity — the chroniclers record severe penitential discipline in his last months. He died at Harzburg on 19 May 1218, aged about forty-two, attended by his confessor and his brothers. He was buried at the cathedral of Brunswick, in the choir of St Blaise's beside his father and mother, the only emperor of the Welf line.
