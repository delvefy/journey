Pontius Pilate

Roman prefect of Judea, AD 26–36

Pontius Pilate served as the fifth Roman prefect of Judea from about AD 26 to 36, a tenure of ten years under the emperor Tiberius that ended in recall and obscurity. He is the only Roman governor of Judea whose existence is confirmed by a contemporary inscription — the so-called Pilate stone, discovered at Caesarea Maritima in 1961, which bears his name and title, praefectus Iudaeae — and his administration is referred to by the Jewish writers Philo of Alexandria and Flavius Josephus, by the Roman historian Tacitus, and at greatest length, of course, by the four New Testament gospels. The convergence of these sources lets us see him with unusual clarity for a minor Roman official, and what they show is a hard, uncompromising governor who learned only slowly the cost of provoking the people he ruled.

Of his life before Judea almost nothing is known. The name Pontius indicates a Samnite gentile origin and a long-standing equestrian family in central Italy; the cognomen Pilatus may refer to a javelin and suggest a military background. He probably arrived in Judea with the patronage of Lucius Aelius Sejanus, the praetorian prefect and effective ruler of Rome in the late twenties and early thirties under an absentee Tiberius. The hypothesis fits the dates and the man's behavior: a hard-liner installed by a hard-liner.

Almost as soon as he took office he sent his troops into Jerusalem at night carrying military standards adorned with images of the emperor, and quartered them in the Antonia fortress beside the Temple. Jewish law forbade graven images, and the city woke to an outrage. A delegation of Judeans followed Pilate to his coastal seat at Caesarea and lay prostrate around his residence for five days, refusing to move. On the sixth day he assembled them in the stadium and ringed them with soldiers who drew their swords on a signal; the demonstrators bared their necks and said they would rather die than see their law violated. Pilate, Josephus reports, was so astonished at the depth of their feeling that he ordered the standards withdrawn to Caesarea. It was the only one of his confrontations he resolved by yielding.

He raised the temperature again, perhaps a few years later, by seizing money from the Temple treasury to fund an aqueduct that would bring water from the Bethlehem hills into Jerusalem. When the inevitable protest erupted, Philo says, he had soldiers in civilian dress mingle with the crowd carrying clubs concealed under their cloaks; on a signal they fell upon the demonstrators and beat many to death. The aqueduct was finished. A second incident, briefly mentioned by Luke, involved Galilean pilgrims whose blood Pilate had mingled with their sacrifices in the Temple court. Philo, writing within the next decade, called him naturally inflexible, a blend of self-will and relentlessness, and accused him of briberies, insults, robberies, outrages and wanton injuries, executions without trial constantly repeated, and ceaseless, supremely grievous cruelty.

It was during the great Passover of about AD 30 or 33 that Pilate's most famous case came before him. The gospels agree on the broad sequence: the Sanhedrin, led by the high priest Caiaphas, brought the Galilean teacher Jesus of Nazareth to the prefect's residence and pressed for a death sentence on the political charge of claiming to be a king of the Jews. Pilate, interviewing the prisoner privately, found no clear case for sedition. He sought to release him under a Passover amnesty, only to have the crowd demand the bandit Barabbas instead; he sent him to Herod Antipas, who returned him; he tried scourging in the hope that the lesser punishment would satisfy the accusers. When at last the cry went up that to release this man would be no friend of Caesar, Pilate yielded — perhaps remembering, as Tiberius's man, that Sejanus had fallen in Rome the previous autumn and that no procurator could afford a letter to the imperial archives accusing him of failing to suppress a self-proclaimed king. He washed his hands, ordered the crucifixion, and had a notice fixed to the cross in three languages reading, Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews. It is the line of these proceedings — the symbolic handwashing, the wife's dream, the question what is truth — that has fixed Pilate in Christian art and liturgy as the supreme image of irresolute power.

His end came shortly afterward. In AD 36 he intercepted an armed gathering of Samaritans climbing Mount Gerizim under a self-styled prophet who had promised to reveal vessels hidden by Moses. Pilate's troops scattered them, captured many, and executed the leaders. The Samaritans complained to Lucius Vitellius, the legate of Syria, who ordered Pilate to Rome to account for himself. By the time he arrived, Tiberius was dead, and Pilate disappears from the historical record. Eusebius, writing nearly three centuries later, reports a tradition that he was banished and committed suicide; the later Christian apocrypha give him every imaginable fate, from execution to repentance and canonization in the Ethiopian and Coptic churches.

What survives is the act. Pilate is the man who signed the warrant of the crucifixion, the only secular figure named in the great creeds of Christendom — suffered under Pontius Pilate — and the standing emblem of the moment at which earthly power, faced with a question it had not asked for, washed its hands and turned away. He governed a small province for ten years and was recalled. The single afternoon in Jerusalem on which he passed sentence on a Galilean prophet has outlived every aqueduct and standard and uprising of his career.
