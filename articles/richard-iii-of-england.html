Richard III of England

1452 – 1485, last Yorkist King of England, the king under the car park, killed at Bosworth

Richard was born on 2 October 1452 at Fotheringhay Castle in Northamptonshire, the eleventh child and youngest surviving son of Richard, Duke of York, and Cecily Neville. He was eight years old when his father and his brother Edmund were killed at Wakefield on 30 December 1460; the head of the duke of York, in a paper crown, was set on Micklegate Bar at York. His eldest surviving brother Edward proclaimed himself King Edward IV three months later, and the boy Richard, with his sister Margaret and brother George, was for safety sent to the Low Countries until the Yorkist victory at Towton on Palm Sunday 1461 made it possible to bring them home.

He grew up in the household of his cousin Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, the Kingmaker, at Middleham in Wensleydale, where he was schooled in arms and in the administration of a great northern estate alongside Warwick's daughters Isabel and Anne. The relationship would later turn complicated. When Warwick rebelled against Edward IV in 1469 and 1470, Richard, alone of the king's brothers, stayed loyal; he joined his brother in Burgundian exile in the Low Countries and returned with him in March 1471. He fought at Barnet on Easter morning, where Warwick was killed in the rout, and at Tewkesbury on 4 May, where, according to most contemporary accounts, he commanded the Yorkist vanguard. The seventeen-year-old Edward of Lancaster, only son of Henry VI, was killed in the battle or in its immediate aftermath. Henry VI himself died in the Tower of London on the night of 21 May, almost certainly murdered; the order, as everyone understood, was Edward IV's, but the act, by long tradition, was Richard's.

In 1472, against the wishes of his brother George, Duke of Clarence, who had married the elder of Warwick's daughters, Richard married the younger, Anne Neville, then sixteen. The marriage gave him a half share of the great Neville inheritance in the north and was the basis of his career; it was also, by some accounts, a personal match, since they had grown up together at Middleham, and at the time of the wedding Anne was already a widow, Edward of Lancaster having been killed at Tewkesbury six months before.

For the rest of Edward IV's reign Richard was, by deliberate distance, a northern viceroy. As Duke of Gloucester he was lord of Middleham, of Sheriff Hutton, of Penrith, of Barnard Castle; he was warden of the West March and, from 1480, in effective command of the war against Scotland, which he conducted with characteristic efficiency. In August 1482 he took Berwick-upon-Tweed, recovering for England a town lost since 1461 and not lost again. He kept clear of the Woodville faction at court; he supported Clarence's execution in 1478 only reluctantly; he was, by the standards of late medieval royal brothers, conspicuously loyal.

Edward IV died unexpectedly at Westminster on 9 April 1483. His son Edward V was twelve. Richard had been named protector by his brother's deathbed wish; the Woodvilles, who controlled the young king and the council in London, moved to forestall the protectorate. On 30 April at Northampton, Richard, riding south with Henry Stafford, Duke of Buckingham, arrested Earl Rivers — Anthony Woodville, the king's maternal uncle and guardian. He took custody of his nephew at Stony Stratford and rode with him into London on 4 May. Within a month Elizabeth Woodville and her younger son, the nine-year-old Richard of York, had fled to sanctuary at Westminster Abbey. They were prevailed upon to surrender the boy on 16 June; he joined his brother in the royal apartments of the Tower of London, the residence in which English kings traditionally lodged before coronation.

On 22 June the preacher Ralph Shaa, at Paul's Cross, declared the children of Edward IV illegitimate on the grounds of their father's alleged earlier pre-contract with Eleanor Butler. Parliament, in the act later known as Titulus Regius, set out the claim; the throne was offered to Richard as the next legitimate heir. He accepted on 26 June and was crowned with his queen Anne on 6 July 1483 in Westminster Abbey, a coronation conspicuous for the attendance of almost every peer of the realm.

By the end of the summer the two boys had disappeared from the upper rooms of the Tower. They were never seen again. What happened to them remains the most contentious question in English history; the contemporary rumour, persistent and probably correct, was that they had been murdered by their uncle's order. The disappearance broke Richard's position with the political nation: in October Buckingham, Richard's chief lieutenant in the usurpation, rebelled in alliance with Margaret Beaufort and the exiled Henry Tudor; the rebellion collapsed in flooding rain on the Severn and Buckingham was executed at Salisbury on 2 November. But the political cost was permanent. Many of the men who had ridden with Richard in the spring would never ride with him again.

His brief reign was administratively careful. He held a parliament early in 1484 that confirmed Titulus Regius, established the court of requests, regulated land law, and gave English subjects the right to bail. He worked in person in the council and in the courts; he travelled assiduously; he was, on the surviving evidence, a competent and largely conscientious king. But two private catastrophes overtook him in 1484 and 1485. His only legitimate son, Edward of Middleham, ten years old, died at Middleham in April 1484. His queen Anne died of tuberculosis at Westminster on 16 March 1485, on a day on which contemporary chroniclers noted an eclipse of the sun. Rumour at once held that Richard had poisoned her in order to marry his niece Elizabeth of York; he was driven, in an unprecedented step for a king, to deny the rumour in public at the Hall of the Knights of St John at Clerkenwell.

In August Henry Tudor, descended from Catherine of Valois by Owen Tudor and from John of Gaunt by Margaret Beaufort, landed at Milford Haven with a small force from France. The two armies met on 22 August at Bosworth in Leicestershire. The battle turned on the defection of Sir William Stanley, brother of Henry's stepfather Lord Stanley; Richard, refusing to flee, charged at Henry's standard with his household knights and was killed in the press, the last English king to die in battle. His body, stripped naked, was thrown across a horse and carried to Leicester, and buried without honour at Greyfriars. The grave was forgotten; the friary was suppressed under his successors and built over. In 2012 a skeleton, identified by mitochondrial DNA, was recovered from beneath a council car park in Leicester. He was reburied in Leicester Cathedral in 2015.

The dynasty he had attempted to refound did not survive him. The Yorkist line was effectively extinct in the male line within two years. The Tudor line that supplanted him descended in part from his own family, since Henry VII married his niece Elizabeth of York, and ruled England for the next 118 years. Richard's reputation went on after his death: blackened by the Tudor historians, satirised by Sir Thomas More, dramatised by Shakespeare into the archetypal political villain of the English stage, defended in the eighteenth century by Horace Walpole and in the twentieth by a society of partisans formed in his name. The historical Richard III remains a difficult man to recover from beneath the layered images of him: a competent northern lord, an effective brother to Edward IV, an able administrator, who in the high summer of 1483 made a usurpation that destroyed his line and his name.
