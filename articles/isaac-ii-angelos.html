Isaac II Angelos

1156 – 1204, Byzantine emperor, founder of the Angelos dynasty

Isaac was born in September 1156, the second son of the sebastos Andronikos Angelos and Euphrosyne Kastamonitissa. The Angeloi were a Philadelphian family of modest provincial standing who had risen at court because Andronikos’s mother Theodora was the daughter of the emperor Alexios I Komnenos; Isaac was therefore the great-grandson of the founder of the Komnenian restoration on the female line. He grew up at the court of his cousin Manuel I, served as a soldier in the Anatolian campaigns of the 1170s, and at the death of Manuel in 1180 settled into the quiet life of a courtier in the regency of the empress Maria of Antioch. The seizure of power by his great-cousin Andronikos in 1182 left him initially unmolested; under the suspicious reign of the old emperor in 1183–1185, however, Isaac came to be regarded by Andronikos’s informers as a likely centre of disaffection, and on 11 September 1185 the prefect of the city, Stephen Hagiochristophorites, came with a small detachment of soldiers to his house in the quarter of the Theotokos Hodegetria to arrest him.

What followed was unplanned and decisive. Isaac drew a sword on Hagiochristophorites and cut him down on his own threshold, threw on a riding cloak over his courtier’s tunic, and rode through the streets of the capital to Hagia Sophia, where he claimed sanctuary at the great altar. The crowd of the cathedral, who had been waiting almost a year for such a moment, acclaimed him emperor on the spot; the patriarch Basil Kamateros performed the coronation in the late afternoon; by evening the city was in revolt. Andronikos, returning from a small palace on the Asian shore, found the gates of the capital closed against him and was the next day torn apart by the mob he had once relied on. Isaac, twenty-nine years old and a moderate man by temperament, found himself emperor of a state in collapse.

The Norman war was the immediate crisis. William II of Sicily had just sacked Thessalonica with extraordinary brutality; the army of his general Baldwin was advancing on Constantinople. Isaac sent his uncle Alexios Branas, an experienced commander, with what remained of the field army; Branas defeated the Sicilians at Mosynopolis in November 1185 and again at Demetritzes a few days later, recovered Thessalonica, and drove the Sicilians back to the Adriatic. The victory restored a brief euphoria at court; the more permanent damage to the empire was already done. In the same months a tax revolt in Bulgaria and Vlachia — on the steep slopes north of the Balkan mountains — turned under the leadership of the brothers Asen and Peter into a national uprising. The campaign Isaac mounted against them in 1186 failed in the mountain passes; his second campaign of 1187 failed in turn. By the end of his reign the second Bulgarian Empire was a fact on his northern frontier and the Byzantine reconquest of the late twelfth century had been reversed.

His domestic position was undermined from the start by the man who had saved Thessalonica. Alexios Branas, returning with the army from his victory in 1187, marched on Constantinople and proclaimed himself emperor; Isaac was rescued by the chance presence in the city of Conrad of Montferrat, the Latin marquess in town to marry Isaac’s sister Theodora, who at the head of a small body of Italian crusaders broke Branas’s rebellion outside the walls in a single afternoon and personally killed Branas in the mêlée. Conrad sailed for the Holy Land a few months later and would in 1190 be elected king of Jerusalem before his murder by Assassins outside Tyre. Isaac, in gratitude and in need of an alliance, married in 1186 the ten-year-old Margaret of Hungary, daughter of his protege King Bela III, and through the rest of his reign relied on the Hungarian alliance for whatever stability his northern frontier retained.

The Third Crusade passed through his empire in 1189–1190, in the largest of the three great armies, that of the emperor Frederick Barbarossa. Isaac negotiated badly. Suspicious of Frederick’s ultimate ambitions, in secret correspondence with Saladin, he arrested the German envoys and demanded a treaty of subordination from a man who was at that moment the most powerful sovereign in Europe and was about to be reinforced by sixty thousand soldiers. Frederick took Adrianople by storm in November 1189, threatened to march on Constantinople, and forced Isaac in February 1190 to the Treaty of Adrianople, by which the crusader army was ferried across the Hellespont and provisioned at imperial expense. Frederick drowned in a river of Cilicia in June 1190; the German army dispersed; the humiliation of the Byzantine court remained. The same years saw the loss of Cyprus to a kinsman, Isaac Komnenos, who declared an independent rule on the island and lost it in 1191 to Richard I of England.

The reign collapsed in the spring of 1195. Isaac’s elder brother Alexios, who had been kept in modest court office for a decade, accompanied the emperor on a hunting expedition near Kypsella in Thrace in April; while the emperor was on horseback in a forest stand, Alexios assembled the guards around the imperial tent, had himself acclaimed emperor in his brother’s absence, and arrested Isaac on his return. The deposed emperor was blinded by surgeons in the camp tent and confined under guard. He was forty-two. His brother Alexios III took the throne on 8 April 1195 and ruled in his stead for eight years.

The end of Isaac’s story belongs to the Fourth Crusade. His son Alexios IV, then thirteen, was kept under house arrest at Constantinople for the first years of his uncle’s reign; in 1201 he escaped, made his way to the West, presented himself at the court of his German brother-in-law Philip of Swabia, and there persuaded the leaders of the assembling crusade — the Doge of Venice Enrico Dandolo, the marquess Boniface of Montferrat, and a council of French and Italian princes — to divert their expedition to Constantinople in return for a vast subsidy and the reunion of the Greek and Latin churches. The crusader fleet arrived in the Bosphorus in June 1203; Alexios III fled the capital with the treasury and a daughter on the night of 17 July; the next morning the citizens of Constantinople went down to the prison of the Anemas tower, broke open the gate, brought out the blind Isaac, and acclaimed him emperor for the second time. He was crowned again in Hagia Sophia in the same week, with his son Alexios IV as co-emperor; he was forty-seven and could not see his own court. The reign was short and miserable. The Latin army outside the walls demanded the payments that had been promised; the populace of the city, blamed for the diversion of the crusade, turned against the restored Angeloi; Alexios IV was strangled by a popular revolt on 28 January 1204. Isaac died a few days later, of grief or fever or both. Within ten weeks the Fourth Crusade had sacked the capital.
