Drusus the Elder

38 BC – 9 BC · Roman general, brother of Tiberius, conqueror of Germany

Nero Claudius Drusus, called Drusus the Elder to distinguish him from his son and his grandson of the same name, was born in early 38 BC at Rome, three days before his mother Livia Drusilla was transferred from her first husband Tiberius Claudius Nero to her new and overwhelming one, the young Octavian, the future Augustus. He grew up in the household of his stepfather; he died at twenty-nine in a campaigning accident on the river Saale in central Germany, leaving behind a brief but spectacular military career, two infant sons who would shape the next half-century of Roman history — the future emperor Claudius and the great popular general Germanicus — and a posthumous reputation as the most promising and best-loved of the early Julio-Claudians.

His paternity was, even at his own birth, a subject of comment at Rome. The wedding of Livia and Octavian three days after Drusus's delivery left it unclear under whose name the child should be raised; both husbands were Claudians of the same line, and Octavian, in a public gesture, sent the infant back to the household of Tiberius Claudius Nero, where he was brought up with his elder brother Tiberius until their father's death three years later. The two boys were then transferred to the imperial house, raised by their mother and stepfather, and from that time on were treated as part of Augustus's own family.

He grew up close to his older brother Tiberius and far closer to his stepfather than Tiberius ever managed to be. Augustus, who had no son of his own and a daughter he loved without quite knowing what to do with, found in Drusus an open and warm temperament that he could not extract from the colder, more reserved Tiberius; the surviving correspondence shows him addressing Drusus with terms of affection used for no one else. Drusus repaid the affection with an open admiration that was reportedly tinged with old-republican sympathy — Suetonius reports the rumor that he intended, after his campaigns were over, to ask his stepfather to restore the Republic, a sentiment Augustus is said to have heard with patient amusement.

His career was a series of escalating commands on the northern frontier. In 15 BC, at twenty-three, he and Tiberius were sent jointly into the Alps, where in a single summer's campaign they subdued the Raeti and the Vindelici, brought the entire arc of the Alpine peoples under direct Roman administration, and added the new provinces of Raetia and Noricum to the empire. The achievement was celebrated by Horace in two of his most famous odes — the address to the elder Drusus comparing him to a young eagle thrust from the nest, and the praise of the joint victory of the brothers in the same year. Augustus came north to meet them; the new provinces were organized within a single year.

From 12 BC, Drusus was given command of the Rhine armies. He launched a series of four annual campaigns deep into Germany, the most aggressive Roman offensive ever undertaken east of the Rhine, with the apparent strategic intent of pushing the frontier from the Rhine to the Elbe. He bridged the Rhine at multiple points, cut a great canal — the fossa Drusiana — from the Rhine to the lakes of what is now the Netherlands so that a Roman fleet could enter the North Sea, took the fleet around the German coast as far as the Weser and the Elbe, and won a series of pitched battles against the Sicambri, Chatti, and Cherusci. He was the first Roman commander to cross the Elbe, an exploit that Augustan propaganda treated as the symbolic equivalent of Caesar's first crossings of the Rhine and the Channel.

In the summer of 9 BC, on his way back to winter quarters after the fourth of these campaigns, his horse fell on him in a German forest east of the Rhine. He broke his thigh and lingered, in great pain, for thirty days. His brother Tiberius, when news reached him in Italy, rode day and night across the Alps and through Gaul and arrived at the camp in time to be with him at the end. Drusus died in his brother's arms. The army carried the body in procession back across the Rhine; Tiberius walked on foot at the head of the cortege the whole distance to Rome, where Augustus, who had come out to meet them, delivered the funeral oration from the rostra and ordered honors on a scale he had never granted before to a member of the family. Drusus was given the hereditary cognomen Germanicus — conqueror of Germany — to be carried by his eldest son.

His wife was Antonia the Younger, the daughter of Mark Antony and Octavia, the sister of Augustus. She was nineteen when he died and would live a further forty-six years, never remarrying, raising in his name the three surviving children of their marriage: the elder Germanicus, who would become the most popular Roman general of his generation and the father of Caligula and grandfather of Nero; Livilla, who would marry into the imperial line and be executed by her mother in old age for her part in a conspiracy; and the lame, stammering, scholarly Claudius, despised by his family and dismissed as unfit, who would become emperor by the unlikeliest accident in Roman political history.

The Drusus name, then, runs through three crucial generations. Drusus the Elder himself was the favored son of Livia, the favored stepson of Augustus, the joint conqueror of the Alps with his brother Tiberius, and the explorer who took Roman arms to the Elbe; his death at twenty-nine deprived the dynasty of its most popular member and shifted the long succession to the gloomier and less suited Tiberius. Had he lived, the principate might have passed to him on Augustus's death rather than to his brother, and the long, dark withdrawal of Tiberius to Capri, with all that followed from it, might have been spared the Roman state.

He left behind, instead, an idea of the Roman officer at his best — energetic, generous, popular with his soldiers, openly admiring of the old republican forms his own dynasty was making obsolete — that the historians of the next century would set against the long shadow of Tiberius. His name passed to his son Germanicus, who carried it back across the Rhine in turn, and to his grandson Caligula, who profaned it.

The road continues to Germanicus.
