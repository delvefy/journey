Livilla

c. 13 BC – AD 31 · Julio-Claudian princess, lover of Sejanus

Livilla, born Claudia Livia Julia about 13 BC and dead at Rome late in AD 31, was the only daughter of Drusus the Elder and Antonia the Younger, the younger sister of Germanicus and the elder sister of the future emperor Claudius. She was twice married into the imperial line, was the mother of Tiberius Gemellus and Julia Livia, was the lover of the praetorian prefect Sejanus, was the principal agent in the poisoning of her own husband Drusus the Younger in AD 23, was exposed in the fall of Sejanus in October of AD 31, and was killed by starvation in her own mother's house. The Senate, after her death, voted a damnatio memoriae and had her name erased from inscriptions and her likenesses removed from public places. She is the most carefully condemned woman of the Julio-Claudian house; nothing of the case for her has come down to us, and what survives of her is what her enemies wrote.

She was born about 13 BC in the year of her younger brother Claudius's conception, four years before her father Drusus the Elder's death east of the Rhine. Her mother Antonia, then a young woman of twenty-three, kept the household at Rome through Drusus's long campaigning seasons; Drusus died in 9 BC, when Livilla was about four. She was raised in Antonia's house at Rome with her two brothers and the larger extended household of cousins, freedmen, and political wards that Antonia kept — including the Jewish prince Herod Agrippa I, sent from Palestine as a hostage in his youth, and the older children of her own half-sisters by Octavia's first marriage.

She was married first, about 1 BC, to Augustus's grandson Gaius Caesar, the elder of the sons of Julia the Elder by Agrippa whom Augustus had adopted as his own and was visibly grooming as a joint heir with his brother Lucius. She was a girl of twelve or thirteen at the wedding; Gaius was about eighteen, and was sent immediately to the East as commander of the Roman forces facing Parthia. The marriage was childless. Gaius died of his wounds at Limyra in Lycia in February of AD 4 on his way home from the eastern command. Livilla, then about sixteen, was a widow.

Within the same year she was married again, to her own first cousin and adoptive brother — Drusus the Younger, the son of her uncle Tiberius. Tiberius himself had only been adopted into the imperial line by Augustus a few months earlier, in June of AD 4, in the great post-Gaius reorganization of the succession; the marriage of Livilla and Drusus the Younger was part of the same political package. They were married for nineteen years.

The marriage produced three children. A pair of twin boys, Tiberius Gemellus and Germanicus Gemellus, were born about AD 19, on whom Tiberius the grandfather is said to have stamped the rare double-cornucopia coinage that celebrated their birth — the only image of twins on Roman imperial coinage from a hundred years. Germanicus Gemellus died in early childhood. A daughter, Julia Livia, born earlier, about AD 5, would be married to Nero Julius Caesar, the eldest son of Germanicus and Agrippina the Elder, and after Nero's exile and death in the purges of AD 29 to AD 31 would be married off again to Gaius Rubellius Blandus.

About AD 20 Livilla, by Tacitus's reading, took as her lover the praetorian prefect Sejanus, then a man of about forty and rising fast in Tiberius's confidence. The affair was conducted by the agency of the household physician Eudemus, whom Livilla was using as a procurer; the same Eudemus would three years later be one of the principal agents of the poisoning of Drusus the Younger. Sejanus, by Tacitus's account, divorced his first wife Apicata in AD 23, allegedly to clear the way for an open marriage with Livilla after Drusus the Younger's removal; Apicata, sent away with her three small children, would survive eight years in resentful obscurity before her own dramatic intervention at the end of the story.

In the spring and summer of AD 23 Drusus the Younger, then thirty-six and the formal heir of Tiberius with the tribunician power, fell into the slow illness that killed him after several weeks. The poison was administered, by Apicata's later account, over an extended period through the cup-bearer Lygdus and the physician Eudemus, both of whom Livilla had bribed. The objective was to clear the line of succession both for Sejanus, who would now seek the empty hand of the imperial widow, and for the political reordering of Tiberius's family that Sejanus had been pursuing since his elevation to the prefecture. Drusus died in the late summer of AD 23.

Sejanus's open request to Tiberius for the hand of the widow came probably in AD 25; Tiberius, by Tacitus's report of the famous letter, deferred the request with a careful and deliberate ambiguity. The two waited. Tiberius retired to Capri in AD 26. Sejanus continued to consolidate his position, the destruction of the Agrippina party gathered pace, and Livilla remained at Rome with her son Tiberius Gemellus and her daughter Julia Livia, waiting for the marriage that would put her on the threshold of the principate a second time. By AD 31 Sejanus, having failed to obtain the widow, had been granted instead the betrothal to Livilla's own daughter Julia, an arrangement that placed him at the threshold of the imperial family by a side door and that may have been the immediate occasion of Antonia's intervention.

The whole edifice collapsed in October of AD 31. Antonia the Younger, the boy Caligula's grandmother and, more pointedly, Livilla's own mother, learned through her freedman Pallas that the next move of Sejanus would be against her grandson; she sent the long private letter to Tiberius at Capri that exposed the affair, the marriage plans, and, by Tacitus's reading, the original poisoning of Drusus the Younger eight years earlier. Tiberius removed Sejanus in a single afternoon at the Senate on 18 October AD 31, executed him and his children, and turned to the second strand of the case. Sejanus's discarded first wife Apicata, who had survived in obscurity for eight years, on hearing of her children's deaths wrote out a full account of the poisoning of Drusus the Younger and the names of all those involved, sent it to Tiberius at Capri, and killed herself.

The letter named Livilla. Tiberius handed his niece to her own mother Antonia for whatever punishment Antonia might choose to impose, an extraordinary act for a Roman patriarch and one that ancient sources, even at this distance, find difficult to interpret as anything but a refusal to face the killing of his daughter-in-law in his own name. Antonia, by Cassius Dio's report, locked her daughter in a room in her house and starved her to death. The date is not precisely preserved; the death is generally placed in late AD 31 or early AD 32. Livilla was about forty-four.

The Senate, on Tiberius's report from Capri, decreed a formal damnatio memoriae against her. Her name and image were chiselled from public monuments, her statues taken down, her cognomen obliterated from inscriptions. Her son Tiberius Gemellus, eight at his father's death and twelve at his mother's, survived in the imperial household to be named as joint heir by his grandfather Tiberius in AD 37 and to be executed by his cousin Caligula within six months of his accession. The only surviving line of her marriage to Drusus was through her daughter Julia Livia, whom Claudius would order killed by Messalina's denunciation in AD 43.

What is recoverable of her as a person, behind the savage senatorial verdict, is only the imprint of three relationships — the relationship with Sejanus, the marriage to Drusus the Younger, and the relationship with her mother — and the figure who emerges from those imprints is one of the great political operators of her generation, in a court in which the survival of every woman of the imperial house depended on her political alliances. Her gamble was to ally with the strongest man in Rome below the emperor and to wait for the natural disappearance of the emperor; the gamble failed because Antonia, her own mother, refused to let it succeed.

The road continues to Claudius.
