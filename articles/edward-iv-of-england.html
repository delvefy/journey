Edward IV of England

1442 – 1483, first Yorkist King of England, victor of Towton and Tewkesbury, husband of Elizabeth Woodville

Edward was born at Rouen on 28 April 1442, the eldest surviving son of Richard, Duke of York, and Cecily Neville. His father was at the time lieutenant of Lancastrian France for Henry VI, governing what remained of the Plantagenet possessions across the Channel; his mother, the daughter of Ralph Neville, Earl of Westmorland, by Joan Beaufort, was a sister of the great Neville earls of the North and a niece by half-blood to Cardinal Beaufort. The boy grew up partly at Rouen, partly at Fotheringhay in Northamptonshire, partly at Ludlow on the Welsh Marches, the principal castle of his father's vast March lands; from childhood he was a tall, blond, athletic boy who would in adulthood reach the unusual height of about six feet four inches, the tallest English king then recorded.

His father's claim to the throne, descended through Lionel of Antwerp, the elder son of Edward III, was technically superior to Henry VI's Lancastrian claim from John of Gaunt; the issue had been kept dormant by Yorkist forbearance for forty years. The collapse of Henry VI's government in the 1450s — the king's mental breakdown in 1453, the loss of Normandy and Guyenne the same year, the rise of the Beaufort and Tudor faction at court — brought the constitutional question back. In 1455, when Edward was thirteen, his father defeated the king's army at the first battle of St Albans. The Wars of the Roses had begun. Over the next five years the duke of York moved in and out of the protectorship, recognised in 1460 as heir to Henry VI's son. On 30 December 1460 he was killed at Wakefield in a sudden engagement with a Lancastrian force; his second son Edmund, Earl of Rutland, was killed beside him; his head, in a paper crown, was displayed on Micklegate Bar at York.

The eighteen-year-old Edward, in the Welsh Marches with an army, declined to mourn long. On 2 February 1461 at Mortimer's Cross near Leominster he met and destroyed a Lancastrian force under Owen and Jasper Tudor; the morning's parhelion of three suns above the field he took as a divine sign and adopted as one of his badges. He marched on London, where his cousin Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, had just been defeated at the second battle of St Albans by Queen Margaret of Anjou's northern army. The Lancastrians, having failed to follow up the victory, allowed the city to acclaim Edward king on 4 March. He marched north with a Yorkist army of more than twenty-five thousand men, the largest field force in England in a hundred years, and at Towton in Yorkshire on Palm Sunday, 29 March 1461, in a snowstorm so heavy the archers could not see, he defeated the Lancastrian army in what is generally reckoned the bloodiest battle ever fought on English soil. Perhaps twenty thousand men died in the rout. Henry VI fled to Scotland.

His first reign was administered by Warwick and by his own Neville and Bourchier connections; he himself was a youthful, easily diverted, charming king with an open court and a deserved reputation as a soldier. Then, on 1 May 1464 at the manor of Grafton in Northamptonshire, he married secretly Elizabeth Woodville, the widow of Sir John Grey of Groby, four years older than himself, the daughter of a lesser baron and of Jacquetta of Luxembourg. The match was a personal one; it had no diplomatic value; it offended Warwick, who had been negotiating in France for a royal alliance, and within five years it had turned the whole Neville-Bourchier connection against him. The promotion of Elizabeth's many siblings — her father raised to an earldom, her brother Anthony Woodville to lordship and military command, her sisters married into the great noble houses — consolidated a Woodville faction at court that the older nobility resented.

Warwick, called the Kingmaker, allied in 1469 with Edward's younger brother George, Duke of Clarence, and rose in rebellion. Edward was briefly his prisoner, then released; in 1470, after a second failed rising, Warwick and Clarence fled to France, where Louis XI, calculating, brokered a reconciliation between Warwick and Margaret of Anjou, then in exile. In October 1470 Warwick returned with an army, drove Edward into exile in the Low Countries, and restored Henry VI to the throne in a "readeption" lasting six months. Edward, taking refuge with his brother-in-law Charles the Bold of Burgundy, raised troops with Burgundian help, landed at Ravenspur in Yorkshire in March 1471, and recovered his kingdom in two battles: at Barnet on Easter morning 14 April, in a fog, where Warwick was killed in the rout; and at Tewkesbury on 4 May, where the seventeen-year-old Edward of Lancaster, only son of Henry VI, was killed in the fight or just after it. Henry VI, returned to the Tower, was murdered there on the night of 21 May, by tradition by Edward's youngest brother Richard, Duke of Gloucester.

The second reign, from 1471 to 1483, was outwardly more stable. Edward rebuilt the royal finances by careful management of the Crown estates, by recoinage, by customs reform, and by trading on his own account through London merchants; for the first time since Henry V the English crown was not in debt. He waged a short and bloodless French campaign in 1475 that ended at the bridge of Picquigny, where Louis XI bought him off with a payment of seventy-five thousand crowns and an annual pension of fifty thousand, an arrangement Edward's contemporaries considered shameful and which Louis remembered as one of the best purchases of his reign. He maintained a magnificent court, modelled on Burgundian forms, at the rebuilt Westminster and at Sheen and Windsor, with St George's Chapel at Windsor — which he refounded and largely rebuilt — as its central religious foundation. He was the patron of William Caxton; the first English printed books came from a press he tolerated and his queen patronised.

The shadows of the reign were domestic. His brother George of Clarence, repeatedly disloyal, was at last convicted of treason and put to death in February 1478 in the Tower — according to the later, plausible tradition, by drowning in a butt of malmsey wine. The Woodville faction quarrelled with Clarence's surviving brother Richard, Duke of Gloucester, and with the old nobility around Lord Hastings, the king's chamberlain. Edward held them in balance with a politician's instinct, but the balance was personal to him.

He died at Westminster on 9 April 1483 of a sudden illness, perhaps a stroke after a fishing trip in cold weather, perhaps an infection, possibly aggravated by the steadily increasing weight he had carried in his last decade. He was a few days short of forty-one. His son Edward was twelve. The transition went badly. Within three months his brother Richard had taken the boy into custody, executed the leading Woodvilles, dissolved the marriage by the parliamentary act Titulus Regius, lodged Edward V and his younger brother Richard in the Tower, and assumed the crown as Richard III. The two boys disappeared in the late summer. By the next August Richard had been killed at Bosworth by Henry Tudor, who married Edward's eldest daughter Elizabeth of York.

Edward IV had been the more directly capable English king of the fifteenth century — the soldier who never lost a battle he commanded in person, the financier who balanced his treasury, the patron whose court had drawn the Burgundian Renaissance into England. His reign had nevertheless been built around himself rather than around an institution, and when he died the institution he had not built collapsed within months. The Yorkist house was extinct in the male line within two years of his death; the heiress was his daughter; the dynasty that inherited was descended from his line through her. The reign that had seemed in its central decade the recovery of medieval English monarchy proved, in the event, to be the bridge to its replacement.
