Louis XI of France

1423 – 1483, King of France, called the Universal Spider, breaker of the great feudal nobility, architect of the unified French monarchy

Louis was born at Bourges on 3 July 1423, the eldest son of Charles VII and Marie of Anjou. The kingdom into which he was born was at the worst point of the Hundred Years War: more than half of France was held by the English under Henry VI and the dukes of Burgundy and Bedford, his father had been driven out of Paris and was crowned only at Bourges, and the legitimacy of the Valois succession was openly contested. The recovery of France was the work of his father's reign — Joan of Arc's intervention in 1429, the coronation at Reims, the reconciliation with Burgundy at Arras in 1435, the slow reconquest of the north under Dunois and the brothers Bureau — and Louis grew up watching it. He was sent to Loches in 1433 and was rarely at court, an arrangement he afterwards bitterly resented.

His youth was a long quarrel with his father. In 1436, at thirteen, he was married to the eleven-year-old Margaret of Scotland, daughter of James I, who died in 1445 leaving no children and a famous epigram against him: "fie on life." In 1440, at seventeen, he joined the great feudal revolt known as the Praguerie, named for the Hussite rising at Prague, against Charles VII's plans to bring the royal companies of soldiers under direct crown command. The revolt failed; Louis was forgiven but distrusted. From 1447 he was sent to govern the Dauphiné, which he turned in ten years into a tightly administered duchy of his own, married without his father's consent the twelve-year-old Charlotte of Savoy in 1451, and made his court a kind of shadow court of the kingdom. Charles VII at last marched on him in 1456. Louis fled across the Alps and took refuge at the court of Philip the Good of Burgundy at Genappe, where he spent five years as the duke's pensioner and, watching Burgundian administration and Burgundian wealth at close range, learned by negative example the lessons that would govern his reign.

Charles VII died on 22 July 1461 of an ulcer in the mouth, allegedly worsened by the king's refusal to eat, fearing poison from his son. Louis was crowned at Reims a fortnight later. He at once dismissed his father's councillors, brought back his own Dauphinois servants, the chamberlains Olivier le Daim (a former barber) and Tristan l'Hermite (his hangman-in-chief), and began a programme of compressing royal expenditure, breaking the privileges of the great nobles, and restricting the rights of the parlements. Within four years he had so antagonised the magnates that they formed in 1465 the League of the Public Weal — la Ligue du Bien public — under Charles the Bold of Burgundy, Charles of Berry the king's younger brother, the dukes of Brittany and Bourbon, and the count of Saint-Pol. The league met him at Montlhéry on 16 July 1465 in a confused battle that neither side won; the resulting Treaty of Conflans gave the princes back the Somme towns and conceded the duchy of Normandy to his brother. Louis spent the next decade methodically unpicking the concessions one at a time.

The Burgundian struggle was the central problem of his reign. Charles the Bold, succeeding his father in 1467, was direct, soldierly, and the holder of the wealthiest state in northern Europe; Louis was indirect, dissimulating, and the holder of the larger but poorer one. In October 1468 Louis came in person to Péronne to negotiate, and was held there for two days as something close to a prisoner when news arrived of a Liège revolt that his agents had encouraged. He had to ride with Charles to the sack of Liège, witness it, and sign a treaty there that he repudiated as soon as he was free. The lesson stayed with him; thereafter he made war on Burgundy by every means short of the field. He bribed Charles's allies, he funded the Swiss cantons and the duke of Lorraine, he made and unmade truces, he sent agents to the rebellious Alsatian cities. In 1475 he met Edward IV of England, who had crossed to Calais expecting to invade France, at the bridge of Picquigny, and bought him off with seventy-five thousand crowns and a yearly pension of fifty thousand. Edward sailed home, leaving Charles unsupported. When Charles fell at Nancy on 5 January 1477, Louis pounced. By the autumn he had occupied Burgundy proper, Picardy, the Boulonnais, Artois, and the Somme towns, demanding also the hand of Charles's daughter Mary for the seven-year-old Dauphin. Mary, with the help of her stepmother Margaret of York, married Maximilian of Habsburg instead, and the war over the rest of the inheritance ran on into 1482, when the Treaty of Arras confirmed Louis's gains.

The other great inheritances fell to him by patience. His brother Charles of Berry died in 1472, restoring Normandy to the crown. Anjou and Maine reverted on the death of his uncle Charles of Anjou in 1481; Provence, with its rights to Naples that would haunt the next century, came in by the will of his cousin Charles V of Anjou later the same year. By 1483 the French crown directly held more of the territory of France than any of his medieval predecessors.

His government was a network. He reformed the postal courier system in 1464 into a regular royal service, the first in Europe, that allowed him to receive news from any frontier of his kingdom within days. He founded the Lyon silk industry in 1466 by importing weavers from Italy. He summoned the Estates-General only once, at Tours in 1468, and never again, raising taxes thereafter directly by ordinance. He kept on his payroll an immense network of informers and a smaller network of paid assassins. His old age was famously eccentric — he retired to the castle of Plessis-lez-Tours surrounded by archers and dogs, wore the same shabby coat for years, prayed before plumbeous lead saints sewn into his hats, paid relic-hunters for any scrap of holiness, and called from his deathbed for the holy hermit Francis of Paola from Calabria.

He died at Plessis on 30 August 1483 of a final stroke after several previous ones, in his sixty-first year. He left his thirteen-year-old son Charles VIII the largest, most internally consolidated, and best-administered French kingdom since Saint Louis, and a regency under his shrewd daughter Anne of Beaujeu. The cost of the consolidation had been borne by the great nobility, who were broken, and by the towns, which were taxed; he had been hated in his lifetime and was hated in death. Philippe de Commynes, who had served both Charles the Bold and Louis and who wrote the memoirs that defined his image, gave him the verdict that has lasted: the most cunning of princes, in whom every trick succeeded and every loyalty was provisional, and from whose patience the modern French monarchy descends.
