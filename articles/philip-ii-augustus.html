Philip II Augustus

1165 – 1223, king of France, conqueror of the Plantagenet continent, victor of Bouvines

Philip was born at Gonesse, north of Paris, on 21 August 1165, the only son and the long-awaited heir of King Louis VII by his third wife Adèle of Champagne. His father, who had taken two queens in succession without male issue and feared the failure of the Capetian line, called him Dieudonné, "given by God"; the chroniclers report bonfires and processions across the kingdom at the news of the birth. The Capetian inheritance the boy stood to receive was modest by the standards of the great vassals: a royal demesne consisting of the Île-de-France with extensions into Orléanais and a few outlying castellanies, a body of feudal rights of doubtful enforcement over the great fiefs, and the lingering prestige of the consecration at Reims. Around it lay the Angevin empire of his father's old rival Henry II of England, who held more of what is now France than the king of France did: Normandy, Maine, Anjou, Touraine, Aquitaine, and the overlordship of Brittany.

Philip was crowned at Reims on 1 November 1179, while his father was still living but stricken with paralysis; Louis VII died the following September, and the boy of fifteen entered into the full kingship. His political education had been brisk: at his coronation, his mother's brothers of the house of Champagne and Blois had attempted to dominate the new reign, and Philip's first act of policy was to detach himself from them by an alliance with the count of Flanders, Philip of Alsace, whose ward Isabella of Hainaut he married in April 1180. Isabella brought the county of Artois as her dowry — the first of the territorial accessions by which Philip would, across the next four decades, transform the royal demesne. The marriage was harmonious; Isabella died in childbirth in March 1190 at the age of nineteen, leaving the future Louis VIII as the only surviving child.

The first phase of his reign was the long contest with the Angevins. He learned early to play Henry II's quarrelsome sons against their father, allying in turn with Henry the Young King, with Geoffrey of Brittany, and most consequentially with Richard, the duke of Aquitaine. In the summer of 1188 he sheltered Richard at Paris, where the two slept in the same bed at night and ate from the same dish at table — the gesture of conspicuous intimacy that the chroniclers report at the moment they were planning to make war on Richard's father. The combined assault of Philip and Richard on the Angevin lands in the spring and summer of 1189 broke Henry's defences in the Loire valley. At Ballan, near Tours, on 4 July 1189, the dying Henry submitted to terms which included recognition of Richard as his heir; he was carried back to Chinon and died two days later. Richard was crowned king of England in September. The two new kings, Philip and Richard, then thirty-four and twenty-four, fulfilled their crusading vows together: at Vézelay in July 1190 they took the cross before the assembled host and sailed for the East.

The crusade was the public moment of their personal friendship and of its breaking. They wintered together at Messina, where Philip arbitrated the quarrel between Richard and Tancred, the Norman king of Sicily; he sailed direct to Acre while Richard diverted to conquer Cyprus and to marry Berengaria of Navarre at Limassol. At the siege of Acre in the summer of 1191 the two co-operated effectively, and the city surrendered on 12 July. Within weeks Philip, ill with a wasting fever and rightly believing that nothing was to be gained by remaining in the East under Richard's shadow, took ship for home. He landed at Brindisi in October 1191 and was at Paris by the end of the year. The Third Crusade, with Richard alone in command, continued for another fourteen months and produced a treaty with Saladin that preserved the coastal strip and pilgrim access to Jerusalem.

The intervening years were the foundation of his territorial achievement. While Richard was at war with Saladin, then on his way home, then a prisoner of Duke Leopold of Austria and the Emperor Henry VI from December 1192 until February 1194, Philip violated his crusading oath of non-aggression and seized the eastern Norman castles and the Vexin frontier. Richard's release and return in March 1194 produced five years of intermittent and bitter war in the Norman, Loire, and Aquitanian marches, in which Philip was generally on the losing side: Richard's lightning campaigns and the great fortress of Château Gaillard, raised in eighteen months above Les Andelys on the lower Seine in 1196–1198, drove Philip back to short truces. The contest was ended only by Richard's death at the siege of Chalus-Chabrol in April 1199. His successor was his youngest brother, John.

Against John, Philip was at last evenly matched. The marriage of John in 1200 to Isabella of Angoulême, already betrothed to a Lusignan, produced an appeal to the French king as feudal overlord; in 1202, after John refused to attend his court at Paris, Philip declared his continental fiefs forfeit and went to war. The campaign of 1202–1204 was decisive. The capture of John's nephew and rival Arthur of Brittany and his presumed murder shortly afterwards alienated much of the local nobility; the long siege and surrender of Château Gaillard in March 1204 broke open the road into central Normandy; Rouen surrendered in June. By the end of 1204 Philip held Normandy, Maine, Anjou, Touraine, and most of Poitou. Only Gascony in the southwest remained to the Plantagenets, and remained the focus of Anglo-French conflict for the next two centuries.

The matrimonial troubles of those years are inseparable from the political record. In August 1193, having buried Isabella of Hainaut three years before, Philip married the Danish princess Ingeborg, sister of King Canute VI. He repudiated her the morning after the wedding, on grounds he never publicly explained, and pursued an annulment that no pope would grant. In June 1196 he married Agnes of Merania bigamously; in 1200 Innocent III placed France under interdict to compel him to take back Ingeborg; he submitted publicly, then evaded; the interdict was lifted; Agnes died in 1201, freeing the canonical position; Ingeborg was kept in close custody at Étampes for another twelve years; in 1213 Philip at last restored her to her place at his side, and she was queen of France in name and dignity until his death.

By 1212 the international position had reversed itself again. John, hoping to recover Normandy, had built a coalition with the Welf emperor Otto IV, with the count of Flanders Ferdinand, and with the count of Boulogne; Philip, allied with the young Hohenstaufen Frederick II in Germany and with Innocent III at Rome, awaited their attack. The plan was a double invasion: John was to march on Anjou from the southwest, Otto and the Flemings on Paris from the north. On Sunday 27 July 1214, at the bridge of Bouvines between Lille and Tournai, the two northern armies met. Philip, fifty years old, fell from his horse in the press, was nearly killed, and remounted to give the order that decided the day; Otto fled the field; the count of Flanders and the count of Boulogne were taken captive; the imperial banner was sent to Paris as a trophy. Bouvines settled the matter. Otto lost the empire; John lost any prospect of recovering Normandy and went home to face Magna Carta; Philip emerged the master of his kingdom.

The nine years that remained were spent on the administration. He instituted the system of paid baillis and sénéchaux who administered the royal demesne for the crown, removing the work from the great household officers and the local nobility; he ordered the streets of Paris paved, the city walled, and the round keep of the Louvre raised on the western boundary as the principal arsenal and treasury; he gave the University of Paris its earliest royal privileges in 1200; he kept a careful written record of his lands, his revenues, and his charters in a chancellery archive that survived him and is the foundation of the medieval royal records of France. He died at Mantes on 14 July 1223, aged fifty-seven, on his way to a council at Paris, and was buried at Saint-Denis. The royal demesne he handed on to his son Louis VIII was four times the size, in population and revenue, of the one he had received from his father; the Capetian monarchy, marginal in 1180, had become the leading western European state.
