Pope Innocent III

c. 1160 – 1216, Lothario dei Conti di Segni, theorist of papal monarchy, master of the Fourth Lateran Council

Lothario dei Conti di Segni was born about 1160 at Gavignano, in the volcanic hill country south of Rome, of one of the leading baronial families of Lazio. His father Trasimondo held lands and castles in the Marittima and the Campagna; his mother Claricia, of the Roman family of Scotti, brought a powerful urban kinship into the connection. The Conti were to give the medieval Church four popes between his own pontificate and the end of the thirteenth century. He was educated first at Rome in the schools attached to the Lateran, then sent for the higher studies to Paris, where he studied theology under the master Peter of Corbeil, and to Bologna, where he learned canon law under Uguccio of Pisa — the two faculties that, in equal measure, formed the mind that would later draft the most ambitious papal legislation of the Middle Ages. He returned to Rome about 1187, was ordained subdeacon, and was created cardinal-deacon of Saints Sergius and Bacchus by his uncle Clement III in 1190.

Under Clement's successor Celestine III, a member of the rival Orsini family, Lothario was kept at the margins of curial business. He used the eight years of disfavour to write. The treatise De Miseria Humanae Conditionis, finished in 1195, became one of the most copied works of late medieval Latin literature, a sombre meditation on the corruptibility of the body and the vanity of worldly office; it was translated into a dozen vernaculars and Chaucer's Man of Law's Tale draws on it directly. He also produced the liturgical treatise De Sacro Altaris Mysterio, the first systematic Latin exposition of the mass and an important step in the doctrinal definition of the eucharist. On 8 January 1198, hours after Celestine's death, the cardinals elected Lothario by acclamation in the chapel of the Septizonium on the Palatine. He was thirty-seven, the youngest pope in nearly two centuries, and took the name Innocent.

His programme was clear from the first weeks. The Patrimony of Saint Peter, the central Italian lands of the Roman see, had been progressively occupied during the previous reign by imperial vicars left behind by Henry VI. Innocent expelled them within a year, restored the papal administration of the duchy of Spoleto and the Marches, and re-established a temporal power that none of his predecessors since Gregory VII had fully held. The same months brought him the wardship of the orphaned Frederick of Sicily, son of Henry VI and Constance of Hauteville, who had died in November 1198 leaving the boy to the pope's care; Innocent fought a long defensive war in the southern kingdom against the captain Markward of Anweiler before establishing his ward in Palermo. The contested imperial succession in Germany, between Philip of Swabia of the Hohenstaufen and Otto IV of Brunswick of the Welf line, gave him the opportunity to formulate the doctrine of papal arbitration that runs through his decretals: in Venerabilem (1202) he claimed for the papacy the right to examine and approve the imperial elect, and through the decade of intrigue that followed, in which he successively favoured Philip, lost him to assassination in 1208, crowned Otto in 1209, broke with him in 1210, and by 1212 was pushing the young Frederick into Germany as a third candidate, he treated the empire consistently as a fief to be confirmed by Rome.

The relationship with the western kingdoms was the slower business of his pontificate, and it occupied him to its end. With Philip II Augustus of France he carried on the long matrimonial dispute of the queen Ingeborg of Denmark, whom Philip had repudiated the day after their wedding in 1193; Innocent placed France under interdict in January 1200 to compel a public reconciliation, lifted it when Philip seemed to give way, and was still negotiating the matter in 1213 when Philip took his abandoned queen back at last. With John of England the quarrel was wider. Innocent had nominated the Englishman Stephen Langton, an old Paris classmate, to the see of Canterbury in 1206 against John's candidate; when John refused him entry, Innocent placed England under interdict in March 1208 and excommunicated the king in November 1209. The interdict lasted five years. In May 1213 John submitted, surrendered his kingdoms of England and Ireland to the pope, and received them back as fiefs in return for an annual tribute. Two years later, when the barons of England compelled John to seal the Great Charter at Runnymede, the papal vassal appealed to Rome; Innocent annulled the Charter on 24 August 1215 by the bull Etsi Karissimus, on the ground that it had been extorted by force from a feudal dependent.

The Fourth Crusade was the worst of his miscalculations. He had preached it from the first months of his pontificate; he had organised its taxation, the first general clerical tax for a holy war; he had given it a target, the Ayyubid Egypt of al-Adil; and he saw it diverted, against his repeated objections, first against the Hungarian Christian port of Zara on the Adriatic in 1202 and then, in the spring of 1204, against the Byzantine capital. The sack of Constantinople in April 1204 by the crusader army — the three-day plunder of the greatest Christian city in the world, the breaking of the altars of Hagia Sophia — horrified him. He excommunicated the Venetians for Zara, lifted the excommunication when they pleaded the necessity of provisions, then accepted the new Latin Empire of Romania as a fait accompli, installed a Latin patriarch at Constantinople, and worked for the rest of his life on a project of reunion that the wound of 1204 had made impossible. The Greek churches dated their break from Rome from his pontificate, not from Cerularius in 1054.

The crusade he authorised against the Cathar heretics of Languedoc in 1209, after the murder of his legate Peter of Castelnau in January of that year by an officer of Raymond VI of Toulouse, succeeded in its military aims and laid the foundation of the French royal absorption of the south. The early massacres at Béziers and Carcassonne, conducted under Simon de Montfort the elder, troubled him; the political success of the campaign won over his hesitations. In parallel he received and approved the two new orders — mendicant, evangelical, urban — that were to transform the religious life of the next century: orally to Francis of Assisi and his eleven brothers about 1209 or 1210, and to the Spanish canon Dominic of Caleruega in 1215, on the condition that the new preachers adopt an existing monastic rule.

The crown of his pontificate was the Fourth Lateran Council, summoned for November 1215 and attended by some four hundred and four bishops, eight hundred abbots and priors, and the envoys of every western kingdom, including the still-deposed Frederick II's representatives. It sat for three weeks and issued seventy canons. They defined the doctrine of transubstantiation, required every Christian to confess once a year and to receive communion at Easter, regulated the procedure of episcopal elections, the conduct of the inquisition into heresy, the office of preacher, the obligations of Jews to wear a distinguishing dress, the prohibition of clerics from taking part in trial by ordeal, the consequences of clandestine marriage, the rights of metropolitan provinces, and the calling of a new crusade for 1217. The substance of the canons remained the working law of the western Church until the Council of Trent, and the canons on confession and on transubstantiation passed into the universal practice of Latin Christianity.

He died less than nine months later. On a circuit through the Patrimony, attempting to reconcile the warring cities of Pisa and Genoa for the new crusade, he reached Perugia and died there on 16 July 1216 of malarial fever, aged about fifty-five. The bishop of Acre, the future cardinal Jacques de Vitry, who came on the night of the death to attend the funeral, found the body abandoned and stripped by attendants who had stolen the robes and the rings. He was buried at Perugia. His remains were carried to the Lateran in 1891 by Leo XIII, who had taken the name in homage.
