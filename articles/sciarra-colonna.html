Sciarra Colonna

c. 1270 – c. 1329, Giacomo Colonna, the Roman baron who broke into the papal palace at Anagni and crowned Ludwig the Bavarian at Saint Peter's

Giacomo Colonna, called Sciarra — the word in medieval Roman dialect meant "the brawler" — was born about 1270 to the senior branch of the Colonna family, one of the two great noble houses of Rome (with their hereditary rivals the Orsini) and the lords of an arc of fortresses across the southern Campagna and the Sabine hills. He was the son of Giovanni Colonna and a younger brother or close cousin of Stefano Colonna of Palestrina, the head of the secular line; his uncles included Cardinal Giacomo Colonna (the elder, called Sciarra to distinguish him) and the famous Cardinal Pietro Colonna, the family's representatives in the Sacred College through the pontificates of Nicholas III, Honorius IV, and Nicholas IV. He grew up at the family palaces in Rome and the citadels of Palestrina, Zagarolo, and Colonna in the Campagna.

The Colonna had been the principal supporters of the Caetani in the conclave that elected Benedetto Caetani as Boniface VIII in December 1294, but the alliance broke down within two years over the question of Boniface's legitimacy and over the systematic build-up of Caetani lands at Colonna expense. The first open quarrel came in May 1297 when Sciarra and his men intercepted a papal mule train carrying treasure from the curia to the Caetani fortress at Anagni. The two Colonna cardinals, James and Peter, issued a manifesto on 10 May 1297 denying that Celestine V had been validly able to abdicate, and therefore that Boniface had been validly elected. Boniface answered with stupefying violence: the cardinals were stripped of their dignities and excommunicated, the entire Colonna family was placed under interdict, and a formal crusade was preached against them, complete with indulgences for any who took the cross against their lands. The papal armies, under the Caetani and the Orsini, reduced Zagarolo, Colonna, and finally Palestrina, which was demolished stone by stone in 1298 and the soil ploughed with salt in the antique manner. The surviving Colonna scattered into exile, the cardinals into Sicily and then Languedoc, the lay branch under Sciarra into the courts of France.

For five years Sciarra lived a hunted life in Languedoc and Paris, kept by Philip IV's gold against the day when the king of France would need him in Italy. That day came in the spring of 1303. Philip IV's lawyer Guillaume de Nogaret, sent to Italy in secret with sealed orders and a war-chest, made contact with Sciarra in the summer; the two assembled a small force of perhaps three hundred mercenaries and Colonna retainers, marched on Anagni where the elderly pope was spending the summer in his family palace, and on the morning of 7 September 1303 burst into the town. The townspeople, surprised and intimidated, did not at first resist. The pope, abandoned by all but a handful of his familiars, received the intruders dressed in full pontifical vestments and seated on his throne, prepared (as he is reported to have said) to die in his office.

The episode known to Italian tradition as the Outrage of Anagni, the schiaffo di Anagni, is famously confused in the chronicles — the more sober report only that Sciarra demanded the abdication, that the pope refused, and that he was kept under guard in his palace for three days. The most dramatic tradition, reported by Giovanni Villani and accepted by Dante, has Sciarra strike the pope across the face with his armoured gauntlet, an act symbolic rather than physical, the lay world's blow to the high medieval papacy. On 9 September the townspeople rose against the French force, who had been openly looting the papal treasury and the cathedral, and the pope was freed. Sciarra and Nogaret withdrew. Boniface returned to Rome under Orsini protection, but died on 11 October, by general report broken.

The death of Boniface and the accession of the Dominican Benedict XI cleared the way for the return of the Colonna. Benedict restored the family to its honours in May 1304, though he reserved Sciarra's personal absolution to the Holy See. Clement V, after some hesitation, restored the cardinals and lifted the personal excommunications in 1306. The family fortresses in the Campagna were rebuilt at Roman expense over the following two decades, though Palestrina was never the same place. Sciarra himself, with his elder brother Stefano, took up the leadership of the lay branch of the family and the Colonna's faction in Roman city politics, conducting the rolling skirmish with the Orsini that occupied the next twenty years.

His second moment of European notoriety came a quarter of a century after Anagni. Pope John XXII at Avignon had refused, in defiance of long convention, to confirm the imperial election of Ludwig of Bavaria in 1314, and had excommunicated him in 1324. Ludwig in 1327, with the doctrinal support of Marsilius of Padua and William of Ockham (both refugees from Avignon at the Bavarian court), descended into Italy in arms, summoned a council of his own clerics, declared John XXII deposed as a heretic, and elected an antipope (Nicholas V, the Spiritual Franciscan Pietro Rainalducci). On 17 January 1328 he had himself crowned emperor in Saint Peter's basilica at Rome — not by a pope but, with elaborate symbolism, by the captain of the Roman people, who at that moment, by acclamation of the Roman commune and at the head of the Colonna faction, was Sciarra Colonna. Sciarra placed the imperial crown on Ludwig's head and received the oath; for one day the medieval theory that the emperor was crowned by the Romans — not by the pope — was put into practical effect.

The coup did not last. Ludwig left Rome that summer when the Roman populace turned against the foreign garrison and the food supplies failed. Sciarra and the antipope Nicholas V withdrew north; the antipope eventually surrendered to John XXII at Avignon and ended his life as a pensioned prisoner in the papal palace. Sciarra was excommunicated again, with the entire Colonna household; he died in exile, probably at Aix-en-Provence or in the Provence countryside, about 1329, in his late fifties. He was the last great fighting Colonna of his generation. His grandnephew Cola di Rienzo, the notary who in 1347 declared himself Tribune of Rome and proclaimed the restoration of the ancient Roman republic, was raised in the city he had fought to dominate; Petrarch, who would crown Cola's hopes with poems and letters, took the Colonna of the next generation as his earliest patrons.

Sciarra Colonna's reputation, like that of most of the great Roman baronial captains of his age, has come down through chronicles unfriendly to him. Dante named him in the Purgatorio, in the same passage in which Nogaret is denounced, as the second Pilate at Anagni; Boccaccio retold the schiaffo story in coloured prose; Villani's chronicle gave him the brutal nickname which has stuck. In Italian historical memory he is the prototype of the violent Roman baron in his element, the man who put his fist into the face of the high papacy and lived to crown an emperor in defiance of it; in family memory among the Colonna he was the man who restored the house after its catastrophe. Both portraits are correct, and both, in his lifetime, would have pleased him.
