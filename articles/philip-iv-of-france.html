Philip IV of France

1268 – 1314, called le Bel, the Iron King, the destroyer of the Templars

Philip the Fair was born in 1268 at Fontainebleau, the second son of Philip III of France and Isabella of Aragon. His elder brother Louis died in 1276 and Philip became heir. His father died in 1285 of a fever caught on the disastrous Aragonese Crusade, an enterprise on which Philip had served as a young man, and Philip succeeded at seventeen, crowned at Reims on 6 January 1286. He inherited a kingdom enlarged but financially exhausted by his father's failure, and immediately repudiated the war and made peace with Aragon at the Treaty of Tarascon in 1291.

In 1284, the year before his accession, he had married Joan I of Navarre, the heiress queen of the small Pyrenean kingdom and of the great French county of Champagne. The marriage brought Champagne and Brie permanently into the crown lands and joined Navarre to France as a personal union for three generations. Joan and Philip had four children who survived: the three future kings Louis X, Philip V, and Charles IV — the last legitimate Capetians of the senior line — and the daughter Isabella, married in 1308 to Edward II of England, who would become the mother of Edward III and the genealogical pretext of the Hundred Years' War. Joan died in 1305; Philip never remarried, an unusual fidelity for a king of his time.

The first long crisis of the reign was the war with Edward I of England over Gascony, opened in 1294 when Philip's court declared the duchy forfeit on a legal pretext and his armies overran most of it. Edward built an anti-French coalition of Flemish, German, and Castilian allies; Philip neutralised it by money and diplomacy. The war ended inconclusively in 1303 by the Treaty of Paris, with Gascony restored and the marriage of Edward's son to Philip's daughter Isabella arranged. In Flanders, by contrast, Philip's army was destroyed at the Battle of the Golden Spurs at Courtrai on 11 July 1302 by the Flemish urban militias, the great catastrophe of his reign — the chancellor Pierre Flote was killed there. Philip restored the situation by personal command at Mons-en-Pévèle on 18 August 1304 and imposed the Treaty of Athis-sur-Orge in 1305, but Flanders remained restive for the rest of the reign.

The first conflict with Pope Boniface VIII opened in 1296 when Philip, to finance the English war, taxed the French clergy without papal consent. Boniface answered with the bull Clericis Laicos, forbidding such taxation; Philip banned the export of gold and silver from France, cutting off papal income from the French church; Boniface backed down in 1297 with Etsi de Statu, which conceded the king's right to tax the clergy in cases of necessity that the king himself defined. For five years there was peace.

The second conflict, more famous, opened in 1301 with the arrest of Bernard Saisset, Bishop of Pamiers, on charges of treason and tried before a royal court — a flagrant violation of ecclesiastical immunity. Boniface responded with the bull Ausculta Fili in December 1301. Philip's chief lawyer Guillaume de Nogaret orchestrated a counter-campaign: a forged version of the bull, the so-called Deum Time, was burned in Paris; the first French Estates General, summoned to Notre-Dame in April 1302, voted to support the king against the pope. Boniface then issued Unam Sanctam in November 1302, the most absolute statement of papal supremacy ever published, and prepared to excommunicate the king. Before the excommunication was promulgated, Nogaret with Sciarra Colonna and a small French force seized the aged pope at Anagni on 7 September 1303; he was freed three days later but died at Rome on 11 October. His successor Benedict XI lived less than a year. In the long conclave that followed, French pressure secured the election in 1305 of the Gascon Bertrand de Got as Clement V.

Clement V never entered Rome. He was crowned at Lyon in November 1305 with Philip in attendance, settled the curia first at Poitiers, then in 1309 at Avignon — on the imperial bank of the Rhône but within easy reach of the French king. From that moment the papacy was, for seventy years, the prisoner of French political requirements. Clement annulled the bulls of Boniface against the French crown, absolved Nogaret, and quashed the proceedings against Boniface's memory at the Council of Vienne in 1311–1312, after a long and humiliating posthumous process which Philip had insisted upon.

The destruction of the Order of the Temple was the boldest single act of his reign. The Templars were the richest financial corporation in France, and Philip was their principal debtor. On Friday 13 October 1307, by a coordinated order sent in secret months in advance, the king's officers arrested every Templar in the kingdom — some two thousand men. The prisoners, including the Grand Master Jacques de Molay, were tortured and made to confess to heresy, idolatry, sodomy, and the spitting on the cross at the order's reception ceremony. Clement V at first resisted the proceedings, then yielded; at the Council of Vienne in 1312 he suppressed the order by the bull Vox in Excelso, transferring its property to the Hospitallers (though Philip kept most of the cash). On 18 March 1314 Jacques de Molay and the Preceptor of Normandy, Geoffroi de Charny, retracted their confessions in public on a scaffold before Notre-Dame; Philip ordered them burned the same evening on the Île aux Juifs in the Seine. De Molay was said, in a legend much later, to have summoned both king and pope to God's tribunal within the year. Clement died on 20 April, Philip on 29 November 1314.

The fiscal expedients of the reign were as drastic as the political ones. Philip debased the coinage repeatedly — he was called roi faux-monnayeur, the counterfeit-king — expelled the Jews and seized their property in July 1306, expelled the Lombard bankers and seized theirs in 1311, and squeezed extraordinary aids from clergy, nobles, and towns by way of the Estates General which he had himself summoned into existence. He governed through professional lawyers risen from the lesser nobility or bourgeoisie — Pierre Flote, Nogaret, Enguerrand de Marigny — rather than through the great magnates, and he made the royal council and Parlement of Paris into permanent organs of government. The administrative state of France, in its mature medieval form, was largely his work.

In the spring of 1314 the Tour de Nesle scandal broke: the wives of two of his sons, Louis and Charles, were convicted of adultery with two knights; the knights were flayed alive and broken on the wheel, the princesses imprisoned. The reign ended in November the same year with the king's death at Fontainebleau, said to be from a stroke after a hunting accident; he was forty-six. Within fourteen years all three of his sons had reigned and died without surviving male issue, the senior line of the Capetians failed, the crown passed to the Valois cousins, and the legal opening for Edward III of England to claim the French throne through Isabella was created. The Iron King had built the most centralised monarchy in Europe and broken both the universal papacy and the Templars; he had also burned out his own dynasty.
