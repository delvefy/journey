Frederick Barbarossa

c. 1122 – 1190, Frederick I of Hohenstaufen, German king, Holy Roman Emperor, leader of the Third Crusade, drowned in the Saleph

Frederick was born about 1122, the eldest son of Frederick II, duke of Swabia, of the rising Hohenstaufen family of the upper Rhine, and of Judith of Bavaria, of the Welfs. His parents' marriage had been arranged to soften the long quarrel between the two great houses of the German aristocracy, and the boy's biographers, including his uncle Otto of Freising who wrote the principal contemporary account of his reign, never lost an opportunity to remind the reader that Frederick combined in his person the blood of the Staufen and the Welf. He grew up in the family lands of Swabia, learned arms and the chase before letters, was tall, strong, of fair complexion with the reddish blond beard from which the Italians later took his nickname (barba rossa, "the red beard"), and in 1147, on the death of his father, succeeded as duke of Swabia.

The same year his uncle, King Conrad III of Germany, the first Hohenstaufen on the German throne, took the cross for the Second Crusade in response to Bernard of Clairvaux's preaching after the fall of Edessa. Frederick went with him. The expedition was a disaster — the German army was harassed and starved across Anatolia, the surviving remnant joined Louis VII of France at Acre, the failed attack on Damascus broke up in mutual recrimination — and Frederick returned to Germany in 1149 with the conviction, sharpened by the contrast, that empire and church on the western model needed a stronger hand than his uncle had been able to give. Conrad died, perhaps poisoned, at Bamberg on 15 February 1152, recommending Frederick to the princes in preference to his own infant son. The election at Frankfurt on 4 March 1152, and the coronation at Aachen five days later in the chapel of Charlemagne, gave Frederick the German kingship at thirty.

His first business in Germany was to settle the long Welf-Staufen feud he embodied. He restored to his cousin Henry the Lion, head of the Welf line, the duchy of Bavaria which had been taken from the Welfs after the murder of his grandfather; the formal investiture at the Diet of Regensburg in 1156 left Henry duke of both Saxony and Bavaria, the most powerful prince of the empire outside the king. With the rest of the German lay nobility he established a programme of confirmation and review of fiefs that restored to the crown control over the great escheats. With the church he came into immediate conflict over the principle of investiture, settled in the Concordat of Worms a generation earlier but still in friction at the diocesan level. The agreement of Constance with Pope Eugenius III, in March 1153, set the terms of the imperial-papal cooperation that was to break down within five years: Frederick would protect the Roman Church and suppress the Roman commune of Arnold of Brescia in return for the imperial coronation.

He came south in 1154 on the first of six expeditions to Italy. At Pavia in April 1155 he was crowned king of Italy with the Iron Crown of the Lombards; at Sutri in June he met the new pope, Adrian IV; at the Lateran he refused at first to perform the office of strator (holding the pope's stirrup) which the protocol of the imperial coronation required of a king of Germany, and the embarrassment was smoothed over only after a day's negotiation. On 18 June 1155, in St Peter's, Adrian crowned him emperor. Arnold of Brescia, the leader of the Roman commune that had driven out the popes, was caught by imperial troops on the return road, handed over to the papal authorities, and burned and his ashes thrown into the Tiber. Frederick returned to Germany before the Roman summer, and the next year, on 17 June 1156, at Würzburg, he married Beatrice of Burgundy, heiress of the Free County of Burgundy, which her dowry attached to the imperial demesne.

The second Italian expedition, of 1158–1162, was the high point of his imperial programme. At the Diet of Roncaglia in November 1158, on the banks of the Po, the doctors of law of Bologna were summoned to recite for the imperial commissioners the regalian rights — the rights of coinage, mills, tolls, harbour duties, public roads, judicial fines — that the cities of Lombardy had usurped in the half-century of imperial neglect, and the imperial chancellor Rainald of Dassel published the list as a charter of the emperor's lawful demands. The reaction of the cities was open resistance. Milan refused; in March 1162, after a long siege, Frederick took Milan and razed its walls and most of its churches, dispersing its inhabitants. Cremona and Lodi and the other Milanese rivals participated willingly in the demolition.

The political consequences turned against him almost at once. The death of Pope Adrian IV in September 1159 had produced a disputed election: the majority of the cardinals chose Cardinal Rolando Bandinelli as Pope Alexander III, a minority chose Cardinal Octaviano as Victor IV, and Frederick took the antipope's part and convoked the Council of Pavia in February 1160 to confirm him. The schism that followed lasted eighteen years and consumed three successive antipopes — Victor IV, Paschal III, and Calixtus III — sponsored by the imperial party. Alexander, driven into exile in France from 1162 to 1165, became the natural ally of the cities of Lombardy. In 1167, while Frederick himself was on his fourth Italian expedition (the army that took Rome in July of that year but was destroyed by an outbreak of malaria in August), the cities of Verona, Padua, Vicenza, Treviso, Brescia, Bergamo, Cremona, and Milan, joined later by twelve more, formed at the abbey of Pontida the Lombard League, in alliance with the pope. In 1168 the league founded a new fortified city on the lower Tanaro at the angle of the Genoese-Pavian roads and named it Alessandria, in the pope's honour and to obstruct the imperial march.

The fifth and longest expedition of 1174–1178 was meant to end the league. Frederick's army of perhaps fifteen thousand was reinforced from Germany only in inadequate driblets. His cousin Henry the Lion refused outright, on terms Frederick is reported never to have forgiven, to bring up Saxon and Bavarian contingents from across the Alps. At the bridge of Legnano on the Olona, on 29 May 1176, the Milanese militia and the knights of the Company of Death, fighting around the carroccio that bore the city's standard, broke the imperial cavalry; Frederick was unhorsed in the mêlée and given up for dead, and the imperial army fled back to Como. The Treaty of Anagni in November settled the church question with Alexander; the Treaty of Venice of 24 July 1177 settled it in public — Frederick knelt before the pope at the porch of San Marco and was raised by the kiss of peace — and the Peace of Constance of June 1183, six years later, settled the question with the Lombards. The cities retained their internal autonomy, but acknowledged a residual imperial overlordship and paid an annual tribute; the principle of the regalia, in modified form, survived.

The 1180 settlement with Henry the Lion was the inland counterpart of the Italian settlement. Frederick, in the wake of Legnano, summoned the duke to answer for his refusal of military service. Henry failed to appear at three successive diets; at the Diet of Würzburg in January 1180, on a formal feudal sentence of contumacy, his ducal fiefs were declared forfeit; the duchy of Saxony was partitioned, the duchy of Bavaria given to Otto of Wittelsbach, the founder of the Wittelsbach line. Henry, after a short and unsuccessful resistance, submitted, and was sent into exile at the English court of his father-in-law Henry II. The forfeitures that followed remade the German feudal map: the great compact princely territorial duchies of Saxony and Bavaria were broken up, and the smaller princely states that henceforth made up the political subdivision of Germany begin from this date.

The 1184 court at Mainz, the great Whitsun festival at which Frederick knighted his elder sons Henry and Frederick before a crowd of seventy thousand and the assembled princes, was the public summit of the reign. In 1186, at Milan, his eldest son Henry, by then king of the Romans and heir to the empire, married Constance of Sicily, the daughter of Roger II and heiress (so it would prove) of the Norman kingdom of Sicily. The combination of the empire with the Sicilian kingdom, to be realised in Frederick's grandson Frederick II, was the inheritance Barbarossa set in motion.

The last act was the Third Crusade. The news of the fall of Jerusalem to Saladin in October 1187 reached him at Strasbourg. He took the cross at the Diet of Mainz on 27 March 1188, gave the German lands a year's preparation, and led an army — the chroniclers' figures vary from thirty thousand to a hundred thousand, both probably exaggerated — out of Regensburg in May 1189. He went by the land route through Hungary and Byzantine Anatolia. After difficult negotiations and a battle outside Iconium in May 1190 in which the Seljuks of Rûm were defeated, the army descended the Cilician slope toward the Mediterranean. On 10 June 1190, crossing the Saleph river (the modern Göksu) near Silifke, Frederick was thrown from his horse, or was taken by a sudden heart attack in the cold mountain water, or both: he was sixty-eight, in armour, and was dead by the time his attendants pulled him from the river. The German army, headless and demoralised, dispersed; the small remnant under his son Frederick of Swabia reached Acre, where Frederick of Swabia himself died of fever in January 1191. Barbarossa's body was carried as far as Antioch and dismembered for transport in the heat; his flesh was buried at Antioch, his bones at Tyre, his heart and entrails at Tarsus; the legend that he had not died at all, but slept beneath the Kyffhäuser mountain in Thuringia and would return one day with his white beard grown through the table to restore the empire, took shape in the next century and made him, more than any other emperor, the symbol of medieval German kingship.

The road continues to Henry the Lion.
