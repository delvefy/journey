Henry Cavendish

English natural philosopher (1731–1810)

Henry Cavendish was born in Nice in 1731 into one of the wealthiest aristocratic families in England. Painfully shy and solitary, he shunned society almost entirely and spoke as little as he could, devoting his great fortune and his days to private experiment with extraordinary precision.

In chemistry he isolated and studied the gas he called inflammable air, now known as hydrogen, and showed that when it burned in ordinary air it produced water. This proof that water was a compound rather than an element was among the decisive results of the chemical revolution, though it embroiled him in a priority dispute over its discovery that involved Antoine Lavoisier.

His most famous experiment came near the end of his life. Using a delicate torsion balance to measure the faint gravitational pull between heavy lead spheres, he determined the density of the Earth—in effect weighing the planet and fixing the strength of the gravitation described by Isaac Newton. It was one of the most exact measurements yet made.

Cavendish also carried out pioneering work in electricity, anticipating laws later credited to others, but he left it unpublished. Only a century after his death did James Clerk Maxwell edit and bring to light these hidden researches. He died in 1810, and the great Cambridge physics laboratory founded in his family's honor still bears the Cavendish name.
