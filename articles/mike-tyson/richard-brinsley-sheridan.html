Richard Brinsley Sheridan

Irish-born playwright and politician (1751–1816)

Richard Brinsley Sheridan was born in Dublin in 1751, the son of an actor and elocution teacher. After schooling at Harrow he eloped with the celebrated young singer Elizabeth Linley, fighting two duels over the affair before they married. Settling in London, he turned to the stage with extraordinary success.

His comedies of manners—The Rivals (1775), which gave the language the malapropism through its character Mrs Malaprop, and the brilliant The School for Scandal (1777), followed by the satirical The Critic—made him the wittiest English dramatist since the Restoration. In 1776 he bought into the Drury Lane Theatre, taking over the share of the retiring actor David Garrick, and became its proprietor and guiding spirit.

Sheridan then abandoned playwriting for politics, entering Parliament in 1780 as a follower of Charles James Fox. He proved one of the great orators of the age, and his marathon speeches in the impeachment of Warren Hastings were among the most admired ever delivered in the Commons. A member of the famous Club that gathered round Samuel Johnson and Edmund Burke, he also served as confidant and adviser to the Prince of Wales, the future George IV.

His later years were shadowed by debt and misfortune. He watched Drury Lane burn in 1809, lost his seat in 1812, and died in poverty in 1816. Yet he was honoured with burial in Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey, his comedies still performed two centuries later.
