Thomas Aquinas

Medieval theologian and philosopher (c. 1225–1274)

Thomas Aquinas, born around 1225 at Roccasecca in southern Italy, was the greatest theologian of the Middle Ages and the central figure of medieval Scholasticism. Born into a noble family who imprisoned him for a year to dissuade him, he insisted on joining the new mendicant Dominican Order, and went north to study under the renowned scholar Albertus Magnus.

Aquinas devoted his life to reconciling Christian faith with the philosophy of Aristotle, whose works were newly flooding into the Latin West, often accompanied by the commentaries of the Muslim philosopher Averroes. Against those who feared that pagan reason threatened revelation, Aquinas argued that faith and reason, both gifts of God, could never truly conflict. He absorbed Aristotle—whom he called simply "the Philosopher"—and the authority of Augustine into a vast, ordered synthesis.

His masterpiece, the unfinished Summa Theologiae, lays out Christian doctrine with extraordinary rigor, including his famous "Five Ways" of demonstrating the existence of God and his influential theory of natural law. He taught at Paris and in Italy and wrote at prodigious speed.

Near the end of his life a profound mystical experience led him to lay down his pen, declaring that all he had written seemed "like straw" beside what he had glimpsed. He died in 1274 on his way to a church council. Canonized and later named a Doctor of the Church, Aquinas saw his Thomism become the bedrock of Catholic thought—the framework that shaped the cosmos of Dante's poem.
