Andreas Vesalius

Flemish anatomist, founder of modern anatomy (1514–1564)

Andreas Vesalius founded the modern science of anatomy by daring to trust his own eyes over the authority of the ancients. Born in Brussels into a line of physicians, he studied in Paris and at Padua, where he became professor of anatomy. In his day anatomy was taught by reading aloud the texts of Galen while a menial did the dissecting; Vesalius scandalized and electrified his students by taking the knife in his own hands and demonstrating the body directly.

The fruit of this labor was his masterpiece of 1543, On the Fabric of the Human Body—a magnificent volume whose superb woodcut illustrations, drawn by artists of Titian's circle, set human anatomy before the eyes with unprecedented accuracy. In it Vesalius quietly corrected hundreds of Galen's errors, for the ancient master had dissected animals and assumed their parts matched man's.

The book made Vesalius famous and controversial, and he left teaching to serve as imperial physician to the Emperor Charles V and then to Philip II of Spain. His insistence on observation over authority became a model for the whole scientific revolution to come.

He died in 1564, shipwrecked and ill on the way home from a pilgrimage to Jerusalem. The brilliant successor who took up his chair at Padua and carried his anatomical discoveries further—giving his own name to parts of the body—was Gabriele Falloppio.
