Vasco da Gama

Portuguese explorer (c. 1460s–1524)

Vasco da Gama was the Portuguese navigator who opened the sea road from Europe to the East, one of the most consequential voyages in history. A nobleman entrusted by King Manuel I with the great task that had eluded a generation of mariners, he set sail in 1497 with a small fleet to find a route to the riches of India around the southern tip of Africa.

Rounding the Cape of Good Hope, he beat his way up the unknown coast of East Africa, secured a pilot, and struck out across the Indian Ocean, reaching the spice port of Calicut in May 1498. He was the first European ever to arrive in India by sea. The achievement shattered the old overland monopolies of Venice and the Muslim world and laid the foundation of a Portuguese empire that would dominate the spice trade and the Indian Ocean for a century.

Da Gama's later returns to India were marked by ruthless violence—bombarding Calicut and preying on Muslim shipping—as Portugal fastened its grip on the eastern seas. Honored at home with titles and wealth, he was finally sent out as viceroy of Portuguese India, only to die soon after his arrival, at Cochin in 1524.

His triumph was the fruit of an enterprise begun decades earlier by a visionary prince who had gathered captains, cartographers, and shipwrights to push Portuguese sails ever farther down the African coast—Henry the Navigator.
