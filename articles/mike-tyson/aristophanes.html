Aristophanes

Athenian comic poet, "father of comedy" (c. 446–386 BC)

Aristophanes was the supreme poet of Athenian Old Comedy and the only one whose plays survive in full. Born around 446 BC, he wrote some forty comedies, of which eleven remain—riotous, fantastical, and ferociously topical works that lampooned by name the politicians, generals, philosophers, and poets of his city while the Peloponnesian War raged around it.

His targets were the living and the famous. In The Clouds he ridiculed Socrates as a babbling sophist who taught men to make the weaker argument the stronger; long afterward, in Plato's account, Socrates would trace to that caricature the prejudice that helped condemn him. In play after play—The Frogs, the Thesmophoriazusae, the Acharnians—he made sport of the tragedian Euripides, and in The Frogs he staged a hilarious contest in the underworld between Euripides and the grand old Aeschylus.

Yet Aristophanes was more than a mocker. In Lysistrata he imagined the women of Greece refusing their husbands until the men made peace, one of the earliest and most enduring anti-war comedies; in The Birds he conjured a utopian city in the sky. His verse mixed obscenity, lyric beauty, and shrewd political sense, and his plays remain a vivid, if mischievous, window onto classical Athens.

He himself was remembered with affection by the philosophers he teased: Plato gave him a place at the banquet of the Symposium, where the comic poet spins the famous tale that lovers are the severed halves of a single being, forever seeking to be whole. Among his companions in that brilliant, doomed Athens was the dazzling Alcibiades.
