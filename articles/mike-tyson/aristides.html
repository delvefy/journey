Aristides

Athenian statesman, "the Just" (c. 530–468 BC)

Aristides of Athens was so renowned for fairness and honesty that his countrymen called him simply "the Just." He fought as a general at Marathon, and through the perilous years of the Persian Wars he stood as the upright counterweight to the brilliant, slippery Themistocles, whose policies he often opposed. The contrast between the two men—plain integrity against dazzling cunning—became proverbial.

That rivalry cost him a spell of exile. Plutarch preserves the famous story of his ostracism: as the citizens scratched their votes on potsherds, an illiterate countryman, not knowing him, asked Aristides himself to write the name "Aristides" on the shard; when asked why, the man grumbled that he was simply tired of hearing him called "the Just"—and Aristides calmly wrote his own name and handed it back.

Recalled when Xerxes invaded, he served at Salamis and then commanded the Athenian troops at the decisive land battle of Plataea, fought under the supreme command of the Spartan Pausanias. When victory was won and Athens gathered the Greek states into the Delian League, it was Aristides who was trusted to fix each city's fair share of contributions—an assessment long remembered as a model of justice.

He died poor, his honesty undimmed, and his name became a byword for incorruptible virtue. His deeds were recorded by Plutarch, who set him beside the great Romans; his fellow generals included Miltiades and his political ally Cimon. The Spartan who led the Greeks to triumph at Plataea was Pausanias the Regent.
