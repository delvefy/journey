Julius Caesar

Roman general and dictator (100–44 BC)

Gaius Julius Caesar, born in 100 BC into an old but unremarkable patrician family, rose to become the most powerful man in Rome and, in destroying its republic, opened the way to empire. Ambitious and gifted, he climbed the ladder of Roman office, won a popular following, and in 60 BC joined Pompey and the immensely rich Crassus in the informal alliance known as the First Triumvirate.

As governor in Gaul he waged an eight-year campaign of conquest, subduing the tribes of what is now France and twice crossing into Britain, exploits he recorded in his lucid Commentaries. His success alarmed his rivals, and when the Senate, backed by Pompey, ordered him to give up his command, Caesar in 49 BC led his army across the Rubicon into Italy—reportedly declaring "the die is cast"—igniting civil war.

He crushed Pompey at Pharsalus in 48 BC and pursued the war to Egypt, where he became the lover of Queen Cleopatra. Returning victorious, he reported one swift campaign with the words "veni, vidi, vici." Made dictator, he reformed the calendar, relieved debt, and extended citizenship, but his accumulation of power and his appointment as dictator for life alarmed defenders of the old order.

On the Ides of March, 44 BC, a conspiracy of senators including Brutus and Cassius stabbed him to death in the Senate. His murder, far from restoring the republic, plunged Rome into fresh civil wars from which his adopted heir Octavian emerged as the first emperor, Augustus.
