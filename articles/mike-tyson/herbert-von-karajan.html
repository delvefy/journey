Herbert von Karajan

Austrian conductor (1908–1989)

Herbert von Karajan was the most powerful and famous conductor of the second half of the twentieth century, a figure who shaped how millions heard the orchestral repertoire. Born in Salzburg, he rose rapidly through the German opera houses; his early advancement was eased by membership of the Nazi Party, a fact that clouded but did not halt his postwar ascent.

In 1955 he became principal conductor of the Berlin Philharmonic, a post he held with near-absolute authority for some thirty-five years, while also dominating the Salzburg Festival, founding its Easter Festival, and ruling over the Vienna State Opera. His pursuit of a sumptuous, perfectly blended orchestral sound and his habit of conducting from memory with closed eyes became legendary.

He was among the first musicians to grasp the power of recording and broadcast technology, leaving an enormous discography and lending his prestige to the launch of the compact disc. His interpretations of Ludwig van Beethoven, set down in several complete symphony cycles, were definitive for a generation, and he was equally identified with Johannes Brahms, with the operas of Richard Wagner and Giuseppe Verdi, and with the works of Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart.

Autocratic and endlessly ambitious, he was sometimes called the music director of Europe. He died near Salzburg in 1989. Among living composers he had revered and conducted with special devotion none stood higher than Richard Strauss.
