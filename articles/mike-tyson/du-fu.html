Du Fu

Tang dynasty poet, the “Poet-Sage” (712–770)

Du Fu shares with Li Bai the highest place in Chinese poetry, yet the two men were opposites. Where Li Bai is the wine-drunk immortal of carefree flight, Du Fu is the grave, compassionate Confucian conscience of his age, a craftsman of such control and moral weight that later ages called him the Poet-Sage and the Poet-Historian. The two were friends, and Du Fu wrote many poems longing for his absent companion.

Born into a family of scholar-officials, Du Fu set his heart on serving the state, but he failed the civil-service examinations and spent frustrating years at the capital, Chang'an, seeking a patron and winning only minor posts. Then the rebellion of An Lushan in 755 tore the empire apart and swept away his hopes.

The remaining years of his life were years of war, flight, and hunger. He was caught and held by rebels in the fallen capital, escaped to serve briefly at the exiled court, and then drifted with his family across the war-torn land, often near starvation; one of his own children, he records, died for want of food. For a time he found peace in a thatched cottage at Chengdu, where he wrote some of his finest verse.

His roughly fifteen hundred surviving poems turn private grief and the sufferings of the common people into enduring art, and they shaped all the poetry that came after. He died, poor and ailing, on a riverboat around 770. He had lived under the emperor whose dazzling reign collapsed into the ruin his poems record—Emperor Xuanzong of Tang.
