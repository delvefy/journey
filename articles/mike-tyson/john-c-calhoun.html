John C. Calhoun

American statesman and theorist of states' rights (1782–1850)

John C. Calhoun was the most formidable political theorist of the antebellum South and the intellectual champion of states' rights. A South Carolinian educated at Yale, he entered Congress as a nationalist War Hawk and served ably as Secretary of War, then rose to the vice presidency—a post he held under both John Quincy Adams and Andrew Jackson.

His career and his thought turned increasingly toward the defence of the slaveholding South. He secretly authored the South Carolina Exposition, arguing that a state could nullify a federal law it judged unconstitutional, and his doctrine ignited the Nullification Crisis that pitted him against President Jackson. He resigned the vice presidency—the first ever to do so—to defend the cause from the Senate floor.

There he elaborated theories of the concurrent majority meant to protect minority sections, defended slavery as a positive good, and joined Henry Clay and Daniel Webster as the third of the Senate's Great Triumvirate, while tracing his constitutional creed back to Thomas Jefferson. Too ill to speak, he had a colleague read his final warning against the Compromise of 1850 and the disunion he foresaw.

He died that same year, but his ideas outlived him. The states'-rights and secessionist doctrines he forged became the foundation on which the Confederacy was built, and its president, Jefferson Davis, would carry them into civil war.
