Marcus Junius Brutus

Roman senator and assassin of Caesar (85–42 BC)

Marcus Junius Brutus was the most famous of the men who killed Julius Caesar, and the one whose motives have been most debated. He came of a proud Republican line that claimed descent from the Brutus who had expelled Rome's last king, and he was bound by blood and conviction to the Stoic champion of the old Republic, Cato the Younger, whose daughter Porcia he married.

In the civil war Brutus made the hard choice of principle over vengeance, joining Pompey against Caesar even though Pompey had once put his father to death. After Pompey's defeat, Caesar pardoned Brutus and heaped honours upon him—which made his part in what followed the more wounding. Persuaded by Cassius and by his own fear that Caesar meant to make himself king, Brutus joined the conspiracy and struck on the Ides of March, 44 BC; the dictator, seeing him among the daggers, is said to have reproached him with his last words.

The deed did not save the Republic. Outmanoeuvred by Mark Antony, Brutus and Cassius withdrew to the East, raised armies, and were crushed by Antony and the young Octavian, soon Augustus, at Philippi, where Brutus took his own life.

To later ages he became the very symbol of the tyrannicide—honoured by some as the last defender of liberty, damned by others as the betrayer of his benefactor, and immortalised as the tragic hero of Shakespeare, "the noblest Roman of them all." The great rival of Caesar, under whom Brutus had once served, was Pompey.
