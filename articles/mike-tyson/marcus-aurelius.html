Marcus Aurelius

Roman emperor and Stoic philosopher (121–180 AD)

Marcus Aurelius, born in 121 AD, was Roman emperor from 161 to 180 and the last of the rulers later praised as the "Five Good Emperors." Marked out for power in the careful adoptive succession arranged by the emperor Hadrian, he was raised in philosophy and letters and came to the throne on the death of his adoptive father Antoninus Pius, sharing rule at first with his brother Lucius Verus.

His reign, for all his temperance, was consumed by crisis. War flared with Parthia in the East, and along the Danube the long and exhausting Marcomannic Wars pinned him for years to the northern frontier. A terrible plague, carried home by returning soldiers and treated by his physician Galen, swept through the empire and killed untold numbers. Through it all the emperor labored at his duties with grave conscientiousness.

Amid the camps and campaigns he kept a private notebook, written in Greek, that he never meant for publication. Known as the Meditations, it is a Stoic's running dialogue with himself—on duty, mortality, the rule of reason, and the acceptance of fate—shaped above all by the teachings of the former slave Epictetus and looking back to the example of Socrates. It remains among the most cherished works of practical philosophy.

Marcus died in 180, probably near the Danube front. His passing is often taken to close the golden age of the empire, for he left the throne to his son Commodus, whose reckless misrule opened an era of decline.
