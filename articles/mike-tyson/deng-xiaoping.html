Deng Xiaoping

Paramount leader of China, architect of its reforms (1904–1997)

Deng Xiaoping transformed China more profoundly than anyone since Mao. As a young man he joined the Communist movement while on a work-study program in France, marched on the Long March, and rose to high rank in the party and army. But under Mao his pragmatism made him suspect, and during the Cultural Revolution he was twice purged and disgraced as a “capitalist roader,” only to claw his way back each time.

After Mao's death he outmaneuvered his rivals and, by 1978, became China's paramount leader—though he ruled largely from behind the scenes, never taking the top titles. He then set the country on a wholly new course of “reform and opening up.” Dismantling Mao's collective farms, welcoming private enterprise and foreign investment, and creating special economic zones, he unleashed growth that would lift hundreds of millions out of poverty and make China an economic superpower. His maxim captured the spirit: it did not matter whether a cat was black or white, so long as it caught mice.

His record carries a dark stain: in 1989 he approved the army's bloody suppression of the pro-democracy demonstrators in Tiananmen Square.

His closest comrade across the decades, dating back to their student years in France, the urbane premier who repeatedly shielded him from Mao's wrath, was Zhou Enlai.
