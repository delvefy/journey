Charles XII

King of Sweden, "the Lion of the North" (1682–1718)

Charles XII of Sweden came to the throne in 1697 at the age of fifteen and within three years faced a coalition—Denmark, Saxony-Poland, and the Russia of Peter the Great—bent on dismembering the Swedish empire. Austere, abstinent, and utterly fearless, the young king answered with a campaign of startling boldness that made him the wonder and terror of Europe, a soldier who modelled himself consciously on Alexander the Great.

He knocked Denmark out of the war at a stroke, then in 1700 routed a Russian army many times his size in the snow at Narva. Turning on Augustus the Strong, he overran Poland, dethroned him, and set up a king of his own choosing. For half a decade Charles seemed invincible.

In 1708 he made the fateful decision to march into the heart of Russia. Peter the Great drew him on across a scorched and frozen land; Charles swung south into Ukraine and gambled on an alliance with the Cossack hetman Mazepa. At Poltava in 1709 his exhausted army was annihilated, and the king fled with a remnant into the Ottoman Empire, where he spent years intriguing in vain to renew the struggle.

Returning at last to Sweden, he fought on until 1718, when he was shot dead in the trenches before a fortress in Norway, his death ending Sweden's age as a great power. His extraordinary career was immortalised across Europe by Voltaire, whose History of Charles XII became a model of biography—and a legend that reached back, in the Swedish imagination, to the earlier warrior-king Gustavus Adolphus.
