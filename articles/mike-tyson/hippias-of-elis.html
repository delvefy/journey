Hippias of Elis

Greek Sophist and polymath (c. 460–399 BC)

Hippias of Elis was the most boastful and many-sided of the Sophists, a man who claimed to know everything and to need no one. He prided himself on his self-sufficiency, declaring that he had made with his own hands all that he wore, and on his universal competence, offering to discourse without preparation on any subject a listener might propose—poetry, music, astronomy, grammar, or the art of memory, in which he was said to be unrivaled.

Behind the vanity lay real attainments. In mathematics he is credited with discovering a special curve, the quadratrix, devised to attack the famous problems of squaring the circle and trisecting the angle. As a citizen of Elis, the state that administered the games at Olympia, he compiled the first orderly register of Olympic victors—a labor that gave later Greeks one of their few fixed frameworks for dating the past.

He grew rich on his teaching and served his city as an envoy, but to the philosophers he was a figure of fun. Plato devoted two dialogues to him, setting his cheerful conceit against the probing questions of Socrates, who teased the polymath into confusion over the nature of beauty and the meaning of truth.

Yet his Olympic chronicle outlasted the mockery, preserving the names of champions whose feats had passed into legend. The mightiest of all those recorded victors, a wrestler of fabled strength and a disciple of Pythagoras, was Milo of Croton.
