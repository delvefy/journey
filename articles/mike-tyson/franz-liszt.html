Franz Liszt

Hungarian composer and supreme piano virtuoso (1811–1886)

Franz Liszt was the most dazzling pianist who ever lived, and one of the boldest composers of the Romantic age. A Hungarian prodigy, he was schooled in Vienna by a pupil of Beethoven and, by fond tradition, blessed by the old master himself. Settling in Paris, he was electrified by the playing of the violinist Paganini and resolved to win for the piano the same impossible brilliance—an ambition he fulfilled so completely that his concerts whipped audiences into a frenzy contemporaries called “Lisztomania.”

For years he toured Europe as its idol, the first true musical superstar. But he was far more than a showman. As a composer he invented the symphonic poem, wrote the famous Hungarian Rhapsodies, the great B-minor Sonata, and the Transcendental Studies, and pressed harmony toward the unknown in ways that pointed to the music of the future.

He was also the most generous of artists, lending his fame and labour to championing other composers—Berlioz, and above all Wagner, who became his son-in-law—and teaching a whole generation of pianists without fee. In later life he withdrew toward religion, taking minor holy orders and composing sacred works as the “Abbe Liszt.”

Through his music and his teaching he stood at the very centre of nineteenth-century Romanticism, linked to nearly every great musician of the age. His friend from the brilliant Paris of the 1830s, the supreme poet of the piano, was Frederic Chopin.
