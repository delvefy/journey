Hesiod

Greek poet (c. 700 BC)

Hesiod was an early Greek poet active around 700 BC, ranked with Homer as one of the two great voices of archaic epic, though his temper was utterly different. Where Homer sang of war and wandering, Hesiod wrote of gods, farms, and justice. By his own account he was a Boeotian farmer of the village of Ascra, called to poetry when the Muses appeared to him as he tended sheep on the slopes of Mount Helicon.

His Theogony is a grand account of the origins of the universe and the gods, tracing the emergence of the world from primal Chaos and the violent succession of divine rulers down to the final, ordered reign of Zeus. In systematizing the tangled myths of Greece into a coherent genealogy, it became a foundational text of Greek religion.

His other major poem, Works and Days, is addressed to his brother Perses, with whom he had quarreled over their inheritance. Part moral sermon and part farmer's almanac, it praises honest toil, warns against idleness and injustice, and preserves famous passages such as the myth of Pandora and the fable of the five ages of humankind, from a lost Golden Age to the harsh Age of Iron.

Ancient tradition loved to stage a contest between Homer and Hesiod, the poet of heroes against the poet of work and wisdom. Hesiod's didactic manner shaped later writers—Virgil's Georgics owes him a deep debt—making him the father of a whole tradition of instructive verse.
