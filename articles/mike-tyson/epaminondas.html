Epaminondas

Theban general and statesman (c. 418–362 BC)

Epaminondas, in a single brilliant decade, raised his city of Thebes to the leadership of Greece and broke forever the power of Sparta. Reared in poverty and schooled in Pythagorean philosophy, he was famed for an integrity, learning, and indifference to money rare among Greek statesmen, and he worked in close partnership with his friend Pelopidas, commander of the elite Theban Sacred Band.

His genius showed at Leuctra in 371 BC. Facing the Spartan army with fewer men, he massed his finest troops into an unusually deep column on one wing and held the rest back—the famous "oblique order"—then struck with overwhelming force exactly where the Spartan king and his best men stood. The Spartans were routed and their king slain, and the legend of Spartan invincibility was destroyed in an afternoon.

Epaminondas pressed his advantage by carrying the war into the Peloponnese itself. He freed the Messenians, whom Sparta had held in bondage for centuries, and planted fortified cities to hem Sparta in, crippling her past recovery. A youthful hostage in Thebes during these years, the future Philip II of Macedon, studied his methods closely.

In 362 BC, at Mantinea, Epaminondas won one last victory but fell mortally wounded in the moment of triumph; with his death the brief Theban supremacy died too. Yet his tactical revolution did not perish—through Macedon it passed to the army with which Alexander the Great would conquer the world.
