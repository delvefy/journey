Erasmus

Dutch humanist, "Prince of the Humanists" (c. 1466–1536)

Erasmus of Rotterdam was the most celebrated scholar of the Northern Renaissance, the man Europe acknowledged as the prince of the humanists. Born in Holland, the illegitimate son of a priest, he became an Augustinian canon before scholarship carried him across the continent—to Paris, England, Italy, and finally Basel. Everywhere he went he corresponded with kings, popes, and thinkers, and his elegant Latin made him the conscience and the wit of his age.

His pen ranged widely. The Adages gathered the wisdom of antiquity into proverbs that educated readers still quote unknowingly; the Colloquies taught Latin while skewering folly; and In Praise of Folly, written for his friend Thomas More, mocked the vanity and corruption of monks, scholars, and prelates alike. Above all, his scholarly masterpiece was the first published edition of the Greek New Testament in 1516, with a fresh Latin translation that laid the groundwork for all later biblical study.

Erasmus longed to reform the Church from within, by learning, piety, and gentle satire rather than schism. When Martin Luther shattered Western Christendom, many said Erasmus had "laid the egg that Luther hatched." But Erasmus drew back, and in a famous exchange he defended human free will against Luther's doctrine of the bound will, refusing to the end to leave the Catholic Church.

He died in Basel in 1536, honored and yet caught uneasily between the old faith and the new. His learned face is known to us through the work of the greatest artists of the Northern Renaissance—among them the engraver and painter Albrecht Dürer.
