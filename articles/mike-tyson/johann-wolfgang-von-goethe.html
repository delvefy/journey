Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

German writer and statesman (1749–1832)

Goethe was the supreme figure of German literature and one of the last true universal geniuses—poet, novelist, dramatist, statesman, and scientist in one. Born in Frankfurt, he leapt to European fame as a young man with The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), a novel of doomed love that ignited a continent-wide craze and made its author a celebrity. He became the leading spirit first of the stormy Sturm und Drang and then, with his friend Friedrich Schiller, of the serene movement known as Weimar Classicism.

For most of his long life he served as a minister at the small court of Weimar, all the while pouring out work of astonishing range. His crowning achievement was Faust, the two-part dramatic poem—labored over for some sixty years—telling of the restless scholar who wagers his soul with the devil Mephistopheles. It stands among the greatest works of world literature.

Goethe also wrote novels, lyric poetry, and travel writing, and pursued science with passion, producing a Theory of Colours and studies in botany for which he coined the word "morphology." When he met Napoleon at Erfurt in 1808, the emperor—who had read Werther seven times—exclaimed, "There is a man!"

He died in 1832, revered as the embodiment of German culture. Among the great artists who drew inspiration from his words and sought him out in person was the composer who set his verses and his drama Egmont to immortal music—Ludwig van Beethoven.
