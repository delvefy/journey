Benjamin Franklin

American polymath and Founding Father (1706–1790)

Benjamin Franklin, born in Boston in 1706, was the most versatile of the American Founding Fathers—printer, scientist, inventor, writer, and diplomat—and the embodiment of the self-made man. Largely self-taught, he ran away to Philadelphia as a youth and prospered as a printer, becoming famous for the homespun wisdom of Poor Richard's Almanack.

His curiosity made him a leading light of the Enlightenment. His experiments with electricity—above all the famous kite flown in a thunderstorm—proved that lightning is electrical and led him to invent the lightning rod. He devised bifocal spectacles, an efficient stove, and the glass armonica, and founded libraries, a fire company, a university, and a learned society, asking no patent on his inventions.

As revolution neared he threw himself into the patriot cause, helping Jefferson and others draft the Declaration of Independence. His greatest service came as envoy to France, where his wit and his fame as a scientist made him the toast of Paris—he was famously embraced in public by the aged Voltaire—and where he secured the alliance and aid that won the war. He then helped negotiate the peace with Britain.

The eldest delegate at the Constitutional Convention, he lent his prestige to compromise. The most internationally celebrated American of his age, Franklin died in 1790, mourned on both sides of the Atlantic as a sage of the new age of reason.
