Kabir

Mystic poet-saint of North India (c. 1440–1518)

Kabir is among the most revered and most quoted of all Indian poet-saints, a mystic claimed in love by Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs alike. By tradition he was raised among Muslim weavers in the holy Hindu city of Varanasi and became, against the customs of the day, a disciple of the Hindu saint Ramananda. A weaver by trade, he remained a working man all his life and spoke to working people in their own tongue.

His message cut through the divisions of religion. He proclaimed one God, formless and nameless, beyond the quarrels of temple and mosque, found not in ritual or pilgrimage but in the living heart. With sharp, witty, paradoxical couplets he mocked the vanity of priests and mullahs, the folly of idol-worship and empty ceremony, and the cruelty of caste, insisting that the divine dwelt equally in all.

His verses, sung and memorized across the north of India for five centuries, fuse the devotional fervour of bhakti, the mysticism of the Sufis, and the inner discipline of the yogis. So widely was he honoured that his poems were gathered into the holy book of the Sikhs, and a whole community, the Kabir Panth, took him as its guide.

A famous legend tells that when he died, Hindus and Muslims fell to disputing whether to burn or to bury him—until, lifting the shroud, they found only a heap of flowers, which they divided between them. Among those who shared his vision of one formless God, and into whose scripture his verses were gathered, was the founder of the Sikh faith, Guru Nanak.
