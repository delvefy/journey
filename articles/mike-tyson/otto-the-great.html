Otto the Great

First Holy Roman Emperor (912–973)

Otto the Great, born in 912, was king of Germany and the first Holy Roman Emperor, the ruler who revived the imperial dignity of the West and founded an empire that would endure, in name, for nearly nine centuries. The son of Henry the Fowler, the first Saxon king of Germany, Otto succeeded his father in 936 and set about mastering the fractious German dukes and asserting royal authority over the Church.

His defining triumph came in 955 at the Battle of Lechfeld, near Augsburg, where he annihilated a great raiding army of the Magyars. The victory ended the Hungarian menace that had terrorised Europe for half a century, secured the eastern frontier, and made Otto the foremost prince of Christendom.

Otto then turned to Italy and to Rome. In 962 Pope John XII crowned him emperor, deliberately reviving the title and the vision of Charlemagne, who had been crowned by the pope a century and a half before. With that act the Holy Roman Empire was born—a German-centred successor to Charlemagne's realm, binding the German crown to the imperial office and to the protection of the papacy.

Otto governed his vast lands by leaning on the bishops and abbots of the imperial church, whom he appointed and endowed, creating the distinctive "Ottonian" system of rule. He extended German influence eastward and presided over a cultural revival. When he died in 973, he left a powerful empire to his son and a dynasty that would dominate Europe. As Charlemagne's conscious heir, Otto the Great gave medieval Germany its imperial destiny.
