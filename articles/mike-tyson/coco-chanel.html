Coco Chanel

French couturière (1883–1971)

Coco Chanel founded the House of Chanel and, more than any other designer, freed women's fashion from the constraints of the nineteenth century, shaping the way the modern woman dresses.

From a poor and orphaned childhood she rose through millinery and the help of wealthy admirers to open her own salons. She did away with the corset, borrowed the ease of menswear and sport, worked in supple jersey, and created designs of lasting simplicity—the tailored suit, the little black dress, ropes of costume pearls—and, in her perfume No. 5, the most famous scent in the world.

Between the wars she moved among the most brilliant artists of Paris, financing Diaghilev's Ballets Russes and counting Pablo Picasso and Jean Cocteau among her friends. Her later life carried a darker chapter, an affair with a German officer during the Occupation; she escaped the reckoning that befell many, and her old friend Winston Churchill was said to have wished her well.

After a long exile she returned in triumph in the 1950s, and her house outlived her to be reborn under Karl Lagerfeld. Among the artists she drew into her circle was the composer Igor Stravinsky, whom she sheltered and supported.
