Gabriele Falloppio

Italian anatomist (1523–1562)

Gabriele Falloppio was one of the greatest anatomists of the sixteenth century, the worthy heir of Vesalius who carried the new science of the human body deep into its hidden recesses. Born in Modena and trained in medicine, he taught anatomy at Ferrara and Pisa before settling at Padua, the leading medical school of Europe, where he made his most important discoveries.

His investigations of the head and of the reproductive organs were especially fruitful. He gave the first clear description of the slender tubes leading from the ovaries—ever since called the Fallopian tubes—and named several other structures of the body, including the vagina and the placenta. He explored the delicate anatomy of the inner ear and the course of the cranial nerves, and he wrote on the disease of syphilis, even describing an early protective sheath against it.

His careful, observation-based method continued the revolution Vesalius had begun, and his own pupil at Padua, Fabricius, would in turn teach the young William Harvey. Falloppio published his Anatomical Observations in 1561 and died the following year, still in his thirties.

His chair at the University of Pisa, and the flourishing of learning that supported his early career, owed much to the Florentine prince who refounded that university and gathered scholars to his court—Cosimo I de' Medici.
