Robert Burns

Scotland's national poet (1759–1796)

Robert Burns is the beloved national poet of Scotland and one of the heralds of the Romantic age. Born into a hard-pressed tenant-farming family in Ayrshire, largely self-taught and labouring at the plough, he wrote in the living Scots tongue of his countryside with a directness, tenderness, and earthy humour that spoke to everyone. His first collection, published in 1786, made him an overnight sensation, and Edinburgh society fêted the “ploughman poet.”

His songs and poems became part of the world's common memory. “Auld Lang Syne” is sung to ring in the New Year across the globe; “To a Mouse” gave the proverb of the best-laid schemes that “gang aft agley”; “A Red, Red Rose,” “Tam o' Shanter,” and “A Man's a Man for A' That” remain loved and recited. He also laboured to collect and preserve hundreds of traditional Scottish airs that might otherwise have been lost.

A man of warm democratic sympathies, stirred by the revolutions of his day, generous and famously amorous, he lived fast and died young, worn out at thirty-seven while working as an exciseman to support his family. Scotland and the world have honoured him ever since with the Burns Suppers held each January on his birthday.

His influence has rippled down the centuries and far beyond poetry. The songwriter of a later age who called a single Burns lyric the greatest inspiration of his life was Bob Dylan.
