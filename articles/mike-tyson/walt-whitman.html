Walt Whitman

American poet (1819–1892)

Walt Whitman was born on Long Island in 1819 and was largely self-taught, working as a printer, schoolteacher, and newspaper editor while steeping himself in the Transcendentalist ideas of Ralph Waldo Emerson. In 1855 he published, at his own expense, a thin volume called Leaves of Grass.

It was unlike anything before it. In long, surging lines of free verse, unrhymed and unmetered, Whitman sang of the self and the body, of labor and the open road, and above all of American democracy and the dignity of the common person. Emerson read it and sent a letter of ringing praise—“I greet you at the beginning of a great career”—and Whitman spent the rest of his life enlarging and revising the book through edition after edition.

When civil war came, he went to Washington and served as a volunteer nurse, tending the wounded and dying in the army hospitals, an experience that gave him the poems of Drum-Taps. The assassination of Abraham Lincoln moved him to write his most beloved elegies, “O Captain! My Captain!” and “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd.”

In old age, the white-bearded “Good Gray Poet” of Camden was sought out by admirers from around the world, among them Oscar Wilde. He died in 1892, the father of American free verse and one of the nation's defining voices.
