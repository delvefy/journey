Elagabalus

Roman emperor (c. 204–222)

Elagabalus was the teenage emperor whose brief reign became a Roman byword for decadence and religious scandal. Born in Syria into the powerful Severan family, he was from childhood the hereditary priest of the sun god of Emesa, El-Gabal, from whom he took the name by which history remembers him. He owed his throne to his formidable grandmother, Julia Maesa, who—after the murder of Caracalla and the short rule of the usurper Macrinus—spread the claim that the boy was Caracalla's own son and bought the army's loyalty.

Proclaimed emperor in 218 at about fourteen, Elagabalus shocked Rome less by cruelty than by provocation. He installed his Syrian sun god as the chief deity of the state, above Jupiter himself, and presided over its exotic rites in person. His dress, his string of marriages—including to a Vestal Virgin—and his lavish, capricious conduct outraged the Senate and the soldiers alike.

Even his own family despaired of him. Julia Maesa shifted her support to his cousin, and in 222 the Praetorian Guard murdered the young emperor and his mother and threw their bodies into the Tiber, condemning his memory to oblivion.

The cousin who replaced him, and who would prove a far more sober and conventional ruler as the last of the Severan line, was Severus Alexander.
