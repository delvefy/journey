Charles V of France

King of France, "the Wise" (1338–1380)

Charles V, born in 1338, was the eldest son of John II and the first heir to bear the title Dauphin. He came to prominence in the bleakest hour of the Hundred Years' War: when his father was captured by the English at Poitiers in 1356, the young Charles was left to govern a France in chaos. As regent he faced an empty treasury, the rebellion of the Parisian merchants under Étienne Marcel, the peasant rising of the Jacquerie, and the intrigues of Charles the Bad of Navarre—yet he survived them all, learning statecraft in the hardest school.

Succeeding to the throne in 1364, Charles proved the ablest French king of the age. Sickly and unwarlike himself, he understood that France could not beat the English in open battle, and so he waged a patient war of attrition. In his great constable Bertrand du Guesclin he found a commander who avoided pitched battles, picked off English garrisons one by one, and harried their raiding columns. By the time Charles died in 1380, the vast territories ceded at Brétigny had largely been recovered, and the English held little more than a few coastal enclaves.

A scholar-king, Charles earned the epithet "the Wise." He gathered a great royal library—the seed of France's national collection—commissioned translations of Aristotle and other classics, rebuilt the Louvre as a palace, and raised the fortress of the Bastille. He reformed the coinage and the administration, restoring royal authority and solvency.

His careful work did not long survive him. The crown passed to his son Charles VI, whose descent into madness would plunge France back into the catastrophe from which his father had so painstakingly rescued it.
