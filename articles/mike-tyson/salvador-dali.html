Salvador Dalí

Spanish Surrealist painter (1904–1989)

Salvador Dalí was the flamboyant Catalan Surrealist whose melting clocks and dream landscapes, and whose outrageous public persona, made him one of the most famous artists in the world.

Born in Figueres, he studied in Madrid, where he forged the formative friendships of his youth at the famous student residence. He absorbed the psychoanalytic theories of Sigmund Freud and built upon them his “paranoiac-critical method,” mining dream and delusion for images of uncanny precision. Joining the Paris Surrealists, he painted The Persistence of Memory, with its soft, drooping watches, an icon of twentieth-century art. The painter Pablo Picasso had helped him on his way, and the two stood among the towering Spaniards of modern art.

Dalí craved fame and money, waxing his moustache into upturned spikes and courting publicity until André Breton expelled him from the Surrealist movement. He carried his theatrical imagination into film and beyond, designing the celebrated dream sequence for Alfred Hitchcock's Spellbound. In later years he turned to a grandiose “nuclear mysticism” and religious themes, ever the showman, until his death in 1989 in the town of his birth.

The dearest companion of his student days—the poet with whom he shared an intense and complicated bond before tragedy took him—was Federico García Lorca.
