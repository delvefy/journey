Christopher Columbus

Genoese explorer (1451–1506)

Christopher Columbus, born in Genoa in 1451, was the navigator whose voyages opened sustained contact between Europe and the Americas and so changed the course of world history. Steeped in the lore of eastern riches—not least the famous travels of Marco Polo—and badly underestimating the size of the globe, he became convinced that the wealthy lands of Asia could be reached by sailing west across the open Atlantic.

After years of rejection, he finally won the backing of the Catholic Monarchs Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon, fresh from their conquest of Granada. In August 1492 he set out with three small ships and, that October, made landfall in the Bahamas, certain he had reached the Indies. He went on to explore Cuba and Hispaniola and, over four voyages, the coasts of the Caribbean and the American mainland—yet he died still insisting he had touched the edge of Asia.

His landfall unleashed the Spanish colonization of the New World and the vast exchange of crops, peoples, and diseases that followed, with catastrophic consequences for the indigenous populations, who suffered conquest, enslavement, and epidemic death. As a colonial governor Columbus proved harsh and inept, and was once sent home in chains.

He died in 1506, his role disputed and his name, by an accident of history, given not to the continents he reached but to a strait—those lands instead took the name of his fellow navigator Amerigo Vespucci.
