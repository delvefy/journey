Honoré de Balzac

French novelist, master of realism (1799–1850)

Honoré de Balzac was one of the founders of literary realism and among the greatest novelists in any language. His life's work was a single colossal design he called La Comédie Humaine—some ninety interlinked novels and stories meant to portray the whole of French society after the Revolution, from peasants and provincial misers to bankers, courtesans, and ambitious young men on the make. Through recurring characters who reappear from book to book, he built a teeming, unified world rivalled only by life itself.

No writer saw more clearly the power of money and the engines of ambition that drove his age. Père Goriot, Eugénie Grandet, Lost Illusions, and Cousin Bette dissect desire and corruption with merciless penetration. He worked at a furious, self-destructive pace, writing through the night on rivers of black coffee while perpetually pursued by creditors.

His friends included the giants of French letters—Victor Hugo, who would deliver the eulogy at his graveside, and the novelist George Sand—and his vast ambition lit the way for the Naturalism of Zola and for realists across Europe.

Among the writers who revered him as a master, one Russian began his own towering career by translating Balzac's Eugénie Grandet into his native tongue—Fyodor Dostoevsky.
