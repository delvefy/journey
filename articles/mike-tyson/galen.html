Galen

Greek physician of the Roman Empire (129–c. 216 AD)

Galen of Pergamon, born in 129 AD in Asia Minor, was the most influential physician of the Roman world and, after Hippocrates, the dominant figure in the entire history of pre-modern medicine. He studied across the Greek world—at Smyrna, Corinth, and the great medical center of Alexandria—and began his career tending the wounds of gladiators in his home city, an apprenticeship that taught him much about anatomy and trauma.

Drawn to Rome, he won renown for his skill and learning, and rose to become court physician to the emperor Marcus Aurelius and later to his son Commodus. A writer of astonishing energy, he produced hundreds of treatises that gathered and extended Greek medical knowledge, marrying the Hippocratic theory of the four humors to the teleological philosophy of Aristotle, in which every organ served a purpose.

Through dissection—of apes and pigs, since human dissection was forbidden—he advanced the study of anatomy and physiology, showing for instance that arteries carry blood rather than air and tracing the action of nerves. His errors, inevitable given his animal subjects, were preserved along with his insights.

For some thirteen centuries Galen's writings reigned as the unquestioned authority on medicine, transmitted through the Islamic world by Avicenna and studied in every European school, until the anatomists and physiologists of the Renaissance at last began to correct him. He died around 216 AD.
