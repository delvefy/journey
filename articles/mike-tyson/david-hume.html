David Hume

Scottish Enlightenment philosopher (1711–1776)

David Hume, born in Edinburgh in 1711, was the most influential philosopher of the Scottish Enlightenment and a founder of modern empiricism and skepticism. As a young man he poured his energies into A Treatise of Human Nature, an ambitious attempt to build a science of human nature on the experimental method that Isaac Newton had brought to physics. Published in 1739–40, it in his own words "fell dead-born from the press."

Undeterred, Hume recast his ideas in more accessible essays and enquiries. He argued that all knowledge derives from experience, that we never observe necessary connection but only the constant conjunction of events, and that belief in cause and effect rests on habit rather than reason. His "problem of induction," his critique of the self as a mere bundle of perceptions, his distinction between "is" and "ought," and his skeptical essay on miracles unsettled the philosophy of his age and still frame debates today.

Fame and fortune came not from philosophy but from his multivolume History of England, long a standard work. Hume moved easily among the great: he was the closest friend of Adam Smith, mingled with the philosophes in the Paris salons as a diplomat, and famously hosted—then bitterly quarreled with—Jean-Jacques Rousseau.

His religious skepticism, fullest in the posthumously published Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, made him suspect to the pious, yet he met his death in 1776 with a serenity that astonished observers. By his own account he had jolted later thinkers, above all Immanuel Kant, out of their "dogmatic slumber."
