Charles James Fox

British Whig statesman and orator (1749–1806)

Charles James Fox was born in 1749 into a wealthy aristocratic family and entered Parliament at nineteen. A notorious gambler and bon vivant, he was also the most brilliant debater of his age and became the great champion of the Whig cause. He sympathised early with the American colonists against the ministry of Lord North, and made himself the lifelong adversary of the personal influence of George III, who came to detest him.

In 1783 Fox stunned opinion by forming a coalition with his old enemy North, only to be dismissed when the King intrigued against their India Bill and installed the young William Pitt the Younger. There followed more than two decades of rivalry between Fox and Pitt that defined the politics of the era. Through it all Fox was the patron and friend of the Prince of Wales, the future George IV.

He greeted the French Revolution as a triumph of liberty, a stance that produced his celebrated and tearful rupture with his old friend Edmund Burke, and that aligned his cause with the radical principles of Thomas Paine. A consistent friend of civil and religious liberty, he also worked for the abolition of the slave trade.

Power came to him only at the very end. In 1806, as Foreign Secretary in the Ministry of All the Talents, he sought peace with Napoleon Bonaparte and pressed forward the abolition of the slave trade. He died that September, his great measure passing the following year, and was buried in Westminster Abbey close to his rival Pitt.
