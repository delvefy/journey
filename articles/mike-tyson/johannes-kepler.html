Johannes Kepler

German astronomer and mathematician (1571–1630)

Johannes Kepler, born in Württemberg in 1571, gave the Copernican universe its true shape. Trained for the Lutheran ministry at Tübingen, he was won over to the Sun-centred system and became one of its most ardent and original champions. In 1600 he went to Prague as assistant to the great observer Tycho Brahe, and when Tycho died the following year Kepler inherited both his post as Imperial Mathematician and his priceless hoard of precise observations.

Wrestling for years with Tycho's data on the motion of Mars, Kepler made a discovery that broke with two thousand years of belief in perfect circles: the planets move not in circles but in ellipses, with the Sun at one focus. He found further that a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, and that the squares of the planets' periods stand in fixed ratio to the cubes of their distances—the three laws of planetary motion that bear his name.

A mystic as much as a mathematician, Kepler sought all his life the hidden harmonies of creation, and in his Harmony of the World he pursued the ancient Pythagorean dream of a cosmos ordered like music. He also founded the modern science of optics and explained how the eye and the telescope form images.

His life was shadowed by religious strife, the turmoil of the Thirty Years' War, and a trial in which he defended his own mother against a charge of witchcraft. Generations later, Isaac Newton would show that his laws followed inevitably from a single force of gravity. The observer whose data made it all possible was Tycho Brahe.
