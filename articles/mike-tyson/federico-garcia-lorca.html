Federico García Lorca

Spanish poet and playwright (1898–1936)

Federico García Lorca was the greatest Spanish poet and dramatist of his generation, whose lyrical genius and brutal murder made him a martyr of his country's tragedy.

Born near Granada, steeped in the folk songs, flamenco and Gypsy culture of Andalusia, he came to Madrid's Residencia de Estudiantes, where he formed the famous trio of friends with the painter Salvador Dalí and the future filmmaker Luis Buñuel. He became the brightest light of the “Generation of '27,” and his Gypsy Ballads won him fame across the Spanish-speaking world. A stay in New York produced the anguished, surreal verses of Poet in New York.

He was equally a man of the theatre, writing the great rural tragedies Blood Wedding, Yerma and The House of Bernarda Alba, and under the Spanish Republic he led a travelling company that carried classic drama to the villages. His art throbbed with desire, death and the harsh beauty of the Spanish land.

When the Civil War erupted in 1936, Lorca—a Republican sympathizer and an outspoken spirit—was seized by Nationalist forces near Granada and shot, his body never found. He was only thirty-eight. The great Chilean poet who counted him a dear friend and mourned his death in verse was Pablo Neruda.
