Molière

French playwright and actor (1622–1673)

Molière, born Jean-Baptiste Poquelin in Paris in 1622, is the supreme master of French comedy and one of the great dramatists of world literature. The son of a well-to-do royal upholsterer, he forsook a respectable trade for the stage, founding a theatre company that soon failed and landed him briefly in prison for debt. For thirteen years he toured the provinces as actor, manager, and author, learning every secret of the theatre before returning in triumph to Paris.

There he won the favour of Louis XIV, who became his patron and protector. Drawing on the Roman comic poets and the Italian players, Molière turned comedy into a searching mirror of human folly. In a stream of masterpieces—Tartuffe, Dom Juan, The Misanthrope, The Miser, The Would-Be Gentleman, and The Imaginary Invalid—he skewered hypocrisy, greed, pretension, and quackery with a wit that delighted audiences and enraged the targets of his satire.

Tartuffe, his assault on religious hypocrisy, was banned for years under pressure from the devout, and only the king's personal support allowed it to be staged at last. With the composer Lully he also created the comedy-ballet, a new blend of play, music, and dance for the court.

In 1673, while playing the title role in The Imaginary Invalid, Molière was seized by a fit of coughing and collapsed; he died within hours, an actor to the end. As a player he was at first refused burial in consecrated ground. The troupe he led became the Comédie-Française, still called "the House of Molière," and his genius was later honoured in a life written by Voltaire. His great contemporary in the rival art of tragedy was Jean Racine.
