Anaximenes

Greek philosopher of Miletus (c. 586–526 BC)

Anaximenes was the third and last of the great philosophers of Miletus, the associate and follower of Anaximander. Where Thales had named water as the root of all things and Anaximander an indefinite boundless, Anaximenes returned to a familiar element but chose a subtler one: air, he taught, is the source from which everything is made.

His genius lay less in the choice of air than in the mechanism he proposed for change. All things differ, he argued, only in how thickly or thinly the air is packed: rarefied, air becomes fire; condensed, it turns to wind, then cloud, then water, then earth, and finally stone. By this single principle of condensation and rarefaction he explained the whole variety of the world through measurable degrees rather than mystery—an early reach toward physical explanation.

Air was for him the breath of life as well. Just as the soul, being air, holds our bodies together, so air encompasses and sustains the entire cosmos. The earth, he held, is flat and rides upon the air like a leaf, and the sun and stars float in the same way.

With Anaximenes the bold inquiry of the Milesian school reached its close, but the spirit of Ionian philosophy lived on. The next great thinker of that coast, who rejected calm air for ever-living fire and taught that all things flow, was Heraclitus.
