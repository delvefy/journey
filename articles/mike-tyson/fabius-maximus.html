Fabius Maximus

Roman dictator, “the Delayer” (c. 280–203 BC)

Quintus Fabius Maximus was the man who saved Rome by refusing to fight. When Hannibal was loose in Italy, destroying every army that dared to meet him, the Romans turned to Fabius and made him dictator. He grasped what no one else would admit: that Hannibal could not be beaten in open battle. So he shadowed the Carthaginian army from the hills, cut off its foragers, harassed it without end, and never gave it the great clash it sought.

The strategy was hated. Romans jeered at his caution and called him “the Delayer,” a coward who trailed Hannibal like a pedagogue trailing a boy. But when impatience won out and the legions marched to annihilation at Cannae, the wisdom of delay was proved in blood, and the mocking nickname Cunctator became a badge of honor. The poet Ennius wrote that one man, by delaying, had restored the state.

A grave and conservative elder, five times consul, Fabius lived to distrust the next generation's boldness, opposing young Scipio's daring plan to carry the war to Africa. Plutarch set his patient life beside that of the Athenian Pericles.

If Fabius was the cautious Shield of Rome, the Republic had also its Sword in the same war—a bold and aggressive commander who fought Hannibal head-on and stormed the great city of Syracuse. That general was Marcellus.
