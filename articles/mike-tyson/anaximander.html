Anaximander

Greek philosopher of Miletus (c. 610–546 BC)

Anaximander, pupil and successor of Thales, carried the new philosophy of Miletus to bolder heights. Where his master had named water as the source of all things, Anaximander reasoned that the origin must be something more fundamental than any familiar element—an eternal, indefinite, boundless stuff he called the apeiron, out of which all things separate and into which they pass away.

His cosmology was startlingly original. He held that the earth hangs free at the center of the heavens, supported by nothing at all, since it has no reason to move in one direction rather than another—perhaps the first time anyone imagined an unsupported earth in empty space. The stars and sun he saw as rings of fire glimpsed through openings in the sky.

He was a man of wide and practical learning. He is said to have drawn the first map of the known world, fashioned a celestial sphere, and set up a shadow-casting gnomon to mark the seasons. Most strikingly, he guessed that life had first arisen from moisture and that human beings must have descended from creatures of another kind, since no human infant could have survived alone—a flicker of evolutionary thought two thousand years before its time.

He wrote his ideas in the first known work of Greek prose, and so helped turn philosophy into a written science. The third of the great Milesian thinkers, his own associate, who taught that the first principle was air, thickening and thinning into all the forms of the world, was Anaximenes.
