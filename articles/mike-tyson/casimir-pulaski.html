Casimir Pulaski

Polish nobleman and American cavalry commander (1745–1779)

Casimir Pulaski was a Polish soldier of liberty who became a hero of the American Revolution and is remembered as the father of the American cavalry. Born into the Polish nobility, he first won fame as a young commander in the Bar Confederation, the armed resistance of the nobles against Russian domination of Poland and against their own king. Implicated in a notorious plot against that monarch, he was condemned and driven into exile.

Wandering Europe in disgrace, he met Benjamin Franklin in Paris, who furnished him a recommendation to the struggling Americans. Pulaski sailed to the New World and, at the Battle of Brandywine in 1777, led a cavalry action that helped shield George Washington's retreating army—a service that won him a general's rank and command of the Continental horse.

He set about organising and training a proper mounted force, the Pulaski Legion, drilling American troopers in the European cavalry arts. In October 1779, leading a gallant charge at the Siege of Savannah, he was struck down by grapeshot and died of his wounds.

Honoured ever after in his adopted country with parades and monuments, he stands beside his countryman Tadeusz Kościuszko as one of the two great Polish champions of American independence. The king his confederation had defied, and whom he was accused of seeking to seize, was Stanisław August Poniatowski.
