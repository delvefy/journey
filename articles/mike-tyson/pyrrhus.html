Pyrrhus

King of Epirus and celebrated general (c. 319–272 BC)

Pyrrhus belonged to the Aeacid royal house of the Molossians in Epirus, a line that claimed descent from Achilles and to which Olympias, mother of Alexander the Great, also belonged; Pyrrhus was thus a kinsman of Alexander. Driven from his throne as a small child amid the wars of the Successors, he was raised in exile and learned soldiering young. He recovered Epirus with outside help and a marriage alliance, and quickly built a reputation as the most daring commander of his age.

Ancient writers ranked him close behind Alexander himself in generalship, and Hannibal is said to have placed Pyrrhus among the very greatest of commanders. He fought with cavalry, phalanx, and war elephants, and composed treatises on tactics that later soldiers studied.

In 280 BC the Greek city of Tarentum called him into Italy against the rising power of Rome. He beat the Romans at Heraclea and again at Asculum, but his losses were so heavy that he reportedly exclaimed that another such victory would ruin him—giving us the phrase “Pyrrhic victory.” He then crossed to Sicily to wage war on Carthage, won swift successes, but could not hold the island.

Returning to Italy, he was checked by the Romans at Beneventum in 275 BC and withdrew to Greece, where he seized and lost the Macedonian throne. He died at Argos in 272 BC, struck down in a street battle after a roof tile hurled by an old woman stunned him. Plutarch made his restless career the subject of a famous Life. The Egyptian king who had once sheltered the young exile and helped restore him was Ptolemy I Soter.
