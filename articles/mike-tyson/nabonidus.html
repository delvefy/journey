Nabonidus

Last king of Babylon (reigned 556–539 BC)

Nabonidus was the last king of independent Babylon, and the strangest. An elderly man raised to the throne by a palace coup, he did not belong to Nebuchadnezzar's royal line, and he never won the loyalty of his capital. His devotion to the moon-god Sin, whom he sought to exalt above Marduk, the great god of Babylon, set him at odds with the powerful priesthood whose support every Babylonian king required.

Stranger still, he abandoned his capital for some ten years to live at the remote desert oasis of Tema in Arabia, leaving his son Belshazzar to govern in his absence. Without the king, the city's sacred New Year festival could not properly be kept, and resentment festered.

Yet he had a curious and admirable side. Nabonidus was a passionate antiquarian who restored crumbling temples, dug down to uncover and read the foundation records of kings long dead, and tried to reckon how ancient they were—pursuits that have earned him the nickname of the first archaeologist. His daughter even gathered labeled relics into what may be the earliest known museum.

When Cyrus the Great marched on Babylon in 539 BC, the disaffected city scarcely resisted, and the empire passed to Persia. In his last years Nabonidus had watched the Persian power swallow one neighbor after another; among the great kings who fell before Cyrus in those same years, proverbial for his gold and his ruin, was Croesus of Lydia.
