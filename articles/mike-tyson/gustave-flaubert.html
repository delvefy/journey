Gustave Flaubert

French novelist (1821–1880)

Gustave Flaubert was the supreme craftsman of the French novel, an apostle of exact prose whose pursuit of le mot juste—the one right word—set a standard of literary discipline that shaped fiction ever after. Born at Rouen, the son of a hospital surgeon, he abandoned a half-hearted study of law after a nervous illness and devoted himself entirely to writing, living quietly with his mother at Croisset on the Seine.

He labored for years over each book, reading sentences aloud to test their rhythm. His first published novel, Madame Bovary, traced the adulteries and ruin of a provincial doctor's wife with such cool precision that the state prosecuted it for offending public morals in 1857. His acquittal turned the book into a sensation, and it is now counted among the founding works of literary realism.

There followed the lush Carthaginian epic Salammbô, the disillusioned Sentimental Education, the visionary Temptation of Saint Anthony, and the exquisite Three Tales, including “A Simple Heart.” He left the satirical Bouvard et Pécuchet unfinished at his death. Reserved and exacting, he was nonetheless a generous friend to George Sand, Ivan Turgenev, and Émile Zola, and the devoted literary mentor of the young Guy de Maupassant.

Throughout his life Flaubert returned to one book above all others, a work he said he had known almost before he could read and carried in his blood—the Don Quixote of Miguel de Cervantes. He died at Croisset in 1880.
