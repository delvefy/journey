Joseph Haydn

Austrian composer (1732–1809)

Joseph Haydn was the genial master who, more than anyone, shaped the Classical style of music, earning the affectionate titles "Father of the Symphony" and "Father of the String Quartet." Born to a humble Austrian family and trained as a choirboy in Vienna, he spent nearly thirty years in the service of the princely Esterházy family. Isolated on their remote estate but given a fine orchestra to command, he was, as he put it, "forced to become original."

From that long apprenticeship poured an enormous body of work—over a hundred symphonies, dozens of string quartets, masses, and concertos—in which he refined the forms that Mozart and Beethoven would inherit. His music brimmed with wit, surprise, and warmth, qualities that made "Papa Haydn" the best-loved composer in Europe.

He was generous to his peers and juniors alike: a devoted friend of the younger Mozart, with whom he traded works of mutual admiration, and the teacher of the young Beethoven. Late in life two triumphant journeys to London brought him fame and fortune—and, on hearing the mighty oratorios of Handel performed there, inspired him to compose his own choral masterpieces, The Creation and The Seasons.

Haydn died in 1809 as Napoleon's guns thundered around Vienna. The supreme master of the earlier Baroque age, the fountainhead from whom the whole German musical tradition flowed, was Johann Sebastian Bach.
