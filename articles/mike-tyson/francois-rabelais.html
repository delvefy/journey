François Rabelais

French Renaissance humanist and satirist (c. 1483–1553)

François Rabelais was the great comic genius of the French Renaissance, whose vast, learned, and uproarious tales of giants gave the very word “Rabelaisian” to the language.

He began life in religion, a friar steeped in Greek and the new humanist learning, and later won fame as a physician, lecturing on the ancient masters of medicine and practising at Lyon. But his immortality lies in the books he wrote under a playful pen-name: the chronicles of the giants Gargantua and Pantagruel, which he poured out across four volumes, with a fifth appearing after his death.

These works mingle bawdy farce, tall tale, and deep erudition, mocking monks, schoolmen, and lawyers while celebrating learning, friendship, and the appetites of the body. Their motto, “Do what thou wilt,” inscribed over his imaginary Abbey of Thélème, expressed a humanist's faith in a well-formed nature.

Condemned by the theologians of the Sorbonne, Rabelais survived through powerful friends—the cardinals who employed him and carried him to Rome, and Marguerite de Navarre, to whom he dedicated his Third Book. He revered the great humanist Erasmus as a father of his mind, addressing him in a famous letter of thanks.

The spirit of his mockery he drew from far older springs, above all from the ancient Greek master of satire, Lucian of Samosata. Rabelais died in 1553, leaving laughter as his monument.
