Cyrus the Great

Founder of the Persian Empire (c. 600–530 BC)

Cyrus II of Persia, known as Cyrus the Great, was the founder of the Achaemenid Empire, the largest the world had yet seen. Born around 600 BC, he began as ruler of the small kingdom of Anshan, a vassal of the Medes. Around 550 BC he rose against and overthrew his overlord and grandfather Astyages, uniting the Medes and Persians under his own rule and launching a career of extraordinary conquest.

He turned west to defeat the fabulously wealthy King Croesus of Lydia, bringing the Greek cities of Asia Minor under Persian sway, and in 539 BC he captured Babylon, toppling the Neo-Babylonian Empire. His dominion stretched from the Aegean to the borders of India, governed through provinces and bound together by tolerance for the customs and faiths of its many peoples.

That reputation for clemency made Cyrus unusual among ancient conquerors. The clay Cyrus Cylinder records his respect for local religion, and the Hebrew Bible remembers him with gratitude as the king who freed the Jews from their Babylonian exile and permitted them to return to Jerusalem and rebuild their Temple, even calling him the Lord's anointed.

Cyrus died around 530 BC while campaigning on his empire's northeastern frontier and was laid to rest in a simple tomb at Pasargadae—a monument that Alexander the Great would later visit and order restored. Succeeded by his son Cambyses, he was idealized by Greeks and Persians alike as the very model of a wise and just ruler.
