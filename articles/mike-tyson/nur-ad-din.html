Nur ad-Din

Zengid ruler of Syria (1118–1174)

Nur ad-Din was the ruler who turned the scattered, quarrelsome Muslim lords of Syria into a single instrument of holy war against the Crusader states. On the murder of his father, the conqueror Zengi, he inherited Aleppo, and he spent his life enlarging and disciplining that inheritance, making the recovery of the lands taken by the Franks the announced purpose of his reign.

He pressed the Crusaders hard, destroying the prince of Antioch in battle, and after long effort he took Damascus in 1154, joining the two halves of Muslim Syria under one hand for the first time in generations. With unity came a deliberate revival of Sunni piety: he founded colleges, mosques, and hospitals—the great house of healing he built in Damascus was famous across the Islamic world—and ruled with a reputation for austerity and justice that made him almost a saint in the eyes of his people.

His most fateful decision was to contest Egypt, then ruled by a failing Shia dynasty and coveted by the Crusaders. He sent his general Shirkuh, and with him Shirkuh's nephew, a young officer named Saladin. Through that intervention Egypt fell under Nur ad-Din's sway—and gave Saladin the base from which he would one day eclipse his master's house.

Nur ad-Din died in 1174 with the great prize of Jerusalem still unrecovered, leaving that glory to his ambitious lieutenant. The conqueror whose sword had first built the family power, and whose seizure of a Crusader county had set the whole counter-crusade in motion, was his father, Zengi.
