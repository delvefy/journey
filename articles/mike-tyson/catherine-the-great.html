Catherine the Great

Empress of Russia (1729–1796)

Catherine the Great was born in 1729 as Sophie, an obscure German princess of Anhalt-Zerbst. Brought to Russia at fourteen to marry the heir to the throne, she converted to Orthodoxy, took a Russian name, and patiently mastered the language and politics of her adopted country. When her erratic husband became Emperor Peter III in 1762 and quickly alienated the court and army, Catherine seized the throne in a bloodless coup; her husband was deposed and soon killed, and she ruled in her own right for thirty-four years.

An admirer of the Enlightenment, she corresponded for years with Voltaire, whom she revered as a teacher, and brought the philosophe Denis Diderot to her court, buying his library to relieve his poverty. She composed a celebrated Instruction, or Nakaz, for a commission meant to reform Russia's laws, drawing openly on Montesquieu—though in practice she preserved autocracy and tightened serfdom, especially after crushing the vast peasant rising led by the Cossack Pugachev.

Abroad her reign was one of triumphant expansion. Victorious wars against the Ottoman Empire won Russia the northern coast of the Black Sea and the annexation of Crimea, much of it the work of her brilliant minister and lover Grigory Potemkin, while three partitions of Poland, shared with Prussia and Austria, wiped that kingdom from the map and enlarged Russia enormously.

A tireless patron, she founded schools, amassed the art that became the Hermitage, and made her capital a city of European splendour. Above all she cast herself as the heir to the founder of modern Russia, raising a great equestrian statue to him inscribed "To Peter the First from Catherine the Second." She died in 1796, having made Russia a first-rank European power.
