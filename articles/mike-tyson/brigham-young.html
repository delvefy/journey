Brigham Young

Mormon leader and founder of Salt Lake City (1801–1877)

Brigham Young was the great colonizer of the American West and the second president of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Born in Vermont and trained as a carpenter, he converted to the new Mormon faith in 1832 and rose to become one of its leading apostles, devoted to its founding prophet.

When that prophet was murdered by a mob in Illinois in 1844, Young emerged from the ensuing succession crisis as the church's leader. Facing relentless persecution, he resolved to take his people beyond the reach of their enemies, and in 1846–47 he organised and led the epic Mormon migration across the plains and mountains to the valley of the Great Salt Lake, where he is said to have declared, “This is the place.”

There he founded Salt Lake City and directed the settlement of hundreds of communities across the Great Basin, governing the territory as a near-theocracy. As first governor of Utah he clashed with the federal government in the tense Utah War, and his long tenure was marked both by remarkable organisation and by the controversies of plural marriage and frontier violence.

By his death in 1877 he had planted a permanent commonwealth in the desert and left his name on its university. His whole life's work had been built upon the revelations of the man who founded the faith and ordained its course, Joseph Smith.
