Samuel Johnson

English writer and lexicographer (1709–1784)

Samuel Johnson—"Dr. Johnson"—was the dominant figure of English letters in the eighteenth century, so commanding that the era is often called the Age of Johnson. Born in Lichfield, the son of a struggling bookseller, and afflicted from childhood by illness and nervous tics, he left Oxford without a degree for want of money and fought his way up through the hack-work of London's Grub Street with little but his genius and his will.

His great monument was A Dictionary of the English Language, which he compiled almost single-handed over nine years and published in 1755—the most important English dictionary before the Oxford and a marvel of learning leavened with wit. He wrote moral essays, the philosophical tale Rasselas, a famous critical edition of Shakespeare, and the Lives of the Poets, becoming the supreme critic and conversationalist of his day.

Generous, devout, melancholic, and endlessly quotable, Johnson gathered the finest minds of London into his famous literary Club. There he held court among painters, players, and statesmen, his talk recorded for posterity by the devoted companion who would write his life.

Honored at last with a royal pension and an honorary doctorate, he died in 1784, mourned as a national institution. Among the brilliant members of his Club, the orator and political thinker whose eloquence would shape the age of revolutions was Edmund Burke.
