J. M. W. Turner

English Romantic painter, “the painter of light” (1775–1851)

Joseph Mallord William Turner was the supreme landscape and marine painter of English Romanticism, who pushed paint toward pure light and atmosphere a generation before anyone followed.

The son of a Covent Garden barber, he was a prodigy who entered the Royal Academy Schools at fourteen and was exhibiting soon after. He made his early living in topographical watercolours before turning to oils and to his lifelong obsession: the sublime drama of storm, fire, sea and sun. He measured himself ceaselessly against the old masters, learning the management of light from Rembrandt and the grandeur of historical landscape from Titian, while idolizing above all the serene seaports of Claude Lorrain, beside whose work he longed to hang.

His mature canvases—The Fighting Temeraire, Rain, Steam and Speed, the blazing slave-ship and shipwreck pictures—dissolved solid form into swirling colour and vapour, baffling many critics until a young John Ruskin rose to defend him as the greatest painter of the age. Secretive and eccentric in later life, living under an assumed name in Chelsea, he bequeathed his vast output to the nation. So far ahead of his time was his late work that, decades on, Claude Monet and the Impressionists would study it in London and claim him as a forerunner.

He died in 1851, having transformed what landscape painting could be. His great contemporary and rival—the painter of placid English fields and skies, whose calm naturalism stood at the opposite pole from Turner's tempests—was John Constable.
