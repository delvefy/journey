Praxiteles

Athenian sculptor (4th century BC)

Praxiteles was the most admired sculptor of fourth-century Athens, the artist who softened the grandeur of the classical age into grace and tenderness. Where Phidias had given the gods overwhelming majesty, Praxiteles made them gentle and human, modelling marble with a melting smoothness and giving his figures a relaxed, gently curving stance that the ancients prized above all.

His most celebrated work was the Aphrodite of Knidos, the first life-sized statue of a wholly nude goddess in Greek art. It caused a sensation; travellers sailed to the little town of Knidos simply to behold it, and it set the pattern for every later image of Venus. By tradition the goddess wore the features of his lover, the famous courtesan Phryne. Almost as renowned was his Hermes cradling the infant Dionysus, a marble of such poise and finish that it remains a touchstone of Greek art.

Through Roman copies and the praises of ancient writers his fame has never faded, and his sensuous ideal of beauty shaped the art of the Hellenistic and Roman worlds.

He worked within the tradition handed down from Phidias, alongside his great contemporary Scopas. The third member of that supreme generation, and the sculptor chosen by the mightiest patron of the age, was Lysippos.
