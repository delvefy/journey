Pope Leo X

Medici pope, reigned 1513–1521 (1475–1521)

Pope Leo X was the magnificent, spendthrift Medici pope under whose reign the High Renaissance reached its summit and the Protestant Reformation broke loose.

Born Giovanni de' Medici, second son of Lorenzo de' Medici, he was made a cardinal at thirteen and groomed for greatness, surviving the years when his family was driven from Florence. Elected pope in 1513, he gave himself to pleasure, learning and patronage on a colossal scale. He showered commissions on Raphael—the Vatican frescoes, the great tapestry cartoons, the direction of new St Peter's—and set Michelangelo to work on a Medici church, while humanists flocked to his court and Erasmus dedicated his pioneering Greek New Testament to him.

To pay for his splendours and for St Peter's he authorized a vast sale of indulgences, and that traffic provoked an obscure German friar, Martin Luther, to nail up his Ninety-five Theses in 1517. Leo dismissed the quarrel as a monkish squabble until it was too late, and his bull of excommunication in 1521 only confirmed the schism. In the same years he wove the papacy through the rivalry of the great powers, dealing with the Emperor Charles V, striking the Concordat of Bologna with Francis I, and rewarding Henry VIII with the title “Defender of the Faith.” The Florentine statesman Niccolò Machiavelli sought the favour of his Medici house throughout.

He died suddenly in 1521, leaving the treasury empty and Christendom splitting apart. The fiery Dominican prophet whose republic had once expelled his family from Florence, and whose shadow hung over the whole Medici story, was Savonarola.
