Simonides of Ceos

Greek lyric poet (c. 556–468 BC)

Simonides of Ceos was one of the most admired lyric poets of Greece, a master of the victory ode and above all of the lament, whose verses on the death of the imprisoned Danae and her child are among the most moving to survive from antiquity. Long-lived and much in demand, he sang for the tyrants of Athens, the nobles of Thessaly, and the court of Hieron in Sicily, and was reputed the first poet to write for pay. He was the rival of Pindar and the uncle of the poet Bacchylides.

He became the poet of the Persian Wars, composing the epitaphs that Greece carved over its fallen at Marathon, Salamis, and Thermopylae. The most famous of all war epitaphs is his: the couplet for the three hundred Spartans of Leonidas—"Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie." A friend of Themistocles, he gave enduring words to the victory of Salamis.

To Simonides the ancients also traced the art of memory. When the roof of a banqueting hall collapsed and crushed the guests beyond recognition, the poet, who had just stepped outside, identified each mangled body by recalling where every man had reclined—and so, Cicero relates, discovered that orderly places fix things in the mind.

His reflective poetry was weighty enough that Plato devoted a long passage of the Protagoras to interpreting one of his odes. Among the heroes whose deeds his verses helped immortalise was the victor of Marathon, Miltiades.
