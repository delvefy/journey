Malik-Shah I

Sultan of the Great Seljuk Empire (1055–1092)

Malik-Shah I ruled the Seljuk Empire at its zenith, when its sway reached from the highlands of Anatolia and the cities of Syria to the marches of Central Asia. He succeeded his warlike father Alp Arslan in 1072, and through two decades of campaigns and careful governance he held together the largest Muslim state of the age, a Turkish dynasty ruling over a brilliant Persian civilization.

The true architect of that order was his vizier, Nizam al-Mulk, perhaps the greatest minister of the medieval Islamic world, who ran the empire's administration, wrote a famous manual of statecraft, and founded a network of colleges that shaped Sunni learning for centuries. Under their joint rule Isfahan blossomed as a capital of culture and science.

Malik-Shah's patronage reached into the heavens. He built an observatory and gathered astronomers to reform the calendar, among them the mathematician and poet whose reckoning of the year proved more exact than the one Europe would later adopt. Yet the same reign saw a deadly shadow fall: in the mountains the Ismaili sectaries who would be called the Assassins seized their fortresses and began their campaign of murder.

The year 1092 broke the empire's fortune. Nizam al-Mulk was struck down by an assassin's knife, and within weeks the sultan himself was dead, still in his thirties. The succession dissolved into civil war, and the fragmented Seljuk lands lay open to the Crusaders who would arrive a few years later. The astronomer-poet whom Malik-Shah set to reform the calendar, and who is better remembered for his verses, was Omar Khayyam.
