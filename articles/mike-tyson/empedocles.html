Empedocles

Greek pre-Socratic philosopher and poet (c. 494–434 BC)

Empedocles of Akragas, in Sicily, was one of the most extraordinary figures of early Greek thought: philosopher, poet, physician, statesman, and self-proclaimed worker of wonders. Writing in grand hexameter verse, he proposed an idea that would shape science for two thousand years—that all things are made from four eternal "roots," earth, air, fire, and water, mingling and parting in endless combination.

Their union and division, he taught, are driven by two opposing cosmic powers: Love, which draws all things together, and Strife, which tears them apart, in a vast recurring cycle of the world. In passing he glimpsed remarkable truths—that air is itself a bodily substance, and that living forms might arise from the chance combination of parts, the fittest surviving—anticipations that later thinkers would marvel at.

Alongside this natural philosophy ran a deep mystical strain, drawn from the Pythagorean tradition: he believed in the transmigration of souls, preached purification and abstinence, and claimed to walk among men as a fallen divinity. Charismatic and proud, he was honoured as a healer and a champion of the people in his native city.

Legend held that he ended his life by leaping into the fiery crater of Mount Etna, which is said to have cast back one of his bronze sandals. His four elements passed into the mainstream of Greek science, above all through the searching analysis of Aristotle, and the religious vision that coloured his thought reached back to the teaching of Pythagoras.
