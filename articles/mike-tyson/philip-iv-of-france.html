Philip IV of France

King of France, “the Fair” (1268–1314)

Philip IV, called the Fair for his cold good looks, made the French crown the strongest power in Europe and bent both Church and money to his will. Grandson of the sainted Louis IX, he governed through a circle of ruthless lawyer-ministers and pursued the wealth and authority of the monarchy with a single-mindedness that shocked his contemporaries.

His most famous struggle was with the papacy. When Pope Boniface VIII forbade him to tax the clergy and proclaimed the supremacy of Rome over kings, Philip answered with force: his agents stormed the pope's residence at Anagni and seized the old man, who died, broken, soon after. Philip then secured the election of a compliant French pope and saw the papacy removed to Avignon, where for decades it sat under the shadow of the French crown.

Forever short of funds, he debased the coinage, plundered and expelled the Jews, and turned at last upon the rich and secretive order of the Knights Templar. On a single day in October 1307 he had its members arrested across France, wrung confessions from them by torture, and seized their treasure; the order was dissolved and its last grand master, Jacques de Molay, burned in 1314.

That same year Philip died, his line seemingly secure in three grown sons—yet each would reign briefly and die without a male heir, ending the senior Capetian house and opening the way to a century of war. The crusading, canonized grandfather whose holy reputation Philip invoked even as he defied the Church was Louis IX.
