Eudoxus of Cnidus

Greek mathematician and astronomer (c. 408–355 BC)

Eudoxus of Cnidus was among the greatest mathematicians of antiquity, a thinker whose ideas underpinned Greek geometry and astronomy for centuries. He learned his mathematics from Archytas, studied in Egypt, and spent time at the Academy in Athens, where he engaged the philosophy of Plato while pursuing a rigour all his own.

His theory of proportion solved one of the deepest problems in Greek mathematics, allowing magnitudes to be compared exactly even when no common measure exists—a treatment so sound that it was taken over whole into Euclid's Elements and not improved upon until modern times. He also devised the "method of exhaustion," approaching the area or volume of a curved figure ever more closely by means of inscribed shapes; this technique, which foreshadowed the integral calculus, was credited and extended by Archimedes.

In astronomy Eudoxus built the first geometric model of the heavens, a system of nested spheres turning on different axes to reproduce the wandering of the Sun, Moon, and planets. The scheme was adopted and elaborated by Aristotle and became the framework of ancient cosmology.

He wrote, too, a description of the constellations and the risings and settings of the stars. That prose work would be turned into flowing verse by the poet Aratus, whose Phaenomena carried Eudoxus's heavens to a vast audience and became one of the most beloved poems of the ancient world.
