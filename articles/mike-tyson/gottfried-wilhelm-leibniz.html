Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

German polymath and philosopher (1646–1716)

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, born in Leipzig in 1646, was among the last great universal minds, contributing to mathematics, philosophy, logic, law, history, and engineering. Working in Paris in the 1670s, he developed the differential and integral calculus independently of Isaac Newton, and the elegant notation he devised—the integral sign and the d for differentials—is the one mathematics still uses. The bitter priority dispute that followed, refereed by a partisan Royal Society, embittered both men and clouded Leibniz's final years.

In philosophy he taught that the universe is composed of simple, indivisible units he called monads, governed by a pre-established harmony, and he argued in the Théodicée that ours is "the best of all possible worlds"—optimism later mocked mercilessly in Voltaire's Candide. His principle of sufficient reason and his refinement of Aristotle's logic make him, by common estimate, the greatest logician between antiquity and the nineteenth century. He dreamed of a universal symbolic language of reasoning and pioneered binary arithmetic, a distant foundation of the digital computer.

Leibniz engaged the leading thinkers of the age. He improved upon Blaise Pascal's calculating machine, met and wrestled with the philosophy of Baruch Spinoza, criticised and extended the system of René Descartes, and his death-bed rivalry with Newton ran through to Immanuel Kant's later reckoning with the rationalist tradition.

For four decades he served the House of Hanover as librarian, historian, and counsellor, founded the Berlin Academy of Sciences, and kept up a vast correspondence across Europe. Yet he died neglected in Hanover in 1716, his funeral barely attended. His mathematics had been guided in his youth by the Dutch master Christiaan Huygens.
