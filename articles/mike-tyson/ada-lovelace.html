Ada Lovelace

English mathematician, pioneer of computing (1815–1852)

Ada Lovelace was born in 1815, the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron, who left England for good a few weeks after her birth. Her mother, dreading any sign of the father's wild temperament, had her schooled with unusual rigor in mathematics and logic, and she was tutored by gifted teachers of the day, including the science writer Mary Somerville.

It was Somerville who introduced her, at seventeen, to Charles Babbage. Ada was entranced by his calculating machines, and when an Italian engineer published an account of his proposed Analytical Engine, she translated it into English and added a series of notes more than twice the length of the original.

Those notes were her monument. In them she set out, step by step, how the engine might compute a sequence of Bernoulli numbers—a procedure often described as the first published computer program. More remarkably, she grasped that such a machine need not be confined to numbers: properly instructed, it might manipulate symbols of any kind, even compose music. She had glimpsed the general-purpose computer a century before it was built.

Her later life was clouded by illness and gambling, and she died of cancer in 1852, only thirty-six, the age at which her father had died. Long a footnote, she is now honored as a founder of computing, and the questions she raised about whether a machine can truly originate anything were taken up a century later by Alan Turing.
