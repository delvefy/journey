Timoleon

Corinthian general and liberator of Sicily (c. 411–337 BC)

Timoleon of Corinth devoted his life to the hatred of tyranny, beginning with a wound that never healed. When his own brother Timophanes seized despotic power in Corinth, Timoleon pleaded with him to relent, and when pleading failed, allowed him to be struck down. Praised by some as a hero of liberty and reproached by others—his own mother among them—as a fratricide, Timoleon withdrew in anguish from public life for many years.

His redemption came late and far from home. In 344 BC the Corinthians sent him to Sicily, where Syracuse groaned under tyranny and the Carthaginians pressed from the west. With a small band he accomplished wonders: he expelled Dionysius II and the lesser tyrants, and at the river Crimisus he shattered a vast Carthaginian army, securing the freedom of the Greek cities.

Then, instead of grasping the power he had won, he gave it back. He restored law and democracy to Syracuse, repeopled the ruined towns, and presided as an honored elder statesman over a golden age of recovery before laying down his authority. Though he went blind in his last years, the Syracusans revered him as a father and mourned him with public grief.

Plutarch set his life among the Parallel Lives, matching the Corinthian liberator with a great Roman commander of a later century, the victor over Macedon whose triumph dazzled Rome—Aemilius Paullus.
