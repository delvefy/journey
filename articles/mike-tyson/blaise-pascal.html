Blaise Pascal

French mathematician, physicist and religious thinker (1623–1662)

Blaise Pascal, born at Clermont-Ferrand in 1623, was a prodigy whose short life touched mathematics, physics, invention, and faith with rare brilliance. Tutored by his father, he proved an important theorem of projective geometry while still a teenager, and at about nineteen built one of the first mechanical calculating machines, the Pascaline, to ease his father's tax accounting.

In 1654 a famous exchange of letters with Pierre de Fermat laid the foundations of the mathematical theory of probability, born from a question about how to divide the stakes of an interrupted game of chance. In physics Pascal carried out careful experiments on the vacuum and atmospheric pressure, arranging for a barometer to be carried up the Puy de Dôme to show that air pressure falls with altitude. His name survives in the principle of hydrostatics that bears it and in the pascal, the modern unit of pressure.

A night of intense mystical experience in November 1654 turned him decisively toward religion and the austere Jansenist movement centred on Port-Royal, whose Augustinian theology of grace he embraced against the Jesuits. In the Provincial Letters he defended the Jansenists with wit so deadly that the work became a monument of French prose. His unfinished notes for a defence of Christianity were gathered after his death as the Pensées, home to the celebrated wager on God's existence and to the image of man as a "thinking reed."

Pascal died in 1662 at only thirty-nine. His blend of mathematical rigour and religious depth fascinated later writers—Voltaire sparred with the Pensées for decades—and his probability work was carried forward by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz.
