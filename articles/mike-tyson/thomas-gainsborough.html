Thomas Gainsborough

English portrait and landscape painter (1727–1788)

Thomas Gainsborough was born in Sudbury, Suffolk, in 1727, the son of a cloth merchant. A precocious draughtsman, he was sent to London around the age of thirteen to train under the French engraver Hubert Gravelot. He returned to work in Suffolk, then built a fashionable portrait practice in the spa town of Bath, before moving to London in 1774.

A founding member of the Royal Academy in 1768, he became the great rival of its president, Joshua Reynolds. Where Reynolds painted in a learned Grand Manner, Gainsborough worked with a light, feathery, almost improvisational touch, prized for its shimmering surfaces and delicate colour. His portraits—among them the famous "Blue Boy" and the tender double portrait of Mr and Mrs Andrews—made him a favourite of fashionable sitters and of the royal family.

Yet his deepest love was landscape, which he painted for his own pleasure as much as profit. Drawing on the Dutch masters and on Peter Paul Rubens, he became a founder of the English landscape tradition. In portraiture his guiding hero was Anthony van Dyck, whose elegance he consciously emulated.

A favourite of George III, who is said to have preferred him to Reynolds, Gainsborough quarrelled with the Academy over the hanging of his works and exhibited privately. He died of cancer in 1788, reputedly murmuring of his idol that "Van Dyck is of the company," and his landscapes would later be revered by John Constable.
