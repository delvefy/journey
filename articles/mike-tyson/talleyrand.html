Talleyrand

French statesman and diplomat (1754–1838)

Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord was the most adroit and cynical diplomat of his age, a man who served and outlasted every French regime from the old monarchy to the July Monarchy. Born to the nobility but lamed in childhood, he was steered into the Church and became Bishop of Autun, only to throw in with the Revolution, propose the seizure of Church lands, and abandon the clergy altogether.

His genius was for survival and negotiation. He rose to become foreign minister under the Directory and then under Napoleon, shaping the diplomacy of the Consulate and Empire—until, convinced that Napoleon's endless wars would ruin France, he secretly intrigued with the emperor's enemies and helped engineer his fall.

His masterpiece came at the Congress of Vienna in 1814–15. Representing a defeated France, he exploited the rivalries among the victors and the principle of legitimacy so skilfully that he restored his country to a seat among the great powers. Famous for his wit, his bribes, and his unshakable composure, he embodied the maxim that treason was merely a matter of dates.

In his final service he became ambassador to London for the new king Louis Philippe, helping to win recognition for the July Monarchy and to secure the independence of Belgium. He died in Paris in 1838. His great adversary and counterpart across the green table at Vienna had been the Austrian chancellor Klemens von Metternich.
