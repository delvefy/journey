Claude Monet

French painter, founder of Impressionism (1840–1926)

Claude Monet was the founder and most devoted practitioner of Impressionism, the movement that took its very name from his canvas Impression, Sunrise. Raised in the port of Le Havre, he learned from the painter Eugène Boudin to work outdoors, before the motif, and never abandoned the pursuit of capturing fleeting light and weather on the spot.

When a hostile critic mocked that 1872 seascape, the insult stuck, and the loose, luminous style of Monet and his friends became Impressionism. He helped organise their first independent exhibition in 1874, breaking with the official Salon. Years of poverty followed, but he painted on relentlessly, increasingly in series—haystacks at different hours, the facade of Rouen Cathedral, the Thames and the Houses of Parliament—recording the same subject under changing light.

In 1883 he settled at Giverny, where he built the lily pond and Japanese bridge that became his consuming subject. The vast Water Lilies canvases of his last decades, painted as his eyesight dimmed with cataracts, dissolved the world into shimmering colour and pointed the way toward abstraction; the great Orangerie panels he gave to France.

By his death in 1926 he was honoured as a national treasure. He had grown up artistically alongside Paul Cézanne in the Impressionist circle and under the example of Édouard Manet, but his closest companion at the easel, with whom he had first painted the new style at the river resort of La Grenouillère, was Pierre-Auguste Renoir.
