King John

King of England, sealer of Magna Carta (1166–1216)

King John, born in 1166, was the youngest son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, and one of the most reviled kings in English history. Nicknamed "Lackland" because, as a younger son, he was left no great inheritance, he schemed against his absent brother Richard the Lionheart during Richard's crusade and captivity, and on Richard's death in 1199 he seized the throne.

His reign was a litany of disasters. Cruel, suspicious and faithless, he alienated his barons and his allies. Above all he lost the heart of the Angevin Empire: between 1202 and 1204 the French king Philip II Augustus conquered Normandy, Anjou and most of John's continental possessions, an enormous humiliation. John's repeated efforts to recover them ended in the crushing defeat of his coalition at the Battle of Bouvines in 1214.

He also fell into bitter conflict with the Church. When he refused to accept the pope's appointee as Archbishop of Canterbury, the formidable Pope Innocent III placed England under interdict and excommunicated him; John finally capitulated in 1213, surrendering his kingdom to the pope and receiving it back as a papal fief.

Bankrupt from war and resented for his arbitrary, grasping rule, John faced open rebellion from his barons. In 1215, at Runnymede, they forced him to seal Magna Carta, the great charter limiting royal power that would become a foundation of English liberty—though John promptly persuaded the pope to annul it, plunging the realm into civil war. He died in 1216 amid that conflict. Remembered as a tyrant—and as the villain of the Robin Hood legends—John left, ironically, in Magna Carta one of history's most celebrated documents.
