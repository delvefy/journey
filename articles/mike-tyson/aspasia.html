Aspasia

Companion of Pericles, woman of Athens (c. 470–c. 400 BC)

Aspasia came to Athens from Miletus and became the most famous woman of her age, the beloved companion of Pericles. Because she was a foreigner she could not be his lawful wife, yet she shared his life and his counsel so openly, and was so admired for her intelligence and eloquence, that her household became a salon where the leading minds of the city gathered to talk.

Her brilliance both dazzled and scandalized Athens. Plato has Socrates claim, half in jest, that Aspasia taught him rhetoric and even composed the great Funeral Oration that Pericles delivered. The comic poets, meanwhile, savaged her without mercy, mocking her as a low-born seductress and blaming her, in their jesting way, for dragging the city into the Samian and even the Peloponnesian Wars. When enemies could not strike Pericles directly, they prosecuted her for impiety, and he is said to have wept to secure her acquittal.

How much of all this is true is hard to know, for she survives only in the words of men who idealized or reviled her. What is clear is that a foreign woman, denied citizenship and a public voice, impressed herself upon the memory of the most articulate society in history.

The philosophers of Socrates's circle could not leave her alone, and several made her the subject of their dialogues. The one among them who wrote of her and went on to found the hard, ascetic creed of the Cynics was Antisthenes.
