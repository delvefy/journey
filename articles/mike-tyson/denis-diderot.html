Denis Diderot

French philosopher and editor of the Encyclopédie (1713–1784)

Denis Diderot was born at Langres in 1713 and educated by the Jesuits before settling in Paris to live precariously by his pen. His early and daring works of skeptical philosophy, including the Letter on the Blind, alarmed the authorities, and in 1749 he was briefly imprisoned at Vincennes for irreligion.

His life's monument was the Encyclopédie, the vast compendium of Enlightenment knowledge he edited with the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert. Conceived on the model of Francis Bacon's classification of the sciences, it grew over two decades into some twenty-eight volumes, drawing contributions from Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, and many others. Diderot drove the enterprise through repeated censorship, the desertion of his co-editor, and the withdrawal of its official privilege, shielded for a time by Madame de Pompadour.

He was also a restless original writer whose boldest works circulated only in manuscript or appeared after his death: the novels Jacques the Fatalist and The Nun, the dialogue Rameau's Nephew—later translated into German by Goethe—and the materialist speculations of D'Alembert's Dream. In his reviews of the Paris Salons he helped invent modern art criticism.

Often short of money, Diderot was rescued by Catherine the Great of Russia, who bought his library, left it in his keeping, and paid him as its librarian; in 1773 he travelled to St. Petersburg to advise her. He died in Paris in 1784, remembered as one of the founding minds of the Enlightenment and the guiding spirit of its greatest collective work.
