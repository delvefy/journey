Epicurus

Greek philosopher (341–270 BC)

Epicurus, born on the island of Samos to Athenian parents, founded one of the most enduring philosophies of antiquity. In Athens he bought a house with a garden and there gathered his followers—men and women, free and enslaved—into a quiet community that withdrew from politics to pursue wisdom and friendship. From its setting the school took its name: the Garden.

He taught that the world is made wholly of atoms moving in empty space, a physics he took from earlier thinkers and modified with his own ideas. From this materialism he drew a liberating ethics. If the soul is only a fine arrangement of atoms that scatters at death, then death is nothing to us and not to be feared; and if the gods dwell remote and untroubled, then we need not dread their wrath. The highest good is pleasure—but pleasure rightly understood as the absence of pain and the calm of an untroubled mind, won by simple living, modest desires, and the company of friends.

Epicurus wrote voluminously, but almost all of it has perished; his teaching survives chiefly in a few letters and maxims, and in the great Latin poem of his Roman disciple. His rivals among the Stoics scorned his creed, and later ages wrongly turned “epicurean” into a word for indulgence.

He died in 270 BC, by his own account serene amid bodily agony, comforted by the memory of philosophy. The earlier Greek philosopher whose theory of atoms and the void formed the foundation of Epicurus's entire physics was Democritus.
