Frédéric Chopin

Polish composer and poet of the piano (1810–1849)

Frédéric Chopin was the supreme poet of the piano, a composer who poured almost his entire genius into a single instrument and drew from it a new world of feeling. Born near Warsaw and a prodigy from childhood, he left Poland in 1830; when the uprising against Russian rule was crushed soon after, he settled in Paris an exile, and never saw his homeland again. That loss haunted him, and the spirit of Poland breathed through the mazurkas and proud polonaises he never ceased to write.

In Paris he became the darling of the salons, admired by Schumann, befriended by Liszt, and close to the painter Delacroix, who left a famous portrait of him. His nocturnes, preludes, études, ballades, and waltzes are intimate, singing, and harmonically adventurous, perfectly fitted to the hands and to the instrument; he revered Bach and Mozart as his models of purity and form.

His private life was dominated by a long and stormy relationship with the celebrated French novelist George Sand, including a wretched, rain-soaked winter on the island of Mallorca that ruined his fragile health but produced some of his finest music. Their parting hastened his decline.

Always frail, consumptive for years, he died in Paris in 1849, only thirty-nine years old, mourned across Europe. The writer who had been his companion through his most fruitful years was George Sand.
