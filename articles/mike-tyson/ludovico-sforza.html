Ludovico Sforza

Duke of Milan, “il Moro” (1452–1508)

Ludovico Sforza, nicknamed il Moro for his dark looks, was a younger son of the great condottiero Francesco Sforza and rose to rule Milan by patience and cunning. He took power first as regent for his boy nephew, the rightful duke, and held it so completely that when the young man died in 1494 Ludovico simply assumed the ducal title himself, his claim untroubled by the rumors that he had hastened the death.

His court was among the most brilliant in Europe. He drew to Milan the finest minds of the age: Leonardo da Vinci, who painted the Last Supper on a refectory wall and labored for years on a colossal bronze horse, and Bramante, who reshaped the city's churches. Ludovico married the spirited Beatrice d'Este, and around them gathered poets, musicians, and engineers.

But his statecraft proved his ruin. To outflank his Italian rivals he invited Charles VIII of France to march south and claim Naples, and so in 1494 helped unleash the long calamity of the Italian Wars. The French, once summoned, did not forget the rich prize of Milan itself. Louis XII pressed a dynastic claim, invaded, and in 1499 drove Ludovico out.

He regained the city briefly, only to be betrayed by his own Swiss mercenaries and captured at Novara in 1500. Carried off to France, he spent his last years a prisoner in the castle of Loches, where he died in 1508. The father whose sword had won the duchy that Ludovico lost was Francesco Sforza.
