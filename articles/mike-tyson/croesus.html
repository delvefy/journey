Croesus

King of Lydia (reigned c. 585–546 BC)

Croesus, king of Lydia in western Asia Minor, was so fabulously wealthy that his name became a byword for riches in his own day and ever after. The gold that washed down from his mountains filled his treasury at Sardis, and he is credited with striking the world's first true coins of standardized gold and silver. He subdued the Greek cities of the Ionian coast yet loved Greek ways, and showered the oracle at Delphi with dazzling gifts.

His story became, for the Greeks, a parable of fortune's turning. When the Athenian sage Solon visited his glittering court, Croesus asked who was the happiest of men, expecting to hear his own name; instead Solon spoke of humble men who had died well, and warned that no man should be called happy until his life had reached its end. The proud king dismissed the lesson.

Years later, fearing the rising power of Persia, Croesus asked the Delphic oracle whether he should make war, and was told that if he crossed the river Halys he would destroy a great empire. He crossed—and the empire he destroyed was his own.

Cyrus the Great shattered his army, took Sardis, and, the story goes, set Croesus upon a pyre, where the fallen king cried out the name of Solon, understanding at last; Cyrus, struck by the words, spared him and kept him as a counselor. The Athenian whose warning Croesus had scorned, the wise lawgiver who taught that happiness is known only at life's close, was Solon.
