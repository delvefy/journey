Marco Polo

Venetian traveler (1254–1324)

Marco Polo, born in Venice in 1254, became the most celebrated traveler of the Middle Ages and Europe's great window onto the riches of Asia. His father and uncle were merchants who had already ventured east and met the Mongol Great Khan, and in 1271 the teenage Marco joined them on an extraordinary overland journey along the Silk Road, across deserts and mountains, to the court of Kublai Khan, ruler of the vast Mongol empire and emperor of China.

Marco entered the Khan's service and remained in the East for some seventeen years, traveling on imperial business through China and beyond. He marveled at the scale and sophistication he found—great cities, paper money, the burning of "black stones" for fuel, an empire-spanning postal relay—wonders scarcely imaginable to his countrymen.

He returned to Venice by sea after nearly a quarter-century abroad. Soon afterward, captured in a war with Genoa, he passed the time in prison dictating an account of his journeys to a fellow captive, the writer Rustichello of Pisa. The resulting book, The Travels of Marco Polo, became one of the most popular and influential works of the age, even as skeptics dismissed its marvels as exaggerations.

Whatever its embellishments, the book fired European imaginations for centuries and helped inspire the great age of exploration; Christopher Columbus owned a heavily annotated copy. Marco Polo died in Venice in 1324, reportedly insisting he had not told half of what he had seen.
