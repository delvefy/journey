Tacitus

Roman senator and historian (c. 56–120)

Tacitus was the greatest historian of imperial Rome, a senator whose dark, searching prose laid bare the corruption that absolute power works upon men and states. He rose through the full course of senatorial office—serving as consul and later as governor of the rich province of Asia—under the Flavian emperors and their successors, and he married the daughter of the famous general Agricola.

A celebrated orator in his day, Tacitus turned in maturity to history. His shorter works include the Agricola, a loving biography of his father-in-law and conqueror in Britain, and the Germania, a vivid ethnography of the tribes beyond the Rhine that would fascinate—and later be abused by—readers for centuries. But his fame rests on two great masterpieces, the Histories and the Annals, which together chronicle the empire from the death of Augustus through Tiberius, Claudius, and the monstrous reign of Nero, down to the civil wars of the year of four emperors.

He wrote with unequaled compression and moral force, coining phrases that still ring—"they make a desert and call it peace." Beneath his irony lay a tragic vision of liberty lost and tyranny endured, and an unflinching eye for hypocrisy and cruelty in high places.

Much of his work survived only by chance, in fragments rescued by Renaissance scholars. Tacitus reached the height of his own public honors under the soldier-emperor whose reign he lived to praise as a rare age of freedom—Trajan.
