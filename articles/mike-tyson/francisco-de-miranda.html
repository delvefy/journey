Francisco de Miranda

Venezuelan revolutionary, precursor of independence (1750–1816)

Francisco de Miranda lived one of the most extraordinary lives of his century and is remembered as the precursor of Spanish American independence. Born in Caracas, he began as an officer in the Spanish army and fought in the American Revolutionary War before quarrels with his superiors set him on the road as a wandering revolutionary and self-appointed advocate for the freedom of the Americas.

His travels were astonishing. In the newly independent United States he met George Washington and the leading founders; journeying across Europe he was received in Russia by Catherine the Great, who took a great liking to him and extended her protection. In France he became a general of the Revolution, fighting at Valmy, only to be imprisoned and nearly guillotined during the Terror; his name is carved on the Arc de Triomphe.

For decades he lobbied Britain and the United States to back the liberation of Spanish America, and in 1806 he launched a bold but doomed seaborne expedition to Venezuela, raising a flag of his own design.

When revolution finally came, he returned to lead the First Republic of Venezuela. But after its collapse he signed a capitulation that his younger officers—Simón Bolívar among them—deemed a betrayal; they seized him and delivered him to the Spanish. He died in a prison at Cádiz in 1816. Among the statesmen with whom he had schemed for American liberty was Alexander Hamilton.
