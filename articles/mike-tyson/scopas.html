Scopas

Greek sculptor and architect (4th century BC)

Scopas of Paros was one of the three supreme masters of late classical Greek sculpture, ranked with Praxiteles and Lysippos. Where earlier sculptors had sought serene perfection, Scopas brought passion to marble. His figures seem to burn with feeling—turning heads, parted lips, and deep-set, shadowed eyes that convey longing, frenzy, and grief—and in works such as his dancing Maenad, caught in Bacchic ecstasy, he opened the way to the emotional drama of later Greek art.

He was an architect as well as a carver. He designed the temple of Athena Alea at Tegea and sculpted its pediments, and his hand was sought for the grandest projects of the age. He was one of the four great sculptors summoned to adorn the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus for Artemisia II, taking charge of one of its sides, and he is said to have carved one of the columns of the rebuilt Temple of Artemis at Ephesus—so that his work graced more than one of the Seven Wonders of the world.

Across his statues of gods and mortals—an Aphrodite, a brooding figure of Desire, sea-creatures sporting about the gods—Scopas pursued the inner life of his subjects with a power that the Hellenistic age would inherit and magnify.

In all this he worked within the great tradition of Greek sculpture founded in the previous century by its supreme master, Phidias.
