Marlon Brando

American actor who transformed the art of screen acting (1924–2004)

Marlon Brando overturned the conventions of acting and became the most influential film actor of the twentieth century. Trained in the naturalistic “Method,” he brought to the screen a raw, brooding, unpredictable realism that made the polished styles of the past seem suddenly artificial. He exploded into fame as the brutal Stanley Kowalski in A Streetcar Named Desire, and three years later won the Academy Award as the washed-up boxer of On the Waterfront, murmuring the immortal lament that he “coulda been a contender.”

After a restless and uneven middle career he returned in triumph in 1972 as the aging patriarch Don Vito Corleone in The Godfather, a performance of quiet, terrifying authority. He won a second Oscar but refused it, sending an activist in his place to protest Hollywood's treatment of Native Americans. That same year he stunned audiences in Last Tango in Paris, and later loomed out of the jungle as the mad Colonel Kurtz in Apocalypse Now.

Eccentric, rebellious, and increasingly reclusive, he squandered as much as he achieved, yet every serious actor who followed—from James Dean, who idolized him, to the stars of the New Hollywood—worked in his shadow.

The younger actor who played his son and successor as head of the Corleone family, and who inherited his mantle as one of the screen's greatest, was Al Pacino.
