Marcel Proust

French novelist (1871–1922)

Marcel Proust was the author of In Search of Lost Time, the vast novel of memory and society that stands among the supreme achievements of modern literature.

Born into a wealthy Parisian family—his father an eminent physician, his mother cultivated and adored—he was asthmatic and frail from childhood. As a young man he was known chiefly as a brilliant, snobbish frequenter of aristocratic salons, publishing only essays and minor sketches. His true apprenticeship lay in years spent translating and absorbing the English critic he revered, work that taught him to look and to remember.

After the death of his beloved mother he withdrew almost entirely from the world into a cork-lined bedroom and gave himself to a single enormous task. From it came the seven volumes of In Search of Lost Time, whose narrator, tasting a madeleine dipped in tea, finds the whole of his past flooding involuntarily back. In its recurring cast and its anatomy of a vanishing society it owed a deep debt to the method of Honoré de Balzac. The first volume was famously rejected by the publisher's reader André Gide before Proust brought it out at his own expense; the second won the Prix Goncourt and made him celebrated.

At a Paris dinner in 1922 he met the other titan of the age, the novelist James Joyce, though the two giants found little to say to each other. His influence spread quickly: Virginia Woolf read him with awe and envy, and Samuel Beckett devoted his first book to him. He died that same year, his masterpiece not yet fully published. The formative master he had translated and championed was the great Victorian, John Ruskin.
