Robert Hooke

English natural philosopher and polymath (1635–1703)

Robert Hooke, born on the Isle of Wight in 1635, was one of the most versatile experimenters of the Scientific Revolution, sometimes called "England's Leonardo." As Curator of Experiments for the newly founded Royal Society, he devised and demonstrated new experiments week after week for decades, the salaried engine of the Society's early work. He had learned his craft as assistant to Robert Boyle, building the improved air pump with which Boyle established his law of gases.

In 1665 Hooke published Micrographia, a dazzling folio of engravings drawn from his own microscope observations; gazing at a sliver of cork, he named the tiny compartments he saw "cells," giving biology one of its foundational words. He also framed the law of elasticity still taught under his name—that the stretch of a spring is proportional to the force—and made contributions to horology, optics, gravitation, and the study of fossils.

After the Great Fire of London in 1666, Hooke served as a city surveyor and architect, working alongside his friend Christopher Wren to remake the capital; together they raised the Monument to the Fire and other landmarks. His mechanical ingenuity also drew him into a dispute with Christiaan Huygens over the spring-regulated watch.

Hooke's later years were soured by a bitter quarrel with Isaac Newton over the theory of light and over who had first grasped the inverse-square law of gravitation—a feud so deep that Newton, on becoming the Society's president, is said to have let Hooke's memory and likeness fade. Yet Hooke's restless curiosity left its mark across half the sciences, none more than the chemistry he had helped Boyle to found.
