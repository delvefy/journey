Søren Kierkegaard

Danish philosopher and theologian (1813–1855)

Søren Kierkegaard was the melancholy Danish thinker now regarded as the father of existentialism, who set the solitary, choosing individual against the comfortable certainties of his age.

Born in Copenhagen into a wealthy and deeply religious family shadowed by his father's guilt and gloom, he studied theology and took the figure of Socrates as the model of his philosophical method, writing his dissertation on Socratic irony. The decisive event of his private life was his broken engagement to Regine Olsen, whom he loved but renounced in order to give himself wholly to his writing; her memory runs through all his books.

Under a parade of pseudonyms he produced a torrent of brilliant works—Either/Or, Fear and Trembling, The Concept of Anxiety, The Sickness unto Death—exploring dread, despair, and the “leap of faith.” Against the towering systematic philosophy of Hegel, then in fashion, he insisted that truth was subjective and that existence could never be captured in an abstract system. He ended his life in a furious public assault on the smug official Christianity of Denmark.

He died, worn out, at forty-two in 1855, little known beyond Copenhagen. Yet his influence grew immense: he is forever paired with Friedrich Nietzsche as a founder of existential thought, and his ideas of anxiety, choice and authenticity shaped the philosophy of the twentieth century. His own literary career had opened with a stinging review of a novel by his countryman Hans Christian Andersen. The French philosopher who, a century later, would make existentialism a worldwide movement was Jean-Paul Sartre.
