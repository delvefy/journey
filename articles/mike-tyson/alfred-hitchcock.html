Alfred Hitchcock

English film director, "Master of Suspense" (1899–1980)

Alfred Hitchcock was the Master of Suspense, among the most influential and recognisable filmmakers in the history of the medium, a director whose very name became a byword for the cinema of fear.

He learned his craft in the British silent and early sound film, making such thrillers as The 39 Steps and The Lady Vanishes, before crossing to Hollywood, where his first American picture, Rebecca, won the Academy Award for Best Picture. There followed an extraordinary run of masterpieces: Notorious, with Ingrid Bergman and Cary Grant; Rear Window; Vertigo; North by Northwest; and the shocking Psycho, which changed the horror film forever.

His art lay in manipulating the audience—building dread through what the camera withheld, toying with guilt and voyeurism, and chasing the “MacGuffin” that set his plots in motion. He made himself a celebrity as well as an artist, appearing in a fleeting cameo in nearly every film and hosting a popular television series that fixed his rotund silhouette in the public mind.

Famously, he never won a competitive Oscar for direction, an omission long lamented. His films favoured cool, elegant blondes, and among the most luminous was Grace Kelly.
