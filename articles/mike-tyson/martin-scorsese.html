Martin Scorsese

American film director (born 1942)

Martin Scorsese is among the most influential and admired filmmakers in the history of the cinema. Raised in the Little Italy of New York and once drawn toward the priesthood, he brought to his films a deeply Catholic preoccupation with sin, guilt, violence, and the longing for redemption, set most often among the streets and criminals of his native city.

His early masterpieces were made with Robert De Niro: the feverish Taxi Driver, the brutal boxing portrait Raging Bull, and the seductive gangster saga Goodfellas, films that redefined American cinema with their kinetic camerawork, pounding music, and unflinching gaze. He ranged far beyond crime as well—to the religious drama of The Last Temptation of Christ and Silence, the gilded society of The Age of Innocence, and the historical sweep of Gangs of New York.

Long denied the industry's highest honour, he finally won the Academy Award for Best Director with The Departed in 2006. A tireless champion of film history, he founded a foundation to rescue and restore the movies of the past.

In the new century he found a second great muse, a younger star who carried his later films from the slums of old New York to Wall Street and the Osage murders. That actor was Leonardo DiCaprio.
