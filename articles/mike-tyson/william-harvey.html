William Harvey

English physician (1578–1657)

William Harvey was the physician who discovered the circulation of the blood, one of the great turning points in the history of medicine. Born in Folkestone and educated at Cambridge and at the famous medical school of Padua, he combined careful dissection with bold experiment and patient measurement—a thoroughly modern method applied to the oldest questions of the body.

In his epoch-making book of 1628, On the Motion of the Heart and Blood, Harvey demonstrated that the heart is a muscular pump driving the blood in a single continuous circuit around the body. This overthrew the physiology of Galen, which for some fourteen centuries had taught that blood was endlessly created and consumed. Harvey could not see the tiny vessels that closed the loop—the capillaries would await the microscope of Malpighi—but his reasoning was unanswerable.

A man of his age as well as a revolutionary, he drew deeply on the biology of Aristotle, especially in his later pioneering work on embryology. He served as royal physician to James I and then to Charles I, attending the king through the upheavals of the Civil War.

Harvey died in 1657, his discovery the foundation of all later physiology. The anatomical tradition on which he built had been founded a century before by the man who first dissected the human body with scientific rigor and overturned ancient error—Andreas Vesalius.
