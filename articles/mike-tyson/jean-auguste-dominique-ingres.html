Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres

French Neoclassical painter (1780–1867)

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres was the great champion of line and the keeper of the classical flame in nineteenth-century French painting. Born at Montauban, he entered the Paris studio of Jacques-Louis David, won the Prix de Rome, and spent much of his life in Italy, where he steeped himself in antiquity and in the art he worshipped above all others, that of Raphael. He later directed the French Academy in Rome.

His art prized precise contour, smooth finish, and ideal form. He painted the cool, elongated nudes of the Grande Odalisque and the Valpinçon Bather, the swarming Turkish Bath, the solemn Apotheosis of Homer, and grand state portraits, among them Napoleon enthroned in imperial majesty. His penetrating likeness of the press magnate Monsieur Bertin became an emblem of the bourgeois age.

Ingres stood as the implacable opposite of Eugène Delacroix: their quarrel of disciplined line against passionate colour, Neoclassicism against Romanticism, divided the art world for a generation. A keen amateur violinist—he gave his name to the French phrase for a beloved hobby—he moved easily among musicians and, in Rome, drew a famous portrait of the violin virtuoso Niccolò Paganini.

His insistence on draughtsmanship passed directly to his admirer Edgar Degas, and his pure line would later be prized by Henri Matisse and by Pablo Picasso in his classical vein. He died, laden with honours, in Paris in 1867.
