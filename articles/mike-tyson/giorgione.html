Giorgione

Venetian Renaissance painter (c. 1477–1510)

Giorgione was the enigmatic young master of Venice who invented a new kind of painting—poetic, dreamlike and saturated with mood—before dying in his early thirties and leaving scholars to puzzle over him ever since.

Born at Castelfranco and trained in the great workshop of Giovanni Bellini, he absorbed the soft, shadowed modelling that the example of Leonardo da Vinci had carried into northern Italy and turned it toward a wholly original end. In small, mysterious canvases such as The Tempest and The Three Philosophers, and in his pioneering reclining nude, the Sleeping Venus, landscape and atmosphere became the true subject, the human figures merely dwelling within a charged, melancholy air. These “poesie” had no clear story; they were paintings to be felt rather than read.

He worked for a time hand in hand with the young Titian, the two collaborating on frescoes and painting so alike that even now their hands are hard to tell apart; after Giorgione's death Titian completed several of his unfinished pictures. So little was recorded of his short life that the painter-biographer Giorgio Vasari, gathering tales a generation later, told—as tradition—that he was a fine musician and that he caught the plague from a beloved who was already infected.

That plague carried him off in Venice in 1510, his work scarcely begun and his legend already forming. Another talented painter who came out of his circle, finished some of his designs, and carried the Venetian manner to Rome was Sebastiano del Piombo.
