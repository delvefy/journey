Muhammad Ali

American boxer and activist (1942–2016)

Muhammad Ali, born Cassius Marcellus Clay Jr. in Louisville, Kentucky, in 1942, was an American heavyweight boxer widely regarded as the greatest in the sport's history and one of the most significant cultural figures of the twentieth century. He took up boxing as a boy and won a light-heavyweight gold medal at the 1960 Rome Olympics.

In 1964 the brash young challenger shocked the world by defeating the feared champion Sonny Liston. Soon after, he announced his membership in the Nation of Islam and adopted the name Muhammad Ali, rejecting what he called his "slave name." His conversion and his friendship with Malcolm X made him a lightning rod in a divided America.

In 1967 Ali refused induction into the U.S. Army during the Vietnam War, citing his religious convictions. Stripped of his title and barred from boxing for over three years, he became a symbol of principled resistance. He returned to lose the "Fight of the Century" to Joe Frazier in 1971, then authored two of boxing's greatest triumphs: the 1974 "Rumble in the Jungle," where his rope-a-dope tactics felled the powerful George Foreman in Zaire, and the brutal 1975 "Thrilla in Manila" against Frazier.

Ali lost and regained the heavyweight crown, becoming the first three-time lineal champion. His later years were marked by Parkinson's disease, yet he remained a beloved global humanitarian; his trembling lighting of the Olympic flame in Atlanta in 1996 moved millions. Famous for his wit, poetry, and conviction that he was "The Greatest," he died in 2016, mourned worldwide.
