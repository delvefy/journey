Suleiman the Magnificent

Ottoman sultan (1494–1566)

Suleiman I, known to Europe as the Magnificent and to his own people as Kanuni, "the Lawgiver," was the tenth and greatest of the Ottoman sultans. Ruling from 1520 until his death in 1566—the longest reign in the dynasty's history—he carried the empire to the summit of its power, wealth, and splendor, and consciously cast himself as a second Solomon, a just and universal sovereign.

He was a relentless conqueror. He took Belgrade and the fortress island of Rhodes early in his reign, shattered the kingdom of Hungary at the Battle of Mohács in 1526, and in 1529 carried his armies to the very walls of Vienna—the high-water mark of Ottoman expansion into Europe. On the seas his admiral Hayreddin Barbarossa made the Mediterranean an Ottoman lake. His great rival was the Habsburg emperor Charles V, against whom he even allied with Francis I of France.

At home Suleiman earned his Turkish title by reforming and codifying the empire's laws. He was a lavish patron of the arts, and under the genius of his architect Sinan the skyline of Istanbul was crowned with mosques such as the Süleymaniye. His powerful wife, Roxelana, broke precedent to become a force in palace politics.

Suleiman died in 1566 while campaigning in Hungary, his empire stretching across three continents. His reign is remembered as the golden age of Ottoman civilization, and his rivalry with the Christian powers shaped the Europe of his time.
