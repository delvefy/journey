Chiang Kai-shek

Chinese Nationalist leader (1887–1975)

Chiang Kai-shek led the Chinese Nationalists for half a century, first as ruler of the Republic of China on the mainland and then, after his defeat, on the island of Taiwan. A soldier by training and a protégé of Sun Yat-sen, the founder of the Republic, he rose to command the Kuomintang after his mentor's death and led the Northern Expedition that nominally reunited a fractured China under his government.

In 1927 he turned violently on his communist allies, opening the long civil war against the party of Mao Zedong that would shape the rest of his life. When Japan invaded, Chiang led the vast, grinding resistance of the Second Sino-Japanese War, and was raised to the company of the great Allied leaders; with his American-educated, Christian wife Soong Mei-ling at his side as interpreter and advocate, he met Franklin D. Roosevelt and Winston Churchill at the Cairo Conference to plan the defeat of Japan.

Victory over Japan brought no peace. Weakened by corruption and ruinous inflation, his armies were overwhelmed by the communists, and in 1949 Chiang withdrew with his government and gold to Taiwan. There he ruled with an iron hand until his death in 1975, never relinquishing the claim that his was the true government of all China, even as he laid the foundations of the island's prosperity.

His wartime partner among the Allied "Big Four," and the architect of America's part in the struggle, was Franklin D. Roosevelt.
