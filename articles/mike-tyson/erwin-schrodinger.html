Erwin Schrödinger

Austrian physicist (1887–1961)

Erwin Schrödinger was born in Vienna in 1887 and brought a broad, humanist culture to his physics. In 1926, taking up Louis de Broglie's startling idea that matter has a wavelike nature, he wrote down the equation that governs the quantum wavefunction. This wave mechanics offered a more intuitive picture than the rival matrix mechanics of Werner Heisenberg, and the two were soon shown to be mathematically one and the same. It brought him the Nobel Prize in 1933.

The year before, he had succeeded Max Planck in the chair of theoretical physics at Berlin, but he left Germany in disgust when the Nazis took power and, after wanderings, settled in Dublin.

Schrödinger is known to the wider world for a thought experiment. Arguing with Albert Einstein about the meaning of the quantum theory, he imagined a cat sealed in a box with a device that might or might not kill it, so that until observed the animal must be reckoned both alive and dead—a vivid protest against the strangeness of superposition. In the same exchanges he gave the name “entanglement” to the deep linkage of quantum particles.

Late in life his short book What Is Life? suggested that heredity is carried by a molecular code, inspiring the young scientists who would uncover the structure of DNA. He died in Vienna in 1961.
