Cimon

Athenian general and statesman (c. 510–450 BC)

Cimon was the leading man of Athens in the generation after the Persian Wars, and the son of Miltiades, the victor of Marathon. He began under a shadow, for his father had died disgraced and burdened with a ruinous fine, which the young Cimon laboured to pay off. Rising through the war against Persia, he became the dominant Athenian of the 470s and 460s—a brave, open-handed, aristocratic figure, conservative at home and friendly to Sparta abroad.

As commander of the Delian League he carried the fight relentlessly to the Persian empire. His crowning feat was the double victory at the river Eurymedon, where in a single day he destroyed a great Persian fleet and then routed the army drawn up to support it, driving Persian power from the Aegean and confirming Athenian leadership of the Greek alliance. He used his wealth lavishly for the public good, beautifying Athens and opening his gardens to the people.

But his pro-Spartan policy proved his undoing. When Sparta, in the midst of a helot revolt, contemptuously dismissed the Athenian force he had led to its aid, the humiliation discredited him, and the democratic reformers—Ephialtes and the rising Pericles—had him ostracised. His rivalry with Themistocles before, and with Pericles after, frames the politics of his age.

Recalled in time, he died around 450 BC while besieging the Persians in Cyprus. His life was later told by Plutarch. His great ally and elder in the conservative cause was Aristides the Just.
