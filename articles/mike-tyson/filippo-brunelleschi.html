Filippo Brunelleschi

Florentine architect and engineer (1377–1446)

Filippo Brunelleschi began as a goldsmith and ended as the father of Renaissance architecture. In 1401 he competed with Lorenzo Ghiberti for the commission to make the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery; defeated, or unwilling to share the work, he turned away from sculpture toward building and, by tradition, went to Rome with Donatello to study the engineering of the ancients among the ruins.

His supreme achievement was the dome of Santa Maria del Fiore, the cathedral of Florence. The crossing had stood open for decades because no one knew how to vault so vast a span. Brunelleschi proposed to raise it without the usual forest of wooden centering, and from 1420 he did so, building a double shell of brick laid in a self-supporting herringbone pattern and inventing the great hoisting machines that lifted the materials. The dome was closed in 1436 and remains the largest masonry dome ever built.

He was also the man who, through painted panels of the Baptistery, demonstrated the geometry of linear perspective, handing painters a rational system for representing space—an idea soon codified in writing by Alberti. His other buildings, the Foundling Hospital, the Old Sacristy of San Lorenzo, and the Pazzi Chapel, established the calm, proportioned classical style of the age.

Brunelleschi died in 1446 and was honored with burial inside the cathedral his dome crowns. His solution to the dome would awe and instruct later builders, not least Michelangelo at St. Peter's. The friend and theorist who turned his discoveries into the first manual of the new art, and who praised the dome to all Italy, was Leon Battista Alberti.
