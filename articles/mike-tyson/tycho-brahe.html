Tycho Brahe

Danish astronomer (1546–1601)

Tycho Brahe, a Danish nobleman born in 1546, was the greatest observer of the heavens in the age before the telescope. Colourful and combative—he lost part of his nose in a youthful duel and wore a metal replacement ever after—he won the patronage of King Frederick II, who gave him the island of Hven. There he built Uraniborg, a magnificent observatory and research institute, and for some twenty years made measurements of the stars and planets of a precision no one had ever achieved with the naked eye.

His observations shook the old cosmos. In 1572 he saw a brilliant new star blaze forth in Cassiopeia, proof that the heavens were not changeless as Aristotle had taught; and from the great comet of 1577 he showed that such bodies travel far beyond the Moon, through space that should have been filled with solid crystalline spheres.

Yet Tycho could not bring himself to set the Earth in motion. He devised instead a compromise of his own, in which the Sun and Moon circle a fixed Earth while the other planets circle the Sun—a halfway house between Ptolemy and the bold new system of Nicolaus Copernicus.

Quarrelling with a new king, he left Denmark and became Imperial Mathematician in Prague, where in 1600 he took on a brilliant young assistant, Johannes Kepler. When Tycho died the next year, that incomparable treasury of observations passed to Kepler, who wrung from it the laws of planetary motion. As an observer Tycho stood comparison only with the ancient master Hipparchus.
