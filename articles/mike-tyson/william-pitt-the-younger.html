William Pitt the Younger

British prime minister (1759–1806)

William Pitt the Younger was born in 1759, the second son of the great war minister William Pitt the Elder. Precociously brilliant, he went to Cambridge at fourteen and entered Parliament at twenty-one. In December 1783, aged just twenty-four, he was appointed prime minister—the youngest in British history. Mocked at first as a schoolboy ministry, he confounded his critics by winning a decisive general election the following year.

His early years in office were devoted to recovery after the loss of America. Drawing on the free-trade ideas of Adam Smith, he reformed the customs, attacked smuggling, established a sinking fund to reduce the national debt, and negotiated a commercial treaty with France. He favoured parliamentary reform and supported his close friend William Wilberforce in the campaign against the slave trade, though neither cause succeeded during his ministry.

The French Revolution transformed his career. Alarmed, like Edmund Burke, by its spread, Pitt led Britain into a long war against Revolutionary and then Napoleonic France, organising and financing successive coalitions while suspending civil liberties at home and carrying the Act of Union with Ireland. He resigned in 1801 when George III refused to permit Catholic emancipation.

Recalled in 1804 to confront Napoleon Bonaparte, he forged a third coalition, only to see it shattered at Austerlitz. Worn out and gravely ill, he died early in 1806 at the age of forty-six, having dominated British politics for more than two decades.
