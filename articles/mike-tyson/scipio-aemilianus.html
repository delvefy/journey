Scipio Aemilianus

Roman general and statesman (185–129 BC)

Born a son of the conqueror of Macedon, Aemilius Paullus, and adopted into the most illustrious military house of Rome, the Scipios, this young man inherited two of the proudest names of the Republic. He more than lived up to them. In 146 BC, given command in the Third Punic War, he stormed and utterly destroyed Carthage, Rome's ancient rival, leaving the great city a smoking ruin—and, his friend the historian Polybius beside him, wept at the sight, foreseeing that Rome too might one day fall.

Years later he ended another long agony, the bitter Spanish war, by taking and razing the fortress of Numantia, adding a second triumphal surname to his name.

Around him gathered the most cultivated company in Rome, the Scipionic Circle, where Roman gravity met Greek philosophy and letters—the historian Polybius, the Stoic Panaetius, the playwright Terence. Yet his politics set him against the popular reforms of his own kinsmen the Gracchi, and when he died suddenly in 129 BC many whispered of murder. Cicero would later make him the noble voice of the Republic and the dreamer of the famous Dream of Scipio.

He carried the name Africanus by adoption from the greatest soldier of the age before his own, the general who had finally broken Hannibal and won the long war with Carthage—his adoptive grandfather, Scipio Africanus.
