Jean le Rond d'Alembert

French mathematician and philosopher (1717–1783)

Jean le Rond d'Alembert was born in Paris in 1717, the illegitimate child of a society hostess, and was left as a foundling on the steps of the church of Saint-Jean-le-Rond, from which he took his name. Raised by a glazier's family but quietly supported by his father, he showed early mathematical genius and was admitted to the Academy of Sciences while still in his twenties.

In his Treatise on Dynamics of 1743 he set out the principle that still bears his name, reducing problems of motion to conditions of equilibrium. He went on to fundamental work on the vibrating string and the wave equation, on fluid motion, on the precession of the equinoxes, and on the three-body problem, ranking with Leonhard Euler and Daniel Bernoulli among the founders of mathematical physics, though his priority disputes with Euler were bitter and lifelong.

With Denis Diderot he became co-editor of the great Encyclopédie, for which he wrote the celebrated Preliminary Discourse, a survey of the order and progress of human knowledge that stands as a manifesto of the Enlightenment. Worn down by the controversies that swirled around the work, he gave up the editorship in the late 1750s.

A friend of Voltaire and a fixture of the Paris salons, especially that of Julie de Lespinasse, he declined Frederick the Great's offer to head the Berlin Academy and Catherine the Great's invitation to tutor her son, preferring his modest Parisian life. As permanent secretary of the French Academy he wrote eulogies of its members. He died in Paris in 1783.
