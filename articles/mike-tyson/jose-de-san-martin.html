José de San Martín

Liberator of Argentina, Chile, and Peru (1778–1850)

José de San Martín was the great liberator of the southern half of Spanish South America and, with Bolívar, one of the two architects of the continent's independence. Born at Yapeyú in the Río de la Plata, he was taken to Spain as a boy and spent more than two decades in the Spanish army, fighting Moors, Portuguese, and—in the Peninsular War—the invading forces of Napoleon, in whose campaigns he learned his trade.

In 1812 he sailed home to offer his sword to the revolution. Rather than fight the Spanish where they were strongest, he conceived a strategy of breathtaking scale: he raised and drilled the Army of the Andes at Mendoza and in 1817 led it over the high cordillera, descending to shatter the royalists at Chacabuco and Maipú and to secure the freedom of Chile alongside Bernardo O'Higgins.

He then carried the war by sea to the viceregal heartland of Peru, entered Lima, and proclaimed its independence in 1821, governing briefly as Protector. But the task was unfinished, and the decisive figures of the north were closing in.

In 1822 he met Simón Bolívar in a celebrated private conference at Guayaquil. What passed between them is unknown, but San Martín emerged to renounce his command and leave the field to Bolívar. Shunning power and honours, he sailed for Europe and died in quiet exile at Boulogne-sur-Mer in 1850, revered ever after as the father of his country.
