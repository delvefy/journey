Mahavira

The twenty-fourth Tirthankara and great teacher of Jainism (c. 599–527 BC)

Mahavira—“the Great Hero”—was born Vardhamana, by tradition a prince of the Jnatrika clan near Vaishali, in the region of present-day Bihar. Jains revere him as the twenty-fourth and last of the Tirthankaras, the “ford-makers” who show the way across the ocean of rebirth; he did not found Jainism so much as reform and crystallize a tradition held to be far older.

According to the accounts, he renounced his comfortable life at about the age of thirty and became a wandering ascetic, going without possessions, enduring hardship and meditation for some twelve years until he attained kevala—perfect knowledge. For the rest of his life he wandered the towns of the Ganges plain preaching, a near contemporary of the Buddha in the same country, and like him supported by the kings of Magadha.

His teaching rested on great vows binding upon his followers: above all ahimsa, non-violence toward every living being, together with truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity, and non-attachment to possessions. He taught anekantavada, the doctrine that reality has many sides and no single view captures it whole. He organized his disciples into a fourfold community of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen.

By tradition he attained final liberation at Pavapuri, an event Jains commemorate at the festival of Diwali. His insistence on non-violence ran deep into Indian civilization and echoed, more than two thousand years later, in the creed of Mahatma Gandhi. The king of Magadha who extended his protection to both Mahavira and the Buddha was Bimbisara.
