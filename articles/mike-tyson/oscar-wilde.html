Oscar Wilde

Irish poet and playwright (1854–1900)

Oscar Wilde was born in Dublin in 1854 and shone as a classical scholar at Trinity College and then at Oxford, where the teaching of John Ruskin and Walter Pater drew him toward aestheticism and the creed of art for art's sake. He made himself a celebrity of wit, dress, and paradox, and on a lecture tour of America in 1882 sought out and spent a memorable afternoon with the poet Walt Whitman.

His pen matched his reputation. He wrote the unsettling novel The Picture of Dorian Gray, delicate fairy tales, and a string of glittering society comedies—Lady Windermere's Fan, An Ideal Husband, and the flawless The Importance of Being Earnest—whose epigrams made him the most quotable playwright of his day. He admired above all the beauty-worship of John Keats.

At the summit of his success Wilde sued the father of his lover Lord Alfred Douglas for libel. The suit collapsed disastrously, and he was himself prosecuted and convicted of gross indecency, then a crime, and sentenced to two years of hard labour.

Prison broke his health and his fortune but produced his anguished letter De Profundis and The Ballad of Reading Gaol. Bankrupt and disgraced, he went into exile in France and died in a Paris hotel in 1900, only forty-six, later mourned as both a martyr and a wit for the ages.
