H. G. Wells

English writer (1866–1946)

Herbert George Wells was born near London in 1866 into a struggling lower-middle-class family. A scholarship carried him to study biology in South Kensington under Thomas Henry Huxley, the great champion of Darwin, and the evolutionary vision he absorbed there ran through everything he wrote.

In a brilliant burst in the 1890s he created the modern scientific romance. The Time Machine, The Island of Doctor Moreau, The Invisible Man, and The War of the Worlds imagined time travel, the surgical making of beasts into men, invisibility, and invasion from Mars, each shadowed by Darwinian and social anxieties. With these he became, alongside Jules Verne, a founder of science fiction.

Wells was also a tireless social prophet. A socialist and member of the Fabian Society, where he sparred with George Bernard Shaw, he wrote warm comic novels of ordinary English life and campaigned for a rational world state. He foresaw tanks, aerial bombing, and even atomic weapons, and coined the hopeful phrase “the war that will end war.”

His later Outline of History sold in the millions and made him a global sage who met figures from Lenin to Roosevelt, and who counted Winston Churchill among his long acquaintances. Friend of Joseph Conrad and Henry James, he quarreled famously with the latter over the purpose of the novel. He died in 1946, his bright hopes for humanity darkened by two world wars.
