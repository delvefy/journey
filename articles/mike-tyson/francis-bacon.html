Francis Bacon

English philosopher and statesman (1561–1626)

Francis Bacon, born in London in 1561 to a high officer of Elizabeth I's government, rose to become one of England's great statesmen and the herald of a new philosophy of science. Trained in the law, he sat in Parliament and served at the Elizabethan court, and under King James I climbed to the highest legal offices, becoming Attorney General, then Lord Chancellor, and being ennobled as Viscount St Alban.

His political career ended in disgrace in 1621, when he confessed to taking gifts from litigants and was stripped of office, fined, and briefly imprisoned. Freed, he turned the remaining years wholly to writing, completing works that would outlast any judgment against him.

Bacon is remembered above all as the prophet of the experimental method. In the Novum Organum he proposed a "new instrument" of knowledge to replace the deductive logic of Aristotle, urging that truth be drawn from patient observation and systematic experiment rather than received authority. His vision of organised, collaborative research, sketched in the utopian New Atlantis, would later inspire the founders of the Royal Society. He gave the English language the polished essay and the conviction, often condensed as "knowledge is power."

Fittingly, his death in 1626 came of an experiment: he caught a fatal chill while stuffing a fowl with snow to test whether cold preserved flesh. His empirical creed passed to the experimenters Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton, and at the start of his philosophical career he had employed as his secretary a young man who would become a great thinker in his own right, Thomas Hobbes.
