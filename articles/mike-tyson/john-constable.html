John Constable

English Romantic landscape painter (1776–1837)

John Constable was the painter of the quiet English countryside, whose devotion to the truth of skies and fields helped revolutionize landscape art.

Born in the Suffolk village of East Bergholt, the son of a wealthy miller, he made the watery Stour valley around his home—ever since called “Constable Country”—the subject of his life's work. Rejecting the idealized scenery then in fashion, he painted what he saw directly from nature, filling sketchbooks with fresh oil studies and devoting endless attention to the movement of clouds, a practice he called “skying.” He revered the earlier Suffolk landscapist Thomas Gainsborough and studied the rich landscapes of Peter Paul Rubens.

Recognition came slowly in England, where his loose, flickering brushwork and flecks of white light puzzled the academy, and he was elected a full Academician only in his fifties. His rival for the crown of English landscape was the tempestuous J. M. W. Turner, whose blazing visions stood at the very opposite pole from Constable's calm, green naturalism.

Abroad his impact was immediate and electric. When The Hay Wain was shown at the Paris Salon of 1824 it won a gold medal and stunned the French painters; Eugène Delacroix, it was said, hurried back to repaint the background of his own great canvas after seeing it, and a whole generation of French landscapists took the lesson to heart. Constable died in 1837, more honoured after his death than during it. The serene classical master whose ideal landscapes he had copied and absorbed in his youth was Claude Lorrain.
