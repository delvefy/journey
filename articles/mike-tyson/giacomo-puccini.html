Giacomo Puccini

Italian opera composer (1858–1924)

Giacomo Puccini was the last great master of Italian opera and the composer whose works remain among the most performed in the world. Born in Lucca into a long line of church musicians, he turned instead to the theatre after hearing Verdi's Aida as a young man, and trained at the Milan Conservatory. His first lasting success, Manon Lescaut, announced a gift for ravishing melody wedded to a sure instinct for the stage.

There followed the operas that conquered every house: the tender bohemian tragedy of La Bohème, the political thriller Tosca, and the heartbreaking Madama Butterfly, whose disastrous opening night he lived to see transformed into one of his greatest triumphs. He drew on the new verismo taste for raw emotion while enriching his scores with a chromatic, sometimes Wagnerian, harmonic palette.

His later years brought the American-set La fanciulla del West, the one-act triptych Il trittico, and the exotic Turandot, with its blazing aria “Nessun dorma.” A heavy smoker, he died of throat cancer in Brussels in 1924, leaving Turandot unfinished; at its premiere the conductor laid down his baton where Puccini's writing had stopped.

He stood as the direct heir of Giuseppe Verdi in the line of Italian opera. When La fanciulla del West had its world premiere at New York's Metropolitan Opera in 1910, its leading role was created by the most celebrated voice of the age, the tenor Enrico Caruso.
