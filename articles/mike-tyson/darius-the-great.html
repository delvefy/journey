Darius the Great

King of the Persian Empire (c. 550–486 BC)

Darius I, called the Great, ruled the Achaemenid Empire at its height and is remembered above all as its supreme organizer. Born around 550 BC, he seized the throne in 522 BC amid the turmoil following the death of Cambyses, joining a band of seven noble conspirators who killed a usurper. Not of the senior royal line, he secured his legitimacy by marrying Atossa, a daughter of Cyrus the Great, and by proclaiming his version of events in the great trilingual rock relief at Behistun—an inscription that centuries later provided the key to deciphering cuneiform.

Darius gave the sprawling empire lasting structure. He divided it into provinces called satrapies under royal governors, regularized taxation, minted a reliable gold coinage, and built the Royal Road for couriers and the first ceremonial palaces at Persepolis. A canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea and an expedition toward the Indus showed the reach of his ambition.

His later years were dominated by the Greeks. When the Ionian cities of Asia Minor revolted with Athenian help, Darius crushed them and resolved to punish Athens. The expedition he sent was famously defeated on the plain of Marathon in 490 BC, a humiliation that became legend in Greek memory.

Darius died in 486 BC while preparing a second, larger invasion of Greece, bequeathing the war and the throne to his son Xerxes. The historian Herodotus made his reign a centerpiece of the Histories.
