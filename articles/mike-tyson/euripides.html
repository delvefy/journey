Euripides

Athenian tragic poet (c. 480–406 BC)

Euripides was the youngest of the three great tragedians of classical Athens, and in time the most widely read of them all. Born around 480 BC, he composed some ninety plays, of which more survive than of Aeschylus and Sophocles combined—among them Medea, Hippolytus, The Trojan Women, Electra, and The Bacchae. Drawing his stories from the myths first sung by Homer, he reshaped them into searching dramas of human passion and suffering.

What set Euripides apart was his realism and his daring. He gave the stage psychologically complex, often transgressive characters, and powerful, terrifying women such as Medea and Phaedra. He questioned the justice and even the existence of the gods, brought everyday speech and humble figures into the lofty form of tragedy, and probed the irrational forces in the human soul. To many he is the most modern of the ancient dramatists.

In his own lifetime his innovations made him controversial; he won the prize at the festivals far less often than his rivals, and the comic poet Aristophanes pilloried him mercilessly, most famously in The Frogs. He moved in the questioning intellectual world of Socrates, with whom tradition linked him as a friend.

Late in life he left Athens for the court of Macedonia, where he died around 406 BC; his masterpiece, The Bacchae, was staged only after his death. Aristotle, weighing the tragic poets in his Poetics, called Euripides "the most tragic" of them all, and his influence ran through Roman drama down to the French stage of Racine. Yet the founder of the art he perfected was the elder poet Aeschylus.
