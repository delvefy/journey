John Knox

Scottish reformer, founder of Presbyterianism (c. 1514–1572)

John Knox was the thundering prophet of the Scottish Reformation, the man who made Scotland a Protestant nation and founded the Presbyterian tradition. Born near Haddington, he abandoned the Catholic priesthood for the reformed cause, inspired by the preacher George Wishart, whose burning at the stake hardened Knox's resolve. Captured after a rebellion, he spent some nineteen months chained as a slave at the oar of a French galley—an ordeal that left him unbreakable.

Freed at last, he preached in England under Edward VI and then took refuge in Geneva, where he sat under John Calvin and called the city "the most perfect school of Christ" since the days of the apostles. Returning to Scotland in 1559, his fierce sermons set the country ablaze with reformation. Within a year the Scottish Parliament threw off the authority of Rome and established a Reformed Church, the Kirk, governed not by bishops but by elders.

Knox was a fearless and often merciless controversialist. He published The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women, denouncing the rule of queens—a tract that offended Protestant and Catholic sovereigns alike, including Elizabeth I of England.

His greatest battles were with the young Catholic queen who returned from France to rule his newly Protestant land. In a series of famous confrontations he denounced her faith to her face and worked relentlessly against her. That queen, whose tragic life and death would echo through British history, was Mary, Queen of Scots.
