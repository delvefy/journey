Andrea Palladio

Venetian architect (1508–1580)

Andrea Palladio began life as a humble stonemason in Padua and rose to become the most imitated architect in the history of the West. His fortune turned when the humanist Gian Giorgio Trissino noticed his talent, took him into his household, carried him to Rome to measure the ancient ruins, and gave him the classical-sounding name by which the world would know him.

Steeped in Vitruvius and in the remains of antiquity, Palladio built for the landowning nobility of the Venetian mainland a series of villas of serene mathematical harmony, none more perfect than the Villa Rotonda near Vicenza, a domed cube with a temple front on each of its four faces. At Maser he raised the Villa Barbaro, its rooms frescoed by Veronese. In Vicenza itself he wrapped the medieval town hall in luminous classical loggias and built, at the last, the Teatro Olimpico.

For Venice he designed the white churches of San Giorgio Maggiore and Il Redentore, whose facades rise like classical temples from the water. In 1570 he gathered his principles and designs into The Four Books of Architecture, a treatise that taught his manner to every reader.

Through that book his style crossed the seas. It shaped the English country house through Inigo Jones, and in America it gave Thomas Jefferson the model for Monticello and the University of Virginia. Goethe made a pilgrimage to his buildings and wrote of them with awe. Yet Palladio himself looked back to an earlier master who had first revived the grand classical manner in Rome—Donato Bramante.
