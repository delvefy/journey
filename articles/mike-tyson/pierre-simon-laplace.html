Pierre-Simon Laplace

French mathematician and astronomer (1749–1827)

Pierre-Simon Laplace was born in Normandy in 1749 to a family of modest means. Arriving in Paris as a young man, he impressed Jean le Rond d'Alembert, who helped him to a teaching post and entry into the Academy of Sciences, and he soon established himself as the foremost mathematical astronomer of the age.

His masterwork, the five-volume Celestial Mechanics, applied the gravitation of Isaac Newton to the entire solar system and argued that its apparent irregularities were self-correcting, so that the system was fundamentally stable. He proposed the nebular hypothesis for the origin of the planets and was hailed as the Newton of France. When Napoleon remarked that he found no mention of God in the work, Laplace is said to have replied that he had no need of that hypothesis.

Laplace was equally a founder of mathematical probability. His Analytic Theory of Probabilities developed methods of inference and the idea, since called Laplace's demon, that a sufficient intelligence knowing every force and position could predict the whole future. With Antoine Lavoisier he carried out careful experiments on heat and respiration using an ice calorimeter.

A supple political survivor, he came through the Revolution, was appointed Minister of the Interior by Napoleon for a brief and undistinguished tenure before being moved to the Senate, and was honored with titles under both the Empire and the restored Bourbon kings. He died in Paris in 1827.
