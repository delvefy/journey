Zeno of Elea

Greek philosopher of Elea (c. 490–430 BC)

Zeno of Elea was the devoted pupil of Parmenides, and he set himself to defend his master's startling doctrine that all is one and unchanging. His method was to turn the tables on common sense: rather than argue directly for the One, he showed that the ordinary belief in a world of many moving things leads straight into absurdity.

His paradoxes are still famous. Swift Achilles, he argued, can never overtake a tortoise with a head start, for he must always first reach the point the tortoise has left, and so on without end. To cross any distance one must first cross its half, and half of that, forever—so motion can never even begin. A flying arrow, at each instant, occupies a space just its own size and is therefore at rest; how then does it move? Each puzzle seemed to prove that motion and plurality are impossible, exactly as Parmenides had said.

Aristotle honored him as the inventor of dialectic, and his arguments forced thinkers to grapple with infinity, continuity, and the divisibility of space and time—riddles that would not be fully untangled until the mathematics of the infinite, two thousand years later. Tradition told that he died a hero, biting out his tongue under torture rather than betray fellow conspirators against a tyrant.

The Eleatic challenge that Zeno pressed so sharply demanded answers, and the philosopher of Sicily who replied—preserving eternal being yet restoring the changing world by blending four immortal elements under the powers of Love and Strife—was Empedocles. But Zeno was not the last of the school. The third great Eleatic, who carried on the defense of the One and who, as a Samian admiral, even defeated the fleet of Athens in battle, was Melissus of Samos.
