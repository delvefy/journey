Mahakasyapa

Foremost ascetic disciple of the Buddha and first patriarch of Chan (5th century BC)

Mahakasyapa was one of the principal disciples of the Buddha, honoured above all for his austerity. The traditions describe him as the foremost keeper of the strict ascetic practices—wearing rag-robes, eating only alms, dwelling in the forest—and tell that the Buddha once paid him the singular honour of exchanging robes with him, a sign of his standing in the community.

After the Buddha's death he emerged as the natural leader of the leaderless community. It was Mahakasyapa who, alarmed that the teaching might be corrupted or forgotten, summoned the surviving elders to the first great council at Rajagriha and presided over it, having Ananda recite the discourses and the monk Upali recite the rules of discipline, so that the word of the Buddha was gathered and fixed.

In the Chan and Zen schools that later flourished in East Asia, Mahakasyapa is revered as the first patriarch, the heir of a wordless transmission passed directly from the Buddha's mind to his own. The famous story tells how the Buddha, before an assembly, simply held up a single flower; of all those present only Mahakasyapa understood and smiled, and so received the teaching beyond words and scriptures.

By tradition he withdrew at last into meditation within a mountain, to remain there until the coming of the future Buddha. The patriarch who, generations afterward, was said to carry this same transmission across the mountains into China was Bodhidharma.
