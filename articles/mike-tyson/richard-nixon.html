Richard Nixon

37th President of the United States (1913–1994)

Richard Nixon was one of the most consequential and most tragic figures in American political history. He made his name as a fierce anti-Communist congressman, served two terms as Eisenhower's vice president, and then suffered a narrow, bitter defeat in the 1960 presidential race. After a remarkable comeback he won the White House in 1968.

As president he proved a bold and far-sighted statesman in foreign affairs. Working with his adviser Henry Kissinger, he stunned the world in 1972 by travelling to Beijing to meet Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai, ending decades of estrangement and reshaping the global balance; he pursued détente with the Soviet Union and wound down America's war in Vietnam.

Yet his presidency collapsed in scandal. A break-in at the Democratic headquarters in the Watergate complex, and the cover-up he directed from the Oval Office, unravelled into a constitutional crisis. Facing certain impeachment, in August 1974 he became the only president ever to resign the office, departing in disgrace.

The young senator who had narrowly beaten him for the presidency in 1960—in the first televised debates, which Nixon's haggard appearance is said to have lost him—was John F. Kennedy.
