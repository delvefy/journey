Madhava of Sangamagrama

Founder of the Kerala school of mathematics (c. 1340–1425)

Madhava of Sangamagrama was a mathematician and astronomer of Kerala, in southern India, and the founder of the Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics. Working in near-total isolation from the rest of the mathematical world, he made discoveries that would not be matched in Europe for two and a half centuries.

Madhava's supreme achievement was the discovery of infinite series. He found the power series for the sine, the cosine, and the arctangent—the very expansions that Newton, Gregory, and Leibniz would later derive independently—and used them to compute these functions with extraordinary precision. From the arctangent series he obtained a formula for pi and calculated it correctly to eleven decimal places.

In reaching these results he was, in effect, doing the work of the calculus: summing infinitely many vanishing terms and reasoning about limits. He extended and transformed the trigonometry of Aryabhata, whose tables of sines his series rendered exact, and stood at the head of the long tradition descending through Bhaskara II.

Almost none of Madhava's own writings survive. His results are known because his pupils and their pupils preserved and credited them, above all in the works of Nilakantha Somayaji and in the great Malayalam treatise Yuktibhasha, which set out proofs of the series.

The Kerala school flourished for two centuries after him, but its findings stayed unknown to the wider world until the nineteenth century. Madhava is now recognized as one of the boldest mathematical pioneers of the late Middle Ages—a man who glimpsed the calculus from the far side of the world.
