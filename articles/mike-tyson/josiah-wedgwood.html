Josiah Wedgwood

English potter and industrialist (1730–1795)

Josiah Wedgwood was born in 1730 in Staffordshire, the heart of England's pottery district, into a family of potters. A childhood bout of smallpox weakened his knee and eventually cost him a leg, turning him from the throwing wheel toward design, experiment, and business, at which he proved a genius.

Wedgwood transformed pottery from a craft into an industry. He perfected a fine cream-colored earthenware that won the patronage of Queen Charlotte and the name Queen's ware, and developed the matte blue-and-white jasperware and black basalt that made his name a byword for taste. He applied the division of labor and rigorous testing to his factory at Etruria, and pioneered modern marketing with showrooms, illustrated catalogues, and money-back guarantees.

A man of the Enlightenment, he was a founding member of the Lunar Society alongside Joseph Priestley and his close friend Erasmus Darwin, promoted the canals that carried his wares, and threw himself into the campaign against slavery, producing the celebrated medallion of a kneeling enslaved man asking, “Am I not a man and a brother?”

His legacy ran beyond ceramics. His daughter Susannah became the mother of Charles Darwin, who later married a Wedgwood granddaughter, and the family's wealth gave the naturalist the freedom to pursue his science. Wedgwood died in 1795, his firm and his fortune secure.
