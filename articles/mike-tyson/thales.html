Thales

Greek philosopher of Miletus (c. 624–546 BC)

Thales of Miletus stands at the very beginning of Western philosophy and science. He was the first thinker known to have asked what the world is ultimately made of and to have answered without recourse to the gods, proposing that everything arises from a single natural substance—water—and that the flat earth floats upon it. In seeking natural causes for natural things, he opened a path that all later inquiry would follow, and Aristotle looked back to him as the founder of the whole enterprise.

He was honored too as one of the Seven Sages of Greece, famed for practical wisdom as well as speculation. Tradition credited him with predicting a solar eclipse that fell, dramatically, during a battle between the Lydians and the Medes, so startling the armies that they made peace. He measured the height of the Egyptian pyramids from the length of their shadows, reasoned about the geometry of triangles, and reckoned the distance of ships from shore.

Stories clustered about him as about all sages. One told how he tumbled into a well while gazing at the stars, to the laughter of a servant girl; another, how he silenced those who mocked philosophy as useless by foreseeing a fine olive harvest, cornering the presses, and making a fortune.

From his circle at Miletus grew the first school of philosophers. His pupil and successor, who pushed the search for first principles beyond water to a boundless source and drew one of the earliest maps of the world, was Anaximander.
