Horace

Roman poet of the Augustan age (65–8 BC)

Quintus Horatius Flaccus, born in 65 BC, was the supreme lyric poet of Rome. The son of a freed slave who spent everything on his education, Horace studied in Rome and Athens and fought on the losing republican side at Philippi before returning, pardoned and penniless, to make his living by the pen. His genius soon drew the notice of the poet Virgil, who introduced him to the great patron Maecenas; a gift of a Sabine farm gave Horace the leisure and security to write, and a place in the brilliant circle gathered around the emperor Augustus.

His Odes are his crowning achievement, carrying the Aeolic lyric of Sappho and Alcaeus and the grandeur of Pindar into the Latin tongue with matchless polish. Across his Satires, Epodes, and Epistles he perfected an urbane, humane voice that prized friendship, moderation, the countryside, and the fleeting pleasures of life.

Few poets have given the world so many phrases that still circulate: "seize the day," "the golden mean," "dare to be wise," and his proud boast that he had built a monument more lasting than bronze. His Art of Poetry became a handbook of literary taste for centuries.

Trusted enough that Augustus asked him to serve as his private secretary—an honour he gently declined—Horace celebrated the new Roman peace in verse and was chosen to compose the hymn for its great festival. He died in 8 BC. The unapproachable model of the soaring lyric, against whom he measured himself, was Pindar.
