Emperor Taizong of Tang

Second emperor of the Tang dynasty, model of good rule (598–649; r. 626–649)

Li Shimin, who reigned as Emperor Taizong, is remembered as one of the greatest of all Chinese rulers and the true architect of the Tang dynasty's glory. As a young general he was the chief military genius behind his father's rise, winning the campaigns that reunified China out of the chaos left by the fallen Sui.

He took the throne by violence. In the Xuanwu Gate Incident of 626, locked in a deadly rivalry with his elder brother the crown prince, he struck first, killing his brothers at the palace gate and compelling his father to abdicate in his favour. Yet the reign that followed, the Zhenguan era, became the very model of good government to which every later dynasty looked back.

Taizong governed with a frugality and openness rare among emperors. He surrounded himself with able ministers and, famously, welcomed their blunt criticism—above all from the fearless Wei Zheng, whose remonstrances he is said to have treasured as a mirror. He lightened taxes, refined the law code, and chose officials for talent. Abroad he shattered the Eastern Turks and extended Tang power deep into Central Asia, so that the steppe peoples hailed him as their “Heavenly Khan.”

A patron of learning and religion, he received with great honour the traveller who returned in 645 from a sixteen-year journey to India bearing hundreds of Buddhist texts, and supported the vast work of translating them. That pilgrim monk was Xuanzang.
