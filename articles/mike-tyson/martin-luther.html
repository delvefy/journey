Martin Luther

German reformer, founder of Protestantism (1483–1546)

Martin Luther, born in 1483 in Eisleben in Saxony, was the German monk and theologian whose protest against the medieval Church ignited the Protestant Reformation and split Western Christianity. Entering an Augustinian monastery and becoming a professor of theology at the new University of Wittenberg, he was long tormented by anxiety over his own salvation until he found release in the conviction that sinners are justified by faith alone, through God's grace—an insight he drew from Saint Paul and Saint Augustine.

His anger boiled over at the sale of indulgences, payments said to reduce punishment for sin. In 1517 he published his Ninety-Five Theses attacking the practice, by tradition nailing them to the door of the Wittenberg church. Spread rapidly by the new printing press, his ideas swept across Germany. Summoned to recant, Luther refused, and in 1521 Pope Leo X excommunicated him.

Brought before Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms, he again refused to renounce his writings, reportedly declaring that his conscience was captive to the Word of God. Sheltered by Frederick the Wise at the Wartburg Castle, he translated the New Testament into German, a work that shaped the modern German language.

Luther rejected the authority of the pope, insisted on Scripture alone as the rule of faith, and married a former nun, Katharina von Bora, modeling a new Protestant clergy. His later years were darkened by harsh polemics, including vicious attacks on Jews. He died in 1546, having permanently transformed the religious map of Europe.
