Charles Lindbergh

American aviator (1902–1974)

Charles Lindbergh became, almost overnight, the most famous man in the world. In May 1927, flying alone and without rest from New York to Paris in his small monoplane the Spirit of St. Louis, he made the first solo non-stop crossing of the Atlantic by air—thirty-three hours over the open ocean that electrified the globe and crowned the young age of flight. "Lucky Lindy" returned to ticker-tape adulation and devoted himself to promoting aviation across the world.

Fame brought tragedy. In 1932 his infant son was kidnapped and murdered in a case called the crime of the century, and the relentless press drove the grieving family to Europe.

There his reputation darkened. Invited to inspect Nazi Germany's air force, he came away convinced of its might and accepted a German medal, and on his return he became the leading voice of the America First movement against entry into the Second World War. His speeches drew charges of antisemitism and defeatism, and a bitter public clash with President Franklin D. Roosevelt led him to resign his military commission—though after Pearl Harbor he quietly served the war effort, flying combat missions and aiding bomber production.

In later years he turned to conservation and won a Pulitzer Prize for his memoir of the great flight. He had carried to its height the dream first realised by the pioneers of powered flight, the brothers from Ohio—and the surviving brother, Orville Wright.
