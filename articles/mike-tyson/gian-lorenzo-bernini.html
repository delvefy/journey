Gian Lorenzo Bernini

Italian Baroque sculptor and architect (1598–1680)

Gian Lorenzo Bernini was the presiding genius of the Roman Baroque, a sculptor and architect who reshaped the face of the Eternal City and gave marble the appearance of living flesh and motion. A prodigy trained by his sculptor father, he astonished Rome in his youth with works like Apollo and Daphne and his coiled, watchful David, carved for the great collector Cardinal Borghese—sculptures of such drama and virtuosity that they seemed to defy the hardness of stone.

Favored by a succession of popes, above all Urban VIII, Bernini became the impresario of Catholic Rome's visual splendor. He raised the soaring bronze canopy over the high altar of St. Peter's, threw his vast embracing colonnade around its piazza, sculpted the swooning Ecstasy of Saint Teresa, and set the marble giants of the Four Rivers spouting in Piazza Navona. In his hands sculpture, architecture, and light fused into a single theatrical vision.

His fame carried across Europe. Louis XIV summoned him to Paris, where he carved a famous bust of the Sun King, though his grand designs for the Louvre were set aside. Contemporaries hailed him as the Michelangelo of his age, the equal of the Renaissance master in the breadth of his genius.

Bernini died in Rome in 1680, the Baroque style triumphant largely through his work. The painter who, a generation earlier, had ignited that revolution of drama and shadow in Roman art was Caravaggio.
