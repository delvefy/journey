Dante Alighieri

Italian poet (c. 1265–1321)

Dante Alighieri, born in Florence around 1265, was the supreme poet of the Italian Middle Ages and, by writing his greatest work in the Tuscan vernacular rather than Latin, the effective father of the Italian language. As a youth he conceived an idealized love for a Florentine woman, Beatrice, who though she married another and died young became the guiding star of his imagination, celebrated first in the youthful La Vita Nuova.

Dante threw himself into the turbulent politics of Florence, but when a rival faction seized the city in 1302 he was condemned and exiled on pain of death, never to return. Embittered and wandering among the courts of Italy, he poured his learning, longing, and outrage into the Divine Comedy.

That vast poem, divided into Inferno, Purgatorio, and Paradiso, recounts an imagined journey through Hell, Purgatory, and Heaven. The poet is led through the first two realms by the Roman poet Virgil and through Paradise by Beatrice herself. Marrying the philosophy of Aristotle and the theology of the schools to vivid portraits of real men and women, it ranks among the greatest achievements of world literature.

Dante also wrote political and philosophical treatises, including De Monarchia, defending the ideal of a universal empire. He died in exile at Ravenna in 1321. His towering poem shaped not only Italian letters but the Western vision of the afterlife, and his memory was tended by admirers such as Petrarch and Boccaccio.
