Alexander the Great

King of Macedon and conqueror (356–323 BC)

Alexander III of Macedon, born in 356 BC at Pella, was the conqueror whose campaigns created one of the largest empires of the ancient world and carried Greek culture across three continents. The son of King Philip II, who had forged Macedon into the dominant power in Greece, he was tutored as a youth by the philosopher Aristotle. When Philip was assassinated in 336 BC, the twenty-year-old Alexander took the throne and swiftly crushed Greek revolts, destroying the city of Thebes as a warning.

In 334 BC he led his army into the vast Persian Empire. He won at the Granicus, shattered the forces of King Darius III at Issus, took the great fortress of Tyre after a brutal siege, and entered Egypt, where he founded Alexandria. His decisive victory came at Gaugamela in 331 BC, after which Babylon, Susa, and the Persian capital Persepolis fell to him.

Pressing ever eastward, Alexander marched through Central Asia and into India, defeating King Porus at the Hydaspes River. Only when his exhausted troops refused to go farther did he turn back. He adopted Persian dress and customs and arranged mass marriages between his men and Asian women, seeking to fuse his sprawling realm.

Said never to have lost a battle, Alexander died of fever in Babylon in 323 BC, aged just thirty-two. His generals carved the empire into rival kingdoms, but the Hellenistic age he opened endured for centuries, and his legend inspired conquerors from Julius Caesar to Napoleon.
