Dionysius I of Syracuse

Tyrant of Syracuse (c. 432–367 BC)

Dionysius I rose from a humble government clerk to become the most powerful Greek ruler of his time. Seizing supreme power at Syracuse during the desperate wars with Carthage, he held it for nearly forty years and made his city the strongest and richest in the Greek world, the bulwark of Hellenism in the west.

His long struggle with Carthage for the mastery of Sicily drove him to extraordinary feats of war. He ringed Syracuse with the mightiest fortifications of the age, and his engineers, gathered from across the Greek world, are credited with inventing the catapult and building the first great war galleys—a burst of military technology unmatched in antiquity.

He hungered for glory as a poet as well as a conqueror, writing tragedies and at last winning first prize at Athens, though his suspicious cruelty became legendary: tales told of a listening chamber that carried whispers to his ear, and of the courtier Damocles, seated at a banquet beneath a sword hung by a single hair to teach him the terror that shadows a tyrant's pleasures.

Philosophers were drawn to his glittering, dangerous court—Aristippus to enjoy it, and Plato to be appalled by it, the visit ending, by legend, with the philosopher sold into slavery. Closest of all to the tyrant stood his own brother-in-law, who fell under Plato's spell and dreamed of turning Syracuse toward philosophy—Dion of Syracuse.
