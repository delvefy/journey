Augusto Pinochet

Military dictator of Chile, 1973–1990 (1915–2006)

Augusto Pinochet was the army general who overthrew Chile's elected government and ruled the country as a brutal dictator for seventeen years.

Appointed commander-in-chief by Salvador Allende only weeks before, he turned on the president and led the coup of 11 September 1973. As head of the ruling junta he unleashed a savage repression—thousands killed or “disappeared,” tens of thousands tortured—and built a secret-police apparatus that reached across borders in coordination with neighboring dictatorships. At the same time he handed the economy to free-market technocrats schooled in the doctrines of Milton Friedman, whose radical reforms drew both praise for growth and condemnation for deepening inequality.

Confident of his support, he submitted his rule to a plebiscite in 1988 and lost, ceding power to an elected government in 1990 while clinging to his post as army chief and a lifetime senate seat. His impunity cracked in 1998 when he was arrested in London on a Spanish warrant for human-rights crimes—a landmark for international justice—though his friend Margaret Thatcher rallied to his defense and he was eventually sent home on grounds of ill health. He died in 2006, never convicted.

His seizure of power had been encouraged from Washington, where President Richard Nixon's government worked to undermine Allende. The architect of that American foreign policy, who later dealt directly with the Chilean regime, was Henry Kissinger.
