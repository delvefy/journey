Malcolm X

African-American Muslim minister and Black-empowerment leader (1925–1965)

Malcolm X was the fierce and eloquent voice of Black militancy in the America of the civil-rights era, the great counterpoint to the nonviolent gospel of Martin Luther King. Born Malcolm Little, he passed through a hard youth and a prison term, where he converted to the Nation of Islam and discarded what he called his “slave name” for the letter X.

As the Nation's most magnetic spokesman, serving under its leader Elijah Muhammad, he preached Black pride, self-reliance, and self-defence “by any means necessary,” scorning the dream of integration. He drew the young heavyweight champion Cassius Clay—soon Muhammad Ali—into the movement and became his mentor and friend.

Then his world turned. Disillusioned with Elijah Muhammad, he broke from the Nation in 1964, made the pilgrimage to Mecca, and returned an orthodox Muslim with a wider, more hopeful vision of brotherhood across races. It cost him his life: in February 1965 he was gunned down in a Harlem ballroom. Through his Autobiography his ideas outlived him, igniting the Black Power movement that followed.

The pioneering Black nationalist whose “Back to Africa” movement had inspired Malcolm's own Garveyite parents and shaped his thinking was Marcus Garvey.
