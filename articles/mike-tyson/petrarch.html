Petrarch

Italian poet and father of humanism (1304–1374)

Francesco Petrarca, known in English as Petrarch, was the father of Renaissance humanism, the scholar-poet who taught Europe to love the literature of antiquity again. Born in Arezzo and raised near the papal court at Avignon, he became a passionate hunter of forgotten classical manuscripts. His discovery of a cache of Cicero's private letters opened a door onto the living mind of ancient Rome and helped ignite the rebirth of classical learning.

Petrarch revered the ancients above all—he modeled his Latin on Cicero, wrote an epic in imitation of Virgil, and wrestled with his own restless soul in a confessional dialogue with Saint Augustine. He believed the centuries since Rome's fall had been an age of darkness, and that learning could light the world anew. For this vision he was crowned poet laureate in Rome in 1341.

Yet his most beloved works were written not in Latin but in his native Italian. In the songs and sonnets of the Canzoniere, addressed to the idealized "Laura," he perfected a form of love poetry so influential that the "Petrarchan sonnet" shaped the verse of Europe for three hundred years, reaching even the poets of Elizabethan England.

Restless, introspective, and immensely learned, Petrarch stood with one foot in the medieval world and one in the dawning Renaissance. His closest friend, with whom he shared his enthusiasm for the classics and the founding of humanist letters, was the author of the Decameron, Giovanni Boccaccio.
