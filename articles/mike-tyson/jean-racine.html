Jean Racine

French tragic dramatist (1639–1699)

Jean Racine, born in 1639 and orphaned young, was the supreme poet of French classical tragedy. He was raised and schooled by the austere Jansenists of Port-Royal, whose severe Augustinian view of human passion and guilt left a permanent mark on his art, even after he broke with his teachers to seek fame in the theatre.

His first tragedies were staged by the troupe of Molière, but the ambitious young writer soon caused a breach by handing a play to a rival company. Through the 1660s and 1670s he produced the masterpieces on which his fame rests—Andromaque, Britannicus, Bérénice, Iphigénie, and above all Phèdre—tragedies of devouring passion and inexorable fate, written in alexandrine verse of unmatched purity and observing the strict classical unities. Few writers have probed so deeply the torments of love and conscience, especially in his great female roles.

For his subjects and his spirit he turned again and again to the Greek stage, adapting the tragedies of Euripides and the heroes of antiquity to the taste of his own age. After the storm surrounding Phèdre in 1677, he abruptly abandoned the public theatre, reconciled with the Jansenists, and accepted with his friend Boileau the honoured post of historiographer to Louis XIV.

Drawn back to the stage only by royal request, he wrote two late biblical tragedies, Esther and Athalie, for a girls' school under the king's patronage. Ranked with Corneille and Molière among the masters of French classicism, Racine left a model of tragic art whose roots lay in the ancient theatre of Sophocles and Euripides.
