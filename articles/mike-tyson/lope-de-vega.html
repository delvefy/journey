Lope de Vega

Spanish playwright and poet (1562–1635)

Félix Lope de Vega y Carpio created the Spanish national theatre and was, by sheer fertility, one of the most productive writers who ever lived. Born in Madrid, he claimed to have written some fifteen hundred plays; several hundred survive, alongside epics, novels, and a vast body of lyric verse. His contemporaries crowned him the Phoenix of Wits, and even his rival Miguel de Cervantes called him a “monster of nature.”

In the short verse treatise Arte nuevo de hacer comedias he defined the lively, fast-moving popular drama—the comedia nueva—that would dominate Spanish stages for a century: three acts, mingled comedy and tragedy, honour and intrigue, written to please the paying public rather than obey classical rules. Among his most enduring plays are Fuenteovejuna, in which a whole village shoulders the blame for killing a tyrant, The Knight from Olmedo, and Peribáñez.

His life was as turbulent as his output. He sailed with the Spanish Armada in 1588 and survived; he was exiled over a libel against a former lover; he conducted a string of passionate affairs even after taking holy orders as a priest in 1614. Grief and scandal shadowed his last years.

When he died in 1635 the whole of Madrid mourned. His comedia became the mould in which the next master of the Spanish Golden Age stage, Pedro Calderón de la Barca, would work.
