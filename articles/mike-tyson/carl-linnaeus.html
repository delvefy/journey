Carl Linnaeus

Swedish botanist, father of modern taxonomy (1707–1778)

Carl Linnaeus was born in southern Sweden in 1707, the son of a country pastor whose garden kindled in him a lifelong passion for plants. He studied medicine at Uppsala and in the Netherlands, where in 1735 he published the first edition of his Systema Naturae, the work that would grow into a complete ordering of the natural world.

Linnaeus gave biology its enduring language. He established the binomial system, by which every species is named with a two-part Latin name of genus and species, and arranged plants, animals, and minerals into a nested hierarchy of ranks. His sexual system of classifying plants by their reproductive parts was simple enough to be used by anyone, and it spread his method across Europe.

As professor at Uppsala he became the most influential naturalist of his time, sending out a band of devoted students—his “apostles”—to collect specimens from every continent, and naming thousands of species himself. His insistence on a tidy, artificial system drew the scorn of his great French rival, the naturalist Buffon, who favored a fuller account of living nature.

Ennobled as Carl von Linné, he died in 1778. The framework he built proved indispensable: it remains the foundation of biological classification, and it was within the Linnaean order of species that Charles Darwin would later pose the question of their origin.
