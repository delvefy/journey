Charles VII of France

King of France, "the Victorious" (1403–1461)

Charles VII, born in 1403, was a younger son of the mad king Charles VI and Isabeau of Bavaria, and became heir to France only through the deaths of his elder brothers. His inheritance seemed worthless: by the Treaty of Troyes in 1420 his own parents disinherited him in favour of the English king Henry V, who married Charles's sister and was named heir to the French crown. When both Henry V and Charles VI died in 1422, France was split between the infant English king Henry VI in the north and the uncrowned, irresolute Dauphin south of the Loire.

For years Charles drifted, his cause near collapse, until the arrival of Joan of Arc in 1429 transformed everything. Her relief of Orléans and her insistence carried him to Reims, where he was at last crowned the legitimate king of France. Yet he did little to save Joan when she was captured, and she was burned by his enemies in 1431.

Slowly Charles grew into his role. He made peace with Burgundy at Arras in 1435, reorganised his finances with the help of the great merchant Jacques Cœur, and built the first permanent royal army in Europe, equipped with the powerful artillery that battered the English from their last strongholds. By 1453 Normandy and Gascony were reconquered and the Hundred Years' War was won.

Known to history as Charles the Victorious, he left France stronger and more centralised than he had found it. His final years were embittered by the plotting of his ambitious son, the future Louis XI, who feared disinheritance in turn. Charles VII died in 1461, suspicious and weary, but the architect of France's recovery.
