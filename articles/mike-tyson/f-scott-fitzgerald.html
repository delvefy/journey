F. Scott Fitzgerald

American novelist of the Jazz Age (1896–1940)

F. Scott Fitzgerald was the lyrical chronicler of the Jazz Age, author of The Great Gatsby, a writer who embodied the glamour and the disillusion of the 1920s.

A Princeton man from Minnesota, he became famous almost overnight at twenty-four with his first novel and married the dazzling Zelda Sayre, with whom he lived out the giddy, dissipated decade he himself named the Jazz Age. He worshipped the poetry of John Keats above all, drawing the very title of Tender Is the Night from one of Keats's odes, and he labored to make his prose as musical.

His masterpiece, The Great Gatsby, sold modestly in his lifetime but came to be regarded as one of the finest of all American novels; the poet T. S. Eliot, reading it, called it the first step American fiction had taken since Henry James. In the Paris of the 1920s he moved among the expatriate writers, forming a close and competitive friendship with Ernest Hemingway.

But Zelda's breakdowns, his own drinking, and chronic money troubles wore him down; he drifted into Hollywood screenwriting and died of a heart attack in 1940 at forty-four, convinced he was a failure, just as his reputation was about to soar. The formidable hostess and writer at the center of that expatriate Paris circle, who took his measure early and predicted his lasting fame, was Gertrude Stein.
