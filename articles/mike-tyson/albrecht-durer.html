Albrecht Dürer

German painter and printmaker (1471–1528)

Albrecht Dürer was the greatest artist of the Northern Renaissance, the master who brought the intellect and ambition of the Italian masters to the woodcut and the engraving. Born in Nuremberg, the son of a goldsmith, he learned precision and patience at the workbench before turning to painting and, above all, printmaking. Two journeys across the Alps to Venice opened his eyes to Italian art and to the theory of proportion and perspective, which he studied like a scientist and later set down in learned treatises.

It was in prints that Dürer achieved unrivaled fame. His Apocalypse woodcuts thundered with visionary power, and his three "master engravings"—Knight, Death and the Devil, Saint Jerome in His Study, and the brooding, enigmatic Melencolia I—remain summits of the art. His pen and brush could also be tender and exact, as in the famous Young Hare, the Great Piece of Turf, and the praying hands known the world over.

Dürer was the first artist to make himself a subject worthy of repeated study, painting proud self-portraits that asserted the dignity of the artist. Drawn to the religious ferment of his day, he admired Martin Luther and welcomed the Reformation, even as he exchanged works and respect with the Italian master Raphael.

Honored across Europe, he served as court artist to the Holy Roman Emperor and died in his native Nuremberg in 1528. The sovereign who became his great patron, employing his art to glorify the imperial house, was Maximilian I.
