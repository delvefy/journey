Mahmud of Ghazni

Sultan and conqueror, founder of Ghaznavid power (971–1030)

Mahmud of Ghazni was the most powerful ruler of his age in the eastern Islamic world and the maker of the Ghaznavid Empire. The son of Sabuktigin, a Turkic slave-commander who had carved out a principality in what is now Afghanistan, Mahmud seized power in 998 and made the city of Ghazna the seat of a vast dominion.

He was among the first Muslim rulers to bear the title sultan, recognized by the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad, whom he served as a champion of orthodox Sunni Islam. From Ghazna he pushed his power westward over Khorasan and much of Persia, breaking the last of the Samanids and humbling the Buyids who held the caliph in their grip.

He is remembered above all for his campaigns into India. Between 1000 and 1027 he led some seventeen expeditions across the northwest, annexing the Punjab and bearing off immense plunder from the temple-cities of the plains. The most famous was the sack in 1025 of the great Hindu temple of Somnath in Gujarat, an event that made his name legendary—celebrated in Islamic memory and reviled in Indian.

For all his ferocity in war, Mahmud made his court one of the most brilliant of the medieval world. He gathered poets and scholars around him; though tradition says he dealt shabbily with Firdawsi, whose Shahnameh was laid before him, he carried the great polymath al-Biruni from conquered Khwarezm into his service. The philosopher Avicenna, summoned to that same court, chose flight over attendance.

At his death in 1030 his empire stretched from western Persia to the Ganges plain. Though it soon fractured, Mahmud's Indian conquests opened the way for centuries of Muslim rule in the subcontinent, and he endured as the very model of the conquering sultan.
