Caravaggio

Italian Baroque painter (1571–1610)

Caravaggio was the revolutionary who transformed European painting, replacing the grace of the late Renaissance with shocking realism and the raw drama of light torn from darkness. Born near Milan, he made his name in Rome with an art unlike anything before it: saints and apostles painted from the faces of ordinary men and women, sacred scenes staged with the immediacy of real life, and forms modeled by harsh, theatrical shafts of light against deep shadow—the technique called tenebrism.

His masterpieces—The Calling of Saint Matthew, The Supper at Emmaus, the violent Judith Beheading Holofernes, the haunted David with the Head of Goliath—stunned and divided his contemporaries. Patrons clamored for his work even as churchmen recoiled from its earthy frankness.

His life was as turbulent as his art. Quarrelsome and violent, forever in trouble with the law, he killed a man in Rome in 1606 and fled with a price on his head, wandering through Naples, Malta, and Sicily, painting astonishing pictures on the run. In 1610, not yet forty, he died alone on a beach while seeking a papal pardon.

Yet his revolution swept across Europe. A whole movement of "Caravaggisti" carried his drama of light and shadow into every land, shaping Rubens, Velázquez, and—above all in his profound chiaroscuro—the supreme master of the Dutch Golden Age, Rembrandt.
