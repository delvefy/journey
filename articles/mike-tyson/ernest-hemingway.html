Ernest Hemingway

American novelist (1899–1961)

Ernest Hemingway was the American novelist whose lean, understated prose revolutionized modern writing and whose adventurous life made him a global celebrity.

Wounded as a teenage ambulance driver on the Italian front in the First World War, he drew on that experience and on the disillusionment of his generation in his early work. In 1920s Paris he joined the expatriate circle of the “Lost Generation,” drinking with James Joyce and learning his craft, and there he forged his famous “iceberg” style, in which the deepest meaning lies unspoken beneath a spare surface. He freely acknowledged his debt to the source of it all, declaring that all modern American literature came from a single book by Mark Twain.

From The Sun Also Rises and A Farewell to Arms to For Whom the Bell Tolls and the late, perfect fable The Old Man and the Sea, he wrote of war, courage and loss, while cultivating a public legend of bullfights, big game and battlefields. He won the Pulitzer and, in 1954, the Nobel Prize, and his influence reached writers worldwide—Gabriel García Márquez once shouted “Maestro!” across a Paris street at the sight of him.

Plagued by depression and failing health, he took his own life in 1961. The closest and most fraught friend of his Paris years, a fellow chronicler of the Jazz Age whom he both admired and belittled, was F. Scott Fitzgerald.
