Richard II

King of England, deposed in 1399 (1367–1400)

Richard II, born in 1367, was the younger son of Edward the Black Prince and grandson of Edward III. When both his father and grandfather died within a year of each other, the ten-year-old boy succeeded to the throne in 1377, the realm governed in his name by his powerful uncles, chief among them John of Gaunt.

His reign opened dramatically. In 1381 the Peasants' Revolt swept into London, and the fourteen-year-old king rode out to face the rebels at Smithfield; with extraordinary nerve he confronted the crowd after their leader Wat Tyler was struck down, claiming to be their captain and dispersing them. Cultivated and autocratic, Richard surrounded himself with favourites and patronised the arts, his court adorned by the poet Geoffrey Chaucer and by masterpieces such as the Wilton Diptych.

But his exalted view of kingship bred conflict. The Lords Appellant curbed his power and executed his friends in the "Merciless Parliament" of 1388. Richard bided his time, then struck back, ruling with growing absolutism and vengeance through the 1390s. In 1398 he exiled his cousin Henry Bolingbroke, John of Gaunt's son, and on Gaunt's death the next year seized the vast Lancastrian inheritance.

It was a fatal overreach. While Richard campaigned in Ireland, Bolingbroke returned, rallied the disaffected nobility, and met little resistance. Richard surrendered, was forced to abdicate, and Bolingbroke took the crown as Henry IV. Imprisoned in Pontefract Castle, the deposed king died early in 1400, almost certainly murdered. His fall—the deposition and killing of an anointed king—haunted English politics for a century and supplied Shakespeare with one of his most poetic tragedies.
