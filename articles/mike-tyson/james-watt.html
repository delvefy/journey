James Watt

Scottish engineer and inventor (1736–1819)

James Watt was born in Greenock, Scotland, in 1736, the son of a shipwright and merchant. Trained as a maker of mathematical instruments, he set up his workshop within the University of Glasgow, where he fell into the company of its professors, among them the chemist Joseph Black and the economist Adam Smith, whose ideas on heat and on enterprise both bore on his future work.

Asked in the 1760s to repair a model of the Newcomen steam engine, Watt saw how much heat it wasted by cooling and reheating its cylinder at every stroke. His remedy, a separate condenser, transformed the engine into a far more efficient machine and became the central invention of his life. Black's theory of latent heat helped him understand why it worked.

Lacking capital, Watt joined the Birmingham manufacturer Matthew Boulton, and the firm of Boulton & Watt built the engines that powered mills, mines, and factories across the Industrial Revolution. He went on to devise rotary motion, the double-acting engine, a centrifugal governor, and the measure of horsepower. In Birmingham he belonged to the Lunar Society, the brilliant circle of thinkers and makers that also drew in the potter Josiah Wedgwood.

Watt guarded his patents jealously and grew wealthy before retiring. He died in 1819, and the unit of power in which the world now measures engines and electricity, the watt, carries his name.
