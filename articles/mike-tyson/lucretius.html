Lucretius

Roman poet and philosopher (c. 99–c. 55 BC)

Of the life of Titus Lucretius Carus almost nothing certain survives, yet his single poem is one of the boldest works of the ancient mind. In six books of majestic Latin verse, On the Nature of Things, he set out to explain the whole universe without recourse to the gods, and to free his readers from the two great terrors he believed poisoned human life: the fear of death and the fear of the divine.

Everything, he argued, is built from tiny indestructible atoms moving through an infinite void, swerving and combining to form worlds, living things, and minds. The soul too is made of matter and perishes with the body, so death is simply the end of sensation and nothing to be dreaded. The gods, if they exist, dwell apart and care nothing for mortals. From these hard doctrines he drew a serene conclusion: that one should live calmly, without superstition or greed.

The poem ranges over the senses, the weather, the origins of society, and ends starkly with the plague of Athens. A late and doubtful tradition claimed that Lucretius was maddened by a love philtre and died by his own hand, and that Cicero set his manuscript in order; how much is true cannot be known.

For centuries the work nearly vanished, until a Renaissance book-hunter dragged it back into the light to astonish a new age. The Greek philosopher whose system Lucretius devoted his life to expounding, and whom he hailed as the liberator of mankind, was Epicurus.
