Joseph Priestley

English chemist and theologian (1733–1804)

Joseph Priestley was born in Yorkshire in 1733 and raised among religious Dissenters, becoming a Nonconformist minister and one of the founders of English Unitarianism. A restless and prolific writer, he produced works on theology, philosophy, history, and education throughout his life, holding to a rational and materialist faith that scandalized the orthodox.

His scientific fame rests on his experiments with gases. In 1774 he isolated the gas later named oxygen, calling it dephlogisticated air, and he identified many others, including ammonia and nitrous oxide, and devised a method of impregnating water with fixed air to make the first artificial soda water. Encouraged by his friend Benjamin Franklin, he also wrote an influential history of electricity. Yet he clung to the old phlogiston theory even after Antoine Lavoisier's new chemistry had explained his own discoveries.

Priestley was a leading light of the Lunar Society of Birmingham, the circle of industrialists and natural philosophers that included James Watt, and an outspoken supporter of the American and French revolutions. His radical sympathies made him deeply unpopular.

In 1791 a loyalist mob in the Priestley Riots burned his house, library, and laboratory in Birmingham. Driven at last from England, he emigrated to the United States in 1794 and settled in rural Pennsylvania, where he was befriended by Thomas Jefferson and continued to write until his death in 1804.
