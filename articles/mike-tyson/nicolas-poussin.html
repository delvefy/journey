Nicolas Poussin

French classical painter (1594–1665)

Nicolas Poussin was the founding master of French classicism, an intensely intellectual painter whose ordered, antique-inspired compositions became the touchstone of academic art for two centuries.

Born in Normandy, he struggled in Paris before reaching Rome in 1624, and there he stayed for nearly all his life. He immersed himself in the relics of antiquity and in the art of Raphael, whose harmony he took as his ideal, while his early mythological Bacchanals glowed with a warmth learned from Titian. Out of this study he forged a deliberate, almost philosophical art of “history painting”—clear, balanced, morally serious—in works such as Et in Arcadia ego and the great series of the Seven Sacraments.

His fame reached the French court, and in 1640 Cardinal Richelieu summoned him to Paris as First Painter to the King; but the intrigues of the court, where the young Charles Le Brun resented him, drove him back to Rome within two years. There he worked among contemporaries such as the great Baroque sculptor Gian Lorenzo Bernini, who admired him as a master storyteller of the brush.

The classical principles Poussin established outlived him completely. The French Academy enshrined him; the Neoclassical revolutions of Jacques-Louis David and Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres looked back to his example; and centuries later Paul Cézanne declared that he wished to “redo Poussin from nature.” He died in Rome in 1665, the painter's painter of the European tradition. The Bolognese reformer whose revival of classical order had cleared the path before him was Annibale Carracci.
