Cimabue

Florentine painter, teacher of Giotto (c. 1240–1302)

Cimabue, born Cenni di Pepo around 1240 in Florence, was the most celebrated painter of his generation and is traditionally regarded as the last great master of the Italo-Byzantine manner—and the teacher who pointed the way toward something new. Working in the rigid, golden style inherited from Byzantium, he nonetheless began to soften its formulas, lending his solemn Madonnas and saints a new gravity, scale and feeling.

His major surviving works convey this monumental power: the great Santa Trinita Maestà, a towering enthroned Virgin now in the Uffizi; the painted Crucifix for Santa Croce, tragically ruined in the Florence flood of 1966; and the mosaics and frescoes he made at Pisa and at Assisi. In them the old hieratic forms strain toward the naturalism that his successors would achieve.

Cimabue's lasting fame, however, was bound up with the pupil who surpassed him. By tradition it was he who discovered the boy Giotto and trained him, only to be eclipsed by him. Dante captured the moment in a famous passage of the Divine Comedy: Cimabue had thought to hold the field in painting, and now the cry is Giotto's, his own fame obscured. The verse made Cimabue forever the symbol of the master overtaken by his student.

He is said to have died around 1302 while at work on the mosaics of Pisa Cathedral. Vasari opened his Lives of the artists with Cimabue, casting him as the first faint light of the rebirth of painting that Giotto would bring to its dawn.
