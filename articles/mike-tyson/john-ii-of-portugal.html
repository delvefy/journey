John II of Portugal

King of Portugal, "the Perfect Prince" (1455–1495)

John II of Portugal, called the Perfect Prince, was among the ablest and most ruthless monarchs of his age, a king who broke the power of his nobles at home and drove forward the discoveries that opened the wider world.

On coming to the throne he found the great lords grown overmighty, and he crushed them without mercy: the powerful Duke of Braganza was tried and beheaded, and the Duke of Viseu was stabbed to death by the king's own hand. With the nobility cowed, the crown stood supreme.

His true passion was the sea. He revived the work of exploration, sending Bartolomeu Dias to round the southern cape of Africa in 1488 and dispatching agents overland to spy out the spice trade of the Indian Ocean. When a Genoese named Christopher Columbus came to him proposing to reach the Indies by sailing west, John's advisers judged the scheme misconceived, and he turned the venture down—leaving its glory, and its riches, to Spain.

After Columbus's discovery, John acted swiftly to protect Portugal's interest. Against the dividing line drawn by Pope Alexander VI, he negotiated with Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494, shifting the boundary westward—a stroke that would later give Portugal its claim to Brazil.

He died in 1495 without a surviving legitimate son, and the crown passed to his cousin, Manuel I, under whom the harvest of his labours would be reaped. The throne he had held he had inherited from his father, Afonso V.
