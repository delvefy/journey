Pindar

Theban lyric poet (c. 518–438 BC)

Pindar of Thebes was reckoned by the ancients the greatest of the lyric poets of Greece. He is remembered above all for his victory odes, the grand choral songs commissioned to honour the champions of the great athletic festivals at Olympia, Delphi, Nemea, and the Isthmus. Yet his poems are far more than congratulation: from the victor's moment of glory they rise into heroic myth, solemn reflection on the gods, and unforgettable meditations on the brevity of human life—"creatures of a day," he wrote, for man is but "the dream of a shadow."

His dense, soaring, allusive manner—ever after called Pindaric—made him the model of the lofty ode. He sang for patrons across the Greek world, among them the tyrant Hieron of Syracuse, at whose brilliant Sicilian court he met the tragedian Aeschylus, and he drew freely on the inherited treasury of myth first sung by Homer.

Pindar was a Theban, and his city's sympathy with Persia during the great wars placed him in a delicate position among the triumphant Greeks. But his fame outlived all such quarrels.

The measure of that fame is a famous act of homage long after his death: when Alexander the Great razed rebellious Thebes to the ground, he ordered that the house of Pindar and his descendants alone be spared. Among the rival singers of his own day, the most renowned was Simonides of Ceos.
