Leonhard Euler

Swiss mathematician and physicist (1707–1783)

Leonhard Euler was born in Basel in 1707 and studied under Johann Bernoulli, the leading mathematician of the age, becoming a close friend of his son Daniel. In 1727 he joined the new Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg, founded under the Russian crown, and there began an output of work unmatched in the history of mathematics.

Euler reshaped nearly every branch of the subject. He systematized the calculus of Leibniz into modern analysis, founded the study of graphs with his solution of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg, advanced number theory by proving conjectures of Fermat, and made deep contributions to mechanics, optics, and astronomy built upon the foundation laid by Isaac Newton. Much of the notation now standard—the symbols for the function, the base of natural logarithms, the imaginary unit, and the summation sign—came from his pen, as did the famous identity linking them.

In 1741 Frederick the Great drew him to the Berlin Academy, where he spent twenty-five productive but uneasy years, the soldier-king finding the pious Euler too plain for his French-styled court. In 1766 he returned to St. Petersburg at the invitation of Catherine the Great.

Though he lost the sight of one eye in middle age and became almost totally blind in his last years, his prodigious memory and powers of calculation let him continue dictating papers undiminished. He died in St. Petersburg in 1783, leaving a body of work so vast it took decades to publish, and an influence that ran directly into the mathematics of Carl Friedrich Gauss and beyond.
