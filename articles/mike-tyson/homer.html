Homer

Greek epic poet (c. 8th century BC)

Homer is the name traditionally given to the author of the Iliad and the Odyssey, the two epics that stand at the fountainhead of Western literature. They reached their final form around the eighth century BC, but almost nothing certain is known of the poet himself. Ancient tradition pictured him as a blind wandering bard from Ionia, and several cities competed for the honor of his birth; later scholars have so doubted his very existence that the puzzle is simply called the "Homeric Question," debating whether one genius or a long line of oral singers shaped the poems.

Whatever the truth, the achievement is overwhelming. The Iliad tells of a few weeks in the tenth year of the Trojan War, centering on the terrible wrath of Achilles and its toll in human life. The Odyssey follows the cunning Odysseus through ten years of wandering as he struggles home from that war. Composed in rolling hexameter verse and rich with formulaic phrasing born of oral performance, they explore glory and mortality, anger and mercy, fate and the longing for home.

For the Greeks the poems were almost scripture, recited at festivals and learned in school; Homer was, in Plato's phrase, the educator of Hellas. Alexander the Great is said to have slept with a copy of the Iliad beneath his pillow, while Plato and Aristotle wrestled with his power over the imagination.

Through Virgil and countless successors, Homer's shadow falls across all later epic. He remains, real or legendary, the first and in many eyes the greatest of poets.
