Cyrus the Younger

Persian prince and pretender (c. 424–401 BC)

Cyrus the Younger was a prince of the Achaemenid royal house, the younger son of King Darius II and the favoured child of his powerful mother, Parysatis. Bearing the name of the great founder of the empire, he was given command of the western provinces of Asia Minor while still a young man, and there he made a decision that helped settle the fate of Greece.

In the closing years of the long war between Athens and Sparta, Cyrus threw the wealth of Persia behind Sparta, lavishly funding the fleet of the admiral Lysander. That stream of Persian gold allowed Sparta to build and man the navy that finally broke Athenian sea power and ended the Peloponnesian War.

When his father died and his elder brother took the throne as Artaxerxes II, Cyrus, encouraged by his mother and convinced of his own better right, resolved to seize the crown. He gathered a great army that included ten thousand Greek mercenaries and marched into the heart of the empire. At Cunaxa, near Babylon, in 401 BC, the two brothers' armies met; the Greeks scattered the troops before them, but Cyrus, spotting his brother and charging recklessly to kill him, was struck down in the press.

His death left the Greek Ten Thousand stranded deep in Persia, and their long fighting retreat was immortalised by one of their leaders, Xenophon, who painted Cyrus as the very model of a generous and gallant prince. His story was retold, too, in the biographies of Plutarch.
