Louis Philippe I

King of the French, the “Citizen King” (1773–1850)

Louis Philippe I was the last king to reign in France, a constitutional monarch who styled himself King of the French rather than King of France. He belonged to the House of Orléans, the junior branch of the Bourbons; his father, who took the name Philippe Égalité, embraced the Revolution and voted for the execution of his cousin Louis XVI, only to be guillotined himself. The young prince fought for the Revolution at Valmy before fleeing into a long exile across Europe and America.

He returned at the Restoration as a liberal-leaning, fabulously wealthy prince, and when the July Revolution of 1830 swept away the reactionary Bourbon king, the liberals and the aged Lafayette placed Louis Philippe on the throne. His reign, the July Monarchy, favoured the prosperous middle class, and his plain, umbrella-carrying manner earned him the nickname the Citizen King.

Abroad he kept the peace and cultivated a cordial understanding with Britain, exchanging state visits with Queen Victoria. Yet beneath the bourgeois calm, discontent festered over corruption and a narrow franchise.

In February 1848 revolution erupted again; Louis Philippe abdicated and slipped away to England under a false name, dying there in 1850. His fall opened the way to the Second Republic and ultimately to the empire of Napoleon III, and it furnished Karl Marx with a case study of class struggle. The diplomat who had secured his throne's recognition and steered its early foreign policy from London was Talleyrand.
