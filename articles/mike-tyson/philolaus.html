Philolaus

Pythagorean philosopher (c. 470–385 BC)

Philolaus, a Pythagorean of the Greek west and a contemporary of Socrates, holds a singular place in the history of thought: he is said to have been the first of the secretive school of Pythagoras to commit its teachings to writing. The book he produced became a chief source through which later ages learned the doctrines of the brotherhood, and tradition holds that Plato himself obtained a copy.

His boldest idea concerned the structure of the cosmos. Breaking with the universal belief that the Earth stood unmoving at the centre of all things, Philolaus placed there instead a "central fire," the hearth of the universe, about which the Earth, Moon, Sun, planets, and stars all wheel—together with a mysterious unseen "counter-earth." It was not yet the system of the Sun at the centre, but it was the first cosmology to set the Earth itself in motion, and it opened the way that Aristarchus of Samos and, far later, Nicolaus Copernicus would follow.

In philosophy Philolaus taught that the world is built from "limiters" and "unlimited" things bound together by number and harmony, so that knowledge of anything is knowledge of the numbers within it—a deepening of the central faith of Pythagoras.

Much of what we know of his astronomy comes through the reports of Aristotle, and his harmonic philosophy left its mark on the cosmology of Plato. His teaching was carried on by the greatest of the later Pythagoreans, the mathematician and statesman Archytas.
