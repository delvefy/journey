Euclid

Greek mathematician, "father of geometry" (fl. c. 300 BC)

Euclid worked in Alexandria around 300 BC, in the reign of the first Ptolemy, and though almost nothing is known of his life, his great work has made him immortal. In the thirteen books of the Elements he gathered the geometry and number theory of the Greeks and set it out as a single, towering deductive system: from a handful of definitions, postulates, and self-evident notions he derived, proof by proof, the whole edifice of mathematics known to his day.

The Elements included the geometry of the Pythagoreans—the famous theorem of the right triangle appears as one of its propositions—the theory of proportion, an enduring treatment of whole numbers that proved the primes to be infinite, and, in its crowning book, the construction of the five regular solids beloved of the school of Plato.

No textbook in history has been more successful. For more than two thousand years the Elements was the foundation of mathematical education, and after the invention of printing it became one of the most reproduced books in the world. To a king who asked for an easier path through it, Euclid is said to have replied that there is no royal road to geometry.

More than a textbook, the work fixed the very idea of rigorous proof. Its axiomatic method became the model of reasoning for Western science and philosophy, the form in which Isaac Newton would later cast his system of the world. The supreme mathematician who built directly upon Euclid was Archimedes.
