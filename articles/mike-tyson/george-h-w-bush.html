George H. W. Bush

41st President of the United States (1924–2018)

George H. W. Bush was the 41st President of the United States, a patrician public servant who steered America through the end of the Cold War and a triumphant war in the Gulf.

The son of a senator, he was the youngest naval aviator of the Second World War, shot down over the Pacific, before building an oil fortune in Texas and amassing one of the fullest résumés in American politics—congressman, ambassador to the United Nations, envoy to China, and director of the CIA. For eight years he served as the loyal vice-president of Ronald Reagan, and in 1988 he won the presidency in his own right.

His term coincided with the collapse of the communist world. He managed the fall of the Berlin Wall, the reunification of Germany, and the peaceful dissolution of the Soviet Union with a steady, cautious hand, working closely with Mikhail Gorbachev. When Saddam Hussein seized Kuwait, Bush assembled a vast international coalition—with his staunch ally Margaret Thatcher among the first to stiffen his resolve—and drove the Iraqis out in the swift Gulf War of 1991, sending his approval ratings to record heights.

Yet a sour economy and a broken pledge on taxes undid him, and he founded a political dynasty in his son George W. Bush even as his own career ended in defeat. He died in 2018, the elder statesman of a vanished era. The younger Democrat whose victory in the 1992 election turned him out of the White House—and who, improbably, became his close friend in later years—was Bill Clinton.
