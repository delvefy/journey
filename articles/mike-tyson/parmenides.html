Parmenides

Greek philosopher of Elea (c. 515–450 BC)

Parmenides of Elea, a Greek city of southern Italy, founded the Eleatic school and posed a challenge that Greek philosophy would wrestle with for generations. He set down his teaching in a strange and solemn poem in which a goddess unfolds to him two paths of inquiry: the Way of Truth, which reason must follow, and the Way of Opinion, the deceptive beliefs of ordinary mortals.

Along the Way of Truth he argued with relentless logic that “what is, is, and what is not cannot be.” Since nothing can come from nothing, Being can never have begun nor can it perish; it must be one, whole, continuous, and changeless. All the change and multiplicity our senses report—birth, death, motion, variety—he dismissed as illusion. It was the exact opposite of Heraclitus's world of ceaseless flux.

So formidable was this argument that the philosophers who came after could not simply ignore it; the pluralists and the atomists built their systems precisely to save the appearances of change without violating Parmenides's logic, and Plato's eternal Forms owe much to his vision of unchanging Being. In one famous dialogue Plato imagined the venerable Parmenides journeying to Athens to test the young Socrates with his questions.

His doctrine was carried on and fiercely defended by a devoted pupil, who devised a battery of ingenious paradoxes—of Achilles and the tortoise, of the flying arrow—to prove that motion and plurality lead to absurdity. That pupil was Zeno of Elea.
