Jim Bridger

American mountain man and scout (1804–1881)

Jim Bridger was one of the greatest of the mountain men, a trapper, explorer, and guide whose knowledge of the American West was almost unrivalled. Raised in Missouri, he joined a celebrated fur-trapping expedition up the Missouri River as a youth of eighteen and spent the next two decades roaming the Rockies in the heyday of the beaver trade.

His wanderings carried him into country few outsiders had seen. He is often credited as the first white man to reach the Great Salt Lake, which he tasted and briefly took for an arm of the sea, and he brought back wondrous descriptions of the steaming geysers and boiling springs of the Yellowstone—tales so strange that listeners dismissed them as the inventions of a famous teller of yarns.

As the fur trade declined, Bridger turned to outfitting the great overland migration. In 1843 he established Fort Bridger in present-day Wyoming, a vital supply post on the Oregon, California, and Mormon trails. There he came into bitter conflict with the Latter-day Saints, whose followers took over the fort.

In later years, though unable to read or write, he served as a prized army scout in the campaigns of the northern plains, guiding soldiers along the Bozeman Trail. He died near Kansas City in 1881, nearly blind. His closest peer among the legendary frontiersmen had been Kit Carson; the leader whose people seized his fort was Brigham Young.
