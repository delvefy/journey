Zhuangzi

Daoist philosopher of the Warring States, master of paradox (c. 369–286 BC)

Zhuang Zhou, known as Zhuangzi, lived in the turbulent Warring States era in the small state of Song, where he held a humble post before withdrawing to a life of poverty and freedom. The book that bears his name is, with the Tao Te Ching, one of the two pillars of Daoism, and the pairing “Lao-Zhuang” came to stand for the whole philosophy. But where the older classic is terse and oracular, Zhuangzi writes in dazzling fables, dialogues, and flights of fancy unmatched in Chinese letters.

He taught that all distinctions—great and small, useful and useless, life and death—are relative and dissolve before the boundless Dao. The wise man roams free, unattached to fame, gain, or the conventions other men kill themselves to satisfy. He dramatized this in unforgettable images: the giant Peng bird soaring ninety thousand miles; the butterfly dream, in which he could not tell whether he was a man who had dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it was a man; his refusal to govern, preferring to drag his tail in the mud like a living turtle than be revered as sacred bones.

When his wife died, the story goes, a friend found him drumming on a basin and singing, reconciled to death as one more turning of nature. He delighted in puncturing the solemnity of the Confucians, often casting Confucius himself as a character in his tales.

His spirit of carefree wandering ran deep into Chinese painting, poetry, and the Chan Buddhism of later ages. Among the poets who drank most deeply from it was the great Tang master Li Bai.
