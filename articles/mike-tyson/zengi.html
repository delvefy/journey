Zengi

Atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo (c. 1085–1146)

Imad ad-Din Zengi was the hard, ruthless soldier who first showed the Muslim east how the Crusaders might be rolled back. He was the son of a Turkish governor of Aleppo who had served the Seljuk sultan Malik-Shah, but the father was executed when Zengi was a small child, and the boy grew up in the rough school of Seljuk military service at Mosul, learning war and politics together.

His talents carried him upward until the sultan named him atabeg of Mosul, and soon after of Aleppo, giving him command of the two great cities of the northern frontier. Zengi spent his career welding the quarrelsome Muslim lords of Syria and the Jazira into something like a single power, as ready to crush a rival emir as to strike at the Franks.

His name was made in 1144, when he stormed Edessa, the capital of the oldest of the Crusader states. It was the first Frankish capital to be lost, and the blow rang through Christendom, calling forth the Second Crusade in answer. To the Muslim world Zengi became a hero of holy war, though his own men feared his savagery.

He did not live to build on the victory; in 1146, while besieging a fortress, he was murdered in his tent by a servant. His lands passed to his sons, and the cause of unity and counter-crusade descended to the greatest of them, Nur ad-Din. The Seljuk sultan whose empire had given Zengi's father his office, and at whose court the Turkish power in the region had reached its height, was Malik-Shah I.
