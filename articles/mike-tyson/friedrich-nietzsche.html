Friedrich Nietzsche

German philosopher who proclaimed “God is dead” (1844–1900)

Friedrich Nietzsche was one of the most explosive and influential thinkers of the modern age. A prodigy of classical scholarship, he was made a professor of philology at Basel at only twenty-four. His first book, The Birth of Tragedy, set the ecstatic, chaotic “Dionysian” spirit of Greek art against the ordered “Apollonian,” and bore the stamp of the two men he then revered—the philosopher Schopenhauer and the composer Wagner.

Ill health drove him from his chair, and he spent the next decade as a restless invalid, pouring out the works that made him famous: Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Beyond Good and Evil, On the Genealogy of Morals. In them he proclaimed that “God is dead”—that the religious and metaphysical foundations of Western values had collapsed—and called on humanity to face the void without despair and to create new values. He contrasted a life-denying “slave morality” with the affirming “master morality,” spoke of the will to power, and imagined the Ubermensch who would overcome man as man had overcome the beast.

He turned his sharpest weapons on Socrates and Plato, whose rationalism and otherworldly Forms he blamed for the long decadence of the West, and on the Christianity he called “Platonism for the people.” In 1889, in Turin, he collapsed into a madness from which he never recovered, and he died eleven years later, his writings already beginning their vast career.

His sister later twisted his legacy to ugly ends he would have loathed. The composer he had worshipped in his youth and then renounced in bitter disillusion was Richard Wagner.
