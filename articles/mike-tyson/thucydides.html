Thucydides

Athenian historian (c. 460–400 BC)

Thucydides, born about 460 BC, wrote the History of the Peloponnesian War, the long and ruinous struggle between Athens and Sparta, and in doing so founded the rigorous, secular study of the past. An Athenian of wealth and standing, he served as a general; when he failed to save the city of Amphipolis from the Spartans in 424 BC he was sent into a twenty-year exile, which gave him the freedom and the access to both sides to gather his account.

Where the older Herodotus had ranged widely over marvels and the will of the gods, Thucydides confined himself to a single war, weighed his evidence critically, and sought the true causes of events in human nature and the calculations of power. He set down speeches to lay bare the arguments of the moment—most famously the Funeral Oration he gives to Pericles, an idealised hymn to Athenian democracy—and he described with terrible clarity the plague that ravaged the crowded city.

His coldest and most enduring lesson came in the Melian Dialogue, where the Athenians tell the doomed islanders that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must—the classic statement of political realism. He judged the deepest cause of the war to be the growth of Athenian power and the fear it stirred in Sparta.

His unfinished narrative, breaking off in 411 BC, was taken up and continued by Xenophon, and two thousand years later was rendered into English by Thomas Hobbes, who revered him as the wisest of historians. Even Homer he weighed as evidence rather than read as scripture.
