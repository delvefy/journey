Zeno of Citium

Founder of Stoicism (c. 334–262 BC)

Zeno of Citium was the founder of Stoicism, one of the most influential philosophies of the ancient world. Born around 334 BC in Citium on Cyprus, probably of Phoenician descent, he was a merchant until—by the famous story—a shipwreck stranded him in Athens and ruined his cargo. Browsing in a bookshop and struck by an account of Socrates, he asked where such men were to be found, and was pointed toward the Cynic philosopher Crates. He later quipped that he had made a prosperous voyage when he was shipwrecked.

After studying with Crates and with Megarian and Academic teachers, Zeno began to lecture in the Stoa Poikile, the "Painted Porch" of the Athenian marketplace, and from this colonnade his school took the name Stoicism. He taught that the only true good is virtue, grounded in knowledge, and that the wise person lives in agreement with nature and with the divine reason, or logos, that orders the cosmos—an idea owing much to Heraclitus.

The Stoic should master the passions, accept what fate brings, and find freedom in right judgment. Zeno organized his philosophy into logic, physics, and ethics, a structure his successors Cleanthes and Chrysippus would refine.

Honored by the Athenians for his integrity, Zeno died around 262 BC. The school he founded reached across centuries, shaping the inner lives of Romans from Seneca to the slave Epictetus to the emperor Marcus Aurelius.
