Edgar Degas

French painter and sculptor (1834–1917)

Edgar Degas was the supreme draughtsman of the Impressionist generation, an artist of restless line and unexpected angle who took the modern city as his stage. Born in Paris to a banking family, he received a rigorous classical training and venerated above all the great Neoclassical master whose advice on draughtsmanship he never forgot.

Though he helped found the independent exhibitions of the Impressionists and showed with them for years, Degas rejected the label and their open-air practice; he called himself a Realist and worked in the studio from memory and observation, obsessed with capturing movement. His enduring subjects were ballet dancers at rehearsal, racehorses, laundresses, milliners, and women bathing, rendered in oil, pastel, and monotype, and in wax sculptures such as the Little Dancer Aged Fourteen.

He was a close, sometimes abrasive friend of Édouard Manet and a co-exhibitor of Claude Monet, even as he disdained their landscapes; he mentored the American painter Mary Cassatt. Conservative and increasingly solitary, his reputation was later clouded by the bitter antisemitism he displayed during the Dreyfus Affair.

As his sight failed he turned ever more to pastel and modelled wax. He died in Paris in 1917, his draughtsmanship admired by moderns who collected him, including Pablo Picasso. The painter he had idolised since youth, and whose insistence on line shaped his whole art, was Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres.
