Paul Klee

Swiss-German painter (1879–1940)

Paul Klee was the Swiss-German painter whose small, jewel-like, endlessly inventive works made him one of the most original artists of the twentieth century.

The son of musicians and an accomplished violinist himself, he hesitated between music and art before choosing the brush, and the rhythms of music run through everything he made. He joined the circle of Der Blaue Reiter alongside his friend Wassily Kandinsky, and a journey to Tunisia in 1914 unlocked his genius for colour—“Colour and I are one,” he wrote; “I am a painter.” He described drawing as “taking a line for a walk,” and produced thousands of works of childlike wit and deep formal sophistication.

For a decade he taught at the Bauhaus, where his lectures on form and colour—collected in his Pedagogical Sketchbook—became foundational documents of modern art education. When the Nazis seized power they branded his art “degenerate” and confiscated it from German museums, and he withdrew to Switzerland, where a wasting illness darkened his final, prolific years until his death in 1940.

His poetic, sign-laden, dreamlike imagery proved a wellspring for the Surrealists who came after him. Among the painters who drank most deeply from it—turning Klee's floating symbols into a whole personal universe—was Joan Miró.
