Uthman ibn Affan

Third Rashidun caliph (c. 576–656)

Uthman ibn Affan, born around 576, was the third of the Rashidun caliphs and one of the earliest and most distinguished converts to Islam. A wealthy and generous merchant of the powerful Umayyad clan of Mecca, he was so close to the Prophet Muhammad that he married, in succession, two of Muhammad's daughters—an honour that earned him the title Dhul-Nurayn, "Possessor of the Two Lights."

He used his fortune liberally in the cause of Islam, financing the equipping of armies and the expansion of the mosque at Medina. When Umar was assassinated in 644, a council of senior companions chose Uthman as the next caliph.

His reign of twelve years brought both achievement and growing discontent. The Arab conquests rolled on into North Africa and beyond, and a navy was created. His most enduring act was to order the preparation of a single, authoritative text of the Quran, gathering the revelations into the standard version used by Muslims ever since, and suppressing variant copies—a service of incalculable importance.

Yet in his later years Uthman was accused of nepotism, appointing his Umayyad kinsmen—among them Mu'awiya—to the richest governorships. Resentment swelled into open rebellion. In 656 rebels besieged his house in Medina and murdered the aged caliph as he sat reading the Quran. His killing was a watershed: it unleashed the first Muslim civil war, as Ali struggled to govern amid demands—pressed by Aisha and by Mu'awiya—for vengeance on Uthman's murderers. Revered by Sunnis as one of the rightly guided caliphs and the preserver of the Quran, his death marked the end of an age of unity.
