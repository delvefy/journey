Joan of Arc

The Maid of Orléans (c. 1412–1431)

Joan of Arc was born about 1412 to a peasant family in the village of Domrémy, in a France torn by the Hundred Years' War and largely overrun by the English and their Burgundian allies. From around the age of thirteen she believed she heard the voices of saints—Michael, Catherine and Margaret—commanding her to drive the English out and see the disinherited Dauphin crowned king of France.

In 1429, after persuading the wary Dauphin Charles of her mission, the illiterate teenager was given armour and a place with the French army. She rode to the besieged city of Orléans and, by sheer force of conviction, inspired the French to lift the siege within days. A string of victories followed, opening the road to Reims, where in July 1429 Charles VII was anointed king with Joan at his side—the turning point of the long war.

Her triumph was brief. In 1430 she was captured by Burgundian soldiers and sold to the English, who were desperate to destroy the legitimacy her victories had given Charles. Tried for heresy and witchcraft before a church court at Rouen presided over by the pro-English bishop Pierre Cauchon, she defended herself with remarkable shrewdness but was condemned. In May 1431, not yet twenty, she was burned at the stake in the marketplace of Rouen.

A quarter-century later a new inquiry overturned the verdict and declared her innocent. The peasant girl who had changed the course of a war passed into legend as the supreme symbol of French patriotism and faith, was canonised a saint in 1920, and became the subject of works by writers from Voltaire to Mark Twain.
