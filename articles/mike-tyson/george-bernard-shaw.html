George Bernard Shaw

Irish playwright and critic (1856–1950)

George Bernard Shaw was born in Dublin in 1856 and went to London as a young man, where after lean years and failed novels he made his name as a dazzling critic of music and the theatre. Reading Karl Marx converted him to socialism, and he became a leading voice of the Fabian Society, the circle of gradual reformers that also drew in H. G. Wells.

As a critic Shaw was a fierce advocate of new ideas. He championed the social dramas of Henrik Ibsen in The Quintessence of Ibsenism and interpreted the operas of Richard Wagner in The Perfect Wagnerite, and he set out to bring the same seriousness of thought to the English stage.

His own plays married wit to argument. Man and Superman, Major Barbara, Caesar and Cleopatra, Saint Joan, and above all Pygmalion—later the musical My Fair Lady—turned debate into delight, each fitted with a long, combative preface.

Shaw won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1925 and an Academy Award for the film of Pygmalion, the only person to hold both. A vegetarian, spelling-reformer, and inexhaustible public wit, he was also drawn to some opinions—on eugenics and on strongmen—that have not worn well. He remained a fellow Irish dramatist and acquaintance of Oscar Wilde, and died, still writing, in 1950 at the age of ninety-four.
