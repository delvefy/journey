Saladin

Sultan of Egypt and Syria (1137–1193)

Saladin, a Kurd born at Tikrit, rose from the service of others to become the most celebrated of all the champions of Islam against the Crusaders. He came to prominence under the Syrian ruler Nur ad-Din, who sent him with his uncle to Egypt; there the young officer outgrew every expectation, making himself master of the country and, in 1171, sweeping away the old Shia caliphate to restore Sunni rule.

Patiently, over years of war and diplomacy, he gathered Egypt and Syria into a single power that encircled the Crusader states. The reckoning came in 1187 at the Horns of Hattin, where he annihilated the army of the Latin kingdom and captured its holy relic, the True Cross. Within months Jerusalem itself surrendered to him, and where the Crusaders had once drenched the city in blood, Saladin took it with notable mercy.

The loss of Jerusalem shook Christendom and called forth the Third Crusade. Against Richard the Lionheart he fought a long, hard campaign along the coast, neither able to destroy the other, until a truce in 1192 left him the city and granted the Christians their pilgrim road. Their contest was marked by a famous mutual courtesy, gifts and gallantries passing between the lines.

Saladin died at Damascus in 1193, so generous in life that little was left to pay for his funeral. His fame for chivalry crossed the religious divide, and Dante would place him, alone and honored, among the great souls of Limbo. The lord in whose service he had first risen, and whose unifying work against the Crusaders he completed, was Nur ad-Din.
