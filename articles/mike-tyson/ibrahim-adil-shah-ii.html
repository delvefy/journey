Ibrahim Adil Shah II

Sultan of Bijapur, the "Jagadguru" (1571–1627)

Ibrahim Adil Shah II ruled the Deccan Sultanate of Bijapur for nearly half a century and presided over its golden age of art, music, and architecture. Few Indian rulers of his era are remembered so much for culture and so little for conquest.

He came to the throne as a child, and during his minority the affairs of the kingdom were guided by his aunt, the warrior-queen Chand Bibi, until power passed fully into his hands. Once grown, he proved a ruler of striking breadth of mind. Though a Muslim sovereign, he was so devoted to learning and to the Hindu goddess of arts that his subjects called him Jagadguru, "teacher of the world."

His enduring monument is the book of songs he composed, the Kitab-i-Nauras, written in the Dakhni tongue and opening with invocations to Saraswati and Ganesha alongside the Prophet—a remarkable fusion of traditions. He gathered poets, painters, and musicians to his court, where the Bijapur school of miniature painting flowered, and he built a new city of the arts called Nauraspur.

In statecraft he steered Bijapur through the dangerous politics of the Deccan, sometimes resisting and sometimes appeasing the Mughal power of Akbar to the north, to whose family he married a daughter. Like the regent Malik Ambar of neighbouring Ahmadnagar, he understood that survival lay in balancing the great empire against its rivals.

He left as his memorial the exquisite tomb-complex known as the Ibrahim Rauza, whose harmony of dome and minaret is said to have influenced the builders of the Taj Mahal. At his death in 1627 the throne passed to his son, Mohammed Adil Shah.
