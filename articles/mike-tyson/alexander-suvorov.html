Alexander Suvorov

Russian generalissimo (1729–1800)

Alexander Suvorov is reckoned among the greatest generals in history and, by Russian tradition, was never defeated in battle across a career of some sixty engagements. Eccentric, austere, and idolised by his troops, he rose to glory in the service of Catherine the Great, winning her wars against the Ottoman Empire—crowned by the ferocious storming of Izmail in 1790—and crushing rebellion at home and abroad.

He distilled his methods into a famous soldier's catechism, The Science of Victory, preaching speed, boldness, the bayonet, and the maxim that hard training made for easy fighting. In 1794 he suppressed the Polish uprising with the bloody assault on the Warsaw suburb of Praga, a victory that earned him a field marshal's baton.

His final and most celebrated campaign came in 1799, when he led an allied army against the armies of Revolutionary France in northern Italy, sweeping them from the field. Trapped by the collapse of the coalition's plans, he extricated his men in an epic fighting march across the snowbound passes of the Swiss Alps, a feat that won him the supreme rank of generalissimo.

The whims of the erratic Tsar Paul I soon cost him his favour, and he died, out of grace, in 1800. His sovereign through his greatest years had been Catherine the Great, and his most gifted pupil, who would one day undo Napoleon, was Mikhail Kutuzov. The Polish patriot whose revolt he had broken was Tadeusz Kościuszko.
