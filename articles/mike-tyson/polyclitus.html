Polyclitus

Greek sculptor of Argos (5th century BC)

Polyclitus of Argos was, with Phidias, one of the supreme sculptors of the classical age, and the one who made of the human figure a problem in mathematics. Working chiefly in bronze, he sought a perfect harmony of the body's parts, and embodied his theory in a treatise and a matching statue, both called the Canon.

That statue, the Doryphoros or Spear-Bearer, became the textbook of ideal proportion: a calm, muscular youth standing with his weight poised on one leg, the relaxed and tensed limbs balanced in a subtle rhythm later sculptors called contrapposto. Beauty, Polyclitus held, lay in the exact commensurability of part to part, finger to hand, hand to forearm, and so up through the whole frame—a doctrine so influential that the physician Galen would invoke it centuries later as the very definition of the beautiful.

He made other celebrated figures, among them the Diadumenos, a youth binding a victor's ribbon about his head, and for the great temple of Hera near his native Argos he fashioned a colossal image of the goddess in gold and ivory, set beside the fame of Phidias's Zeus.

His bronzes are all lost, surviving only in the marble copies the Romans made, yet through them his canon still governs how we imagine the classical body. The third of the great sculptors of that generation, famed above all for a bronze athlete frozen at the instant of hurling the discus, was Myron.
