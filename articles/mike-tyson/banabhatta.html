Banabhatta

Sanskrit poet and prose master, court poet of Harsha (7th century)

Banabhatta, known simply as Bana, was the foremost Sanskrit writer of the seventh century and the court poet of the emperor Harsha. In a remarkable autobiographical preface to his work he tells of his Brahmin ancestry, his early orphanhood, and a restless, dissipated youth spent roaming with players and adventurers before he was summoned, somewhat under suspicion, to the emperor's service and won his lasting favour.

His fame rests on two works. The Harshacharita, the “Deeds of Harsha,” recounts the life and rise of his royal patron, and though it is ornate and openly flattering, it stands as one of the earliest attempts at biography of a living ruler in Indian literature—a precious, if embellished, historical source. His Kadambari, a vast prose romance of lovers, curses, and rebirths, is reckoned among the supreme masterpieces of Sanskrit; he left it unfinished at his death, and his son completed it.

Bana wrote in the high ornamental style called kavya, spinning sentences of immense length and dense wordplay, packed with simile and learning. So total was his command of the language that a proverb declared all later poets fed merely on “the leavings of Bana.”

Like every cultivated writer of his tradition, he looked back with reverence to the great poets who had gone before, naming them in his verses of homage. The greatest of them, the supreme poet and dramatist of classical Sanskrit, was Kalidasa.
