Miguel de Cervantes

Spanish novelist, author of Don Quixote (1547–1616)

Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra wrote what is widely called the first modern novel and remains the towering figure of Spanish literature. Born at Alcalá de Henares, he led an adventurous and often hard life before he wrote it. As a young soldier he fought in the Holy League's fleet at the great sea battle of Lepanto in 1571, where wounds permanently maimed his left hand—he wore the injury as a badge, calling himself the one-handed man of Lepanto.

Sailing home, he was seized by Barbary corsairs and held captive in Algiers for five years, surviving several daring escape attempts until his family and the Trinitarian order ransomed him. Back in Spain he scraped a living as a commissary gathering provisions for the Armada and as a tax collector, work that landed him briefly in prison.

In 1605 he published the first part of Don Quixote, the tale of a country gentleman driven mad by chivalric romances who rides out with his earthy squire Sancho Panza; a 1615 sequel, spurred by a pirated continuation, completed it. He also wrote the Exemplary Novels and much verse and drama, competing for the stage with the prolific Lope de Vega.

His comic, humane masterpiece was prized across the centuries—by Fyodor Dostoevsky, who thought it the saddest of all books, and by Gustave Flaubert. He is forever paired with William Shakespeare, his great contemporary, both having died in April 1616, while the Spain whose battles and armadas he served was that of Philip II.
