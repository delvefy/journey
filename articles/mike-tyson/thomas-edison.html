Thomas Edison

American inventor (1847–1931)

Thomas Alva Edison was the most prolific inventor of his age and the supreme symbol of American ingenuity, holding well over a thousand patents. Largely self-taught and beginning as a roving telegraph operator, he founded at Menlo Park the world's first true industrial research laboratory—turning invention itself into a systematic, organised enterprise and earning the nickname "the Wizard of Menlo Park." Genius, he liked to say, was one per cent inspiration and ninety-nine per cent perspiration.

From his workshops came a stream of devices that remade daily life: the phonograph, which astonished the world by capturing the human voice; the first practical incandescent light bulb; and, just as importantly, the entire system of power stations and wiring needed to make electric light useful, switched on first in lower Manhattan. He also advanced the telephone and telegraph and helped bring forth the motion picture.

His commitment to direct current drew him into the bitter "War of the Currents" against the alternating-current system of Nikola Tesla and Westinghouse—a contest Edison lost, though his companies grew into one of the giants of American industry.

Among the many who fell under his influence was a young engineer from his Detroit power company whom Edison encouraged to pursue a "horseless carriage." That protégé became his closest friend and a titan in his own right—Henry Ford.
