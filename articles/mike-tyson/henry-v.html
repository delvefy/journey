Henry V

Warrior-king of England, victor of Agincourt (1386–1422)

Henry V, born in 1386, was the eldest son of Henry Bolingbroke, who in 1399 seized the English crown from his cousin Richard II to reign as Henry IV. The young prince served his apprenticeship in hard fighting against Welsh and baronial rebels, and tradition—immortalised by Shakespeare—paints him as a wild youth who reformed utterly on becoming king in 1413.

Whatever the truth of his youth, as king he was disciplined, devout and ruthless. Reviving the long-dormant English claim to the throne of France that reached back to Edward III, he invaded Normandy in 1415. Late that autumn his small, sick army was cornered by a far larger French force near the village of Agincourt. There, in a muddy field, English and Welsh longbowmen cut the flower of French chivalry to pieces in one of the most famous victories in military history.

Agincourt made Henry the dominant figure in western Europe. Over the following years he methodically conquered Normandy and forced the broken French king Charles VI to terms. By the Treaty of Troyes in 1420 Henry married Charles's daughter Catherine of Valois, was recognised as heir and regent of France, and disinherited the Dauphin, the future Charles VII.

His triumph was cut short. In 1422, campaigning still, Henry died of dysentery near Paris at only thirty-five, weeks before the French king whose crown he was to inherit. He left a nine-month-old son, Henry VI, as king of two realms—an inheritance that would unravel within a generation. Yet Henry himself, the warrior-king of Agincourt, remained the supreme English hero of the age of chivalry, a reputation Shakespeare would fix forever.
