Herman Melville

American novelist (1819–1891)

Herman Melville was born in New York in 1819 into a once-prosperous family that slid into hardship after his father's death. As a young man he went to sea, sailing on a Pacific whaler, deserting in the Marquesas, and living for a time among the islanders. These adventures supplied his first and most popular books, Typee and Omoo, which made him briefly famous as the man who had lived among cannibals.

His ambitions soon outgrew the travelogue. Encouraged by his friendship with Nathaniel Hawthorne and fired by an intense reading of William Shakespeare, he poured everything into Moby-Dick in 1851—the story of Captain Ahab's mad pursuit of the white whale, a vast symbolic and philosophical work that he dedicated to Hawthorne. Readers of the day were bewildered, and it sold poorly.

The later novels Pierre and The Confidence-Man fared worse, and Melville's reputation collapsed. He turned to poetry and, for nearly two decades, to a quiet living as a customs inspector on the New York docks.

He died in obscurity in 1891, leaving the short masterpiece Billy Budd unpublished in his desk. Only in the 1920s did a revival of interest, led by critics such as D. H. Lawrence, lift him to his place among the greatest of American writers.
