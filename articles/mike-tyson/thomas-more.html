Thomas More

English humanist, Lord Chancellor, and martyr (1478–1535)

Thomas More was the most brilliant Englishman of the early Renaissance, a lawyer, scholar, and statesman who went to the scaffold rather than betray his conscience. Trained in law and steeped in the new humanist learning, he combined a flourishing public career with a life of scholarship and devout faith. In 1516 he published Utopia, a witty and probing description of an imaginary island commonwealth governed by reason—a book that gave a new word to every European language.

More rose high in the service of Henry VIII, prized for his wit and integrity, and in 1529 became Lord Chancellor, the first layman to hold the office. A fierce opponent of the Protestant Reformation, he defended the old Church in print and in action. But when the king sought to annul his marriage, break with Rome, and have himself declared Supreme Head of the Church of England, More could not follow.

He resigned his office and withdrew into silence, refusing to swear the Oath of Supremacy that would acknowledge the king's new claims. His silence was treated as treason. Imprisoned in the Tower, tried, and condemned, he met his death in 1535 with serene courage, declaring himself "the king's good servant, but God's first."

Four centuries later the Catholic Church made him a saint. Witty, learned, and immovable, More embodied the union of the new learning with the old faith. The dearest companion of his scholarly life, with whom he traded letters and jokes and who wrote his greatest satire under More's roof, was the prince of the humanists, Erasmus.
