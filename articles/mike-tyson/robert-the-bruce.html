Robert the Bruce

King of Scots (1274–1329)

Robert the Bruce was the noble who finished what William Wallace had begun and won Scotland its freedom. Born to a family with its own claim to the throne, he spent his early years shifting warily between the English king and the patriot cause. The decisive break came in 1306, when he met his great rival John Comyn in a church at Dumfries and killed him before the altar—a sacrilege that left him no road back. Within weeks he had himself crowned King of Scots at Scone.

His reign began in disaster. Beaten in the field, excommunicated for the murder, and driven into hiding among the hills and islands, he seemed a lost cause; later legend told of a spider whose stubborn web-spinning taught the fugitive king to try again. Whatever the truth of that tale, Bruce did try again, returning to wage a patient guerrilla war, picking off English garrisons and slighting the castles he took.

The reckoning came in 1314 at Bannockburn, where Bruce's disciplined spearmen shattered the vast army of Edward II in the greatest Scottish victory of the age. Independence followed in fact, and in 1320 the Declaration of Arbroath proclaimed it in ringing words to the Pope; by 1328 England formally acknowledged Bruce as king of a free realm.

He died in 1329, asking that his heart be carried on crusade against the infidel. The English king whose proud host he humbled in the bogs below Stirling, and whose defeat sealed Scotland's freedom, was Edward II.
