Franklin D. Roosevelt

32nd President of the United States (1882–1945)

Franklin Delano Roosevelt led the United States through its two greatest twentieth-century trials, the Great Depression and the Second World War, and was elected to the presidency an unmatched four times. Born to a patrician New York family and stricken in midlife by polio that left him unable to walk unaided—a disability he largely hid from the public—he turned adversity into an iron resolve that he carried into the White House in 1933, at the depths of economic collapse.

His answer was the New Deal, a torrent of relief, public works, and reform—Social Security, banking regulation, labour rights—that permanently enlarged the role of the federal government and restored the nation's confidence. "The only thing we have to fear," he told a frightened country, "is fear itself," and through his radio "fireside chats" he spoke to ordinary Americans as no president had before.

As war engulfed the world he guided the United States from neutrality into the fight, supplying Britain and the Soviet Union and, after Pearl Harbor—"a date which will live in infamy"—leading the nation in arms against Adolf Hitler. With Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin he formed the grand alliance that planned victory, and a warning letter from Albert Einstein moved him to launch the project that built the atomic bomb.

He died in April 1945, on the eve of triumph, having reshaped his country and the world order. His path into public life had been lit by his cousin and his wife's uncle, the earlier president Theodore Roosevelt.
