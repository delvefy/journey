Demosthenes

Athenian orator and statesman (384–322 BC)

Demosthenes, born in 384 BC, is universally reckoned the greatest orator of ancient Greece. Orphaned as a boy and cheated of his inheritance by his guardians, he taught himself eloquence to sue them, and the tradition preserved by Plutarch tells how he conquered a weak voice and a stammer by declaiming with pebbles in his mouth and shouting against the roar of the sea. To shape his prose he is said to have copied out the history of Thucydides many times over.

He rose to become the leading voice of Athens at the moment of its greatest peril, as Philip II of Macedon extended his power over Greece. In a series of blazing speeches—the Philippics and Olynthiacs, which gave our language the word for a furious denunciation—he warned his countrymen and strove to rally them against the Macedonian king. His policy brought Athens and Thebes together to face Philip at Chaeronea in 338 BC, where the Greek cause was lost.

His career was bound up with his rivalry with the orator Aeschines, who favoured peace with Macedon. Their feud climaxed in the great trial known as On the Crown, where Demosthenes' defence of his own life's work crushed his opponent and stands as the supreme masterpiece of Greek oratory.

After the death of Alexander the Great, Demosthenes roused Athens to one last revolt against Macedon. It failed, and condemned by the victors he took poison in 322 BC rather than be captured. Centuries later Cicero would take him as his model, and Plutarch would set the two greatest orators of antiquity side by side.
