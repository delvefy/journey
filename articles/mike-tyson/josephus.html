Josephus

Jewish-Roman historian (c. 37–100 AD)

Josephus, born Yosef ben Matityahu, was a priest and aristocrat of Jerusalem who became the great historian of his people—and, to many of them, a notorious traitor. When the Jews rose against Rome in 66 AD he commanded the rebel forces in Galilee, and was besieged and trapped at Jotapata. As the town fell, he survived a suicide pact among the last defenders and surrendered to Vespasian, before whom he prophesied that the general would become emperor.

When the prophecy came true, Vespasian freed him; Josephus took the imperial family's name of Flavius and attached himself to his patrons, accompanying Titus to the siege of Jerusalem and pleading in vain with the defenders to give up the doomed city. He spent his last decades in Rome, writing in Greek under Flavian protection.

His books are among the most precious to survive from antiquity. The Jewish War recounts the revolt and the destruction of the Temple and the last stand at Masada; the Antiquities of the Jews retells the whole history of his people from the creation to his own day. It is in the Antiquities that there appears the famous and much-disputed passage about Jesus Christ, along with notices of John the Baptist and of James—making Josephus a key, if controversial, witness to the age that gave birth to Christianity.

Reviled as a collaborator, he nonetheless preserved a world that would otherwise be lost. The towering figure of his histories of Judaea was its great client-king, Herod the Great.
