Colin Powell

American general and Secretary of State (1937–2021)

Colin Powell was the soldier-statesman who rose from a Harlem childhood to become the most respected American public servant of his generation—and who saw that reputation marred by a single fateful speech.

The son of Jamaican immigrants, he made his career in the army, serving in Vietnam and climbing to four-star rank. He was national security adviser to Ronald Reagan, and then, as the first Black chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff under George H. W. Bush, he directed the Gulf War and gave his name to the “Powell Doctrine” of overwhelming force and clear objectives. Cautious, popular and widely trusted, he was spoken of as a possible president.

As the first Black Secretary of State, serving George W. Bush alongside the hawkish Dick Cheney, he was the administration's reluctant moderate. In February 2003 he was sent to the United Nations to make the case for war in Iraq, citing weapons that did not exist; the speech, he later said, left a permanent blot on his record. In 2008 the lifelong Republican crossed party lines to endorse Barack Obama.

He died of complications from COVID-19 in 2021, mourned across the political spectrum. The colleague who served beside him as national security adviser and then succeeded him as Secretary of State—the first Black woman to hold that office—was Condoleezza Rice.
