Johannes Brahms

German composer (1833–1897)

Johannes Brahms was the great classicist of the Romantic age, a composer who poured new feeling into the inherited forms of symphony, concerto, and chamber music. Born into poverty in Hamburg, he earned early money playing the piano in dockside taverns. In 1853 the composer Robert Schumann heard him and proclaimed him, in a famous essay, the young genius the age had been awaiting—a verdict that bound Brahms in lifelong devotion to Schumann's wife, the pianist Clara.

Settling in Vienna, he became the standard-bearer of absolute music against the programmatic ambitions of the New German School, and his reserved grandeur set him in open rivalry with Richard Wagner. The weight of the past pressed on him: it took him some twenty years to finish his First Symphony, which an admirer hailed as Beethoven's Tenth.

His mature output is a pillar of the repertoire—four symphonies, the consoling German Requiem, two piano concertos and a soaring Violin Concerto, the Variations on a Theme by Haydn, and the Hungarian Dances. A devoted student of counterpoint, he revered Johann Sebastian Bach and built the finale of his Fourth Symphony on an old Baroque ground bass.

Generous to younger talents, he championed Antonín Dvořák. He died in Vienna in 1897, soon after Clara Schumann. Among the masters of the past he loved most, and whose songful lyricism he treasured and helped to edit, was the Viennese Franz Schubert.
