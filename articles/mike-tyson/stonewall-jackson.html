Stonewall Jackson

Confederate general (1824–1863)

Thomas Jonathan Jackson, forever known as Stonewall, was the most brilliant field commander of the Confederacy after Lee himself. Orphaned young in the Virginia hill country, he made his way to West Point, served in the Mexican-American War, and settled into a quiet life as an eccentric, intensely devout professor at the Virginia Military Institute, where his stiff manner made him an object of student mockery.

War transformed him. At the first battle of Bull Run in 1861 his brigade held firm under fire, and a fellow officer cried that there stood Jackson like a stone wall—a nickname that became legend. The following spring he conducted the Shenandoah Valley Campaign, a dazzling display of speed and deception in which his hard-marching “foot cavalry” bewildered and defeated several larger Union armies in turn.

He became the indispensable right arm of Robert E. Lee, and their partnership reached its peak in the audacious flanking attack at Chancellorsville in May 1863. In the confused darkness after that triumph, Jackson was shot by his own men; his arm was amputated, and he died of pneumonia eight days later. Lee mourned that he had lost his right arm.

His early death deprived the Confederacy of its finest tactician and became, for the South, one of the great might-have-beens of the war. He served the cause of Jefferson Davis; among the Union commanders his Valley Campaign humbled was the explorer-general John C. Frémont.
