John II of France

King of France, "the Good" (1319–1364)

John II, born in 1319, was the son of Philip VI, the first king of the house of Valois, and inherited in 1350 a France already reeling from the opening disasters of the Hundred Years' War and the ravages of the Black Death. Brave but impulsive and a poor judge of men, he was ill-suited to the crisis he faced. He quarrelled bitterly with his treacherous son-in-law Charles the Bad of Navarre and struggled to control his fractious nobility.

His reign turned on a single catastrophe. In 1356 he led the chivalry of France against an English raiding army under Edward the Black Prince near Poitiers. Through poor tactics his much larger force was routed, and John himself, fighting on foot to the last with his youngest son at his side, was taken prisoner. Carried to England, the captive king was treated with the courtesy due his rank but held for an enormous ransom.

The Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 secured his release in exchange for vast territorial concessions and a fortune in gold, with French hostages left in his place. When one of those hostages, his own son, broke parole and fled, John—bound by his sense of honour—voluntarily returned to captivity in England rather than break his word. There he died in 1364 and was succeeded by his abler son, Charles V.

Remembered as "John the Good," more for his knightly honour than his statecraft, he had presided over the lowest ebb of medieval French fortunes. Yet he also founded the chivalric Order of the Star and minted the first gold franc, a coin whose very name—"free"—celebrated his release.
