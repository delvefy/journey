Ali ibn Abi Talib

Fourth caliph and first Shia Imam (c. 600–661)

Ali ibn Abi Talib, born around 600 in Mecca, was the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, the fourth of the Rashidun ("rightly guided") caliphs, and—for Shia Muslims—the first Imam and the rightful successor to the Prophet. Raised in Muhammad's household, he was, by most accounts, among the very first to embrace Islam, a boy when he accepted his cousin's message.

His devotion was total. He slept in the Prophet's bed to foil an assassination plot on the night of the Hijra, and he became one of Islam's greatest warriors, famed for his courage in the early battles of Badr and Uhud and at the conquest of Khaybar. He married Muhammad's beloved daughter Fatima, and their sons Hasan and Husayn carried forward the Prophet's bloodline.

After Muhammad's death, Ali was passed over for the caliphate three times, as Abu Bakr, Umar and Uthman ruled in turn—a succession that the Shia would never accept as just. Only in 656, after Uthman's murder, did Ali finally become caliph. But his reign was consumed by the first Muslim civil war: he faced a revolt led by the Prophet's widow Aisha at the Battle of the Camel, and a grinding struggle against the governor Mu'awiya at Siffin.

In 661 Ali was struck down by an assassin's poisoned sword while at prayer in the mosque of Kufa. Revered across Islam for his learning, justice, piety and eloquence, he became the supreme exemplar of the faith for the Shia, and the dispute over his right to succeed Muhammad opened the enduring division between Sunni and Shia.
