Confucius

Chinese philosopher and teacher, foremost sage of China (551–479 BC)

Confucius—Kong Qiu, “Master Kong”—was born in the small state of Lu in eastern China, into a family of fallen aristocratic rank. He took up minor official posts and, by tradition, rose for a time to high office in Lu, but found rulers unwilling to govern as he urged. For many years he wandered from state to state with a band of pupils, offering his counsel to princes and meeting mostly with polite indifference, before returning home in old age to teach.

His concern was how human beings should live together and how the world might be set in order. He taught the cultivation of virtue—ren, a humane benevolence toward others; li, the proper conduct and ritual that bind society; and the ideal of the junzi, the gentleman who leads by moral example rather than force. Government, he held, should rest on the ruler's own integrity; a prince who was upright would be obeyed without command. He framed an early form of the golden rule: do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire.

Confucius claimed to transmit rather than invent, looking back to the sages and the institutions of the early Zhou as his model, and tradition credits him with editing the ancient classics of poetry, history, and ritual. He never wrote a systematic book; his sayings were gathered by disciples into the Analects.

Honoured for more than two thousand years as the teacher of the Chinese nation, he shaped the ethics, family life, and statecraft of all East Asia. By tradition the young Confucius once sought out an older keeper of the Zhou archives, the sage later revered as the founder of Daoism—Laozi.
