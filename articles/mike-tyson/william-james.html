William James

American philosopher and psychologist (1842–1910)

William James was born in New York in 1842 into a wealthy and restlessly intellectual family; his younger brother was the novelist Henry James. After training in medicine at Harvard, he stayed on to teach there for the rest of his career, moving through physiology to psychology and at last to philosophy.

His vast Principles of Psychology of 1890 founded the modern study of the mind in America. He described thought as a continuous “stream of consciousness,” analyzed habit and emotion, and treated mental life in a functional spirit shaped by the evolutionary ideas of Charles Darwin—asking always what use a faculty served for a living creature.

In philosophy James became the great champion of pragmatism, the doctrine, first sketched by his friend Charles Sanders Peirce, that the meaning and truth of an idea lie in its practical effects. His radical empiricism, grounding all knowledge in immediate experience, ran close to the thought of Ernst Mach.

James also explored the borderlands of mind and faith. His Varieties of Religious Experience treated religion as lived psychological fact, and he kept an open, curious interest in the paranormal. In 1909 he welcomed Sigmund Freud on his only visit to America and judged that the future of psychology lay with such work. He died in 1910, the most influential American thinker of his age.
