Geoffrey Chaucer

English poet, father of English literature (c. 1343–1400)

Geoffrey Chaucer was the first great poet of the English language, the writer who proved that the common tongue of England could carry literature to rival anything in Latin or French. The son of a London wine merchant, he spent his life in royal and public service—as a page, a soldier, a diplomat, a customs official, and a member of Parliament—under Edward III, Richard II, and Henry IV, all the while writing poetry of extraordinary range and humanity.

His diplomatic journeys to Italy were decisive, for there he encountered the works of Dante, Petrarch, and Boccaccio, whose example lifted his ambitions. From these he learned to weave classical and continental material into English verse, producing the tragic romance Troilus and Criseyde and other works of subtle artistry.

His enduring masterpiece is The Canterbury Tales, the unfinished story-cycle in which a company of pilgrims—knight and miller, prioress and wife of Bath—ride toward Canterbury trading tales. In its comedy, sympathy, and sharp observation of every rank of society, it gave English literature its first great gallery of living characters.

By choosing to write in the London English of his day, Chaucer helped fix it as the literary standard, earning him forever the title "father of English poetry." He was the first writer laid to rest in what became the Poets' Corner of Westminster Abbey. The Italian master whose learning and verse he so admired, and who stood at the fountainhead of Renaissance humanism, was Petrarch.
