Antonio López de Santa Anna

Mexican general and president (1794–1876)

Antonio López de Santa Anna dominated Mexican politics for three turbulent decades, serving as president eleven separate times and styling himself the Napoleon of the West. Born in Veracruz, he rose as a soldier in the wars of independence and proved a charismatic, mercurial opportunist, swinging between federalist and centralist causes as advantage dictated.

His name is bound above all to the loss of Texas. In 1836 he marched north, stormed the Alamo and put its defenders to death, and ordered the executions at Goliad—only to be surprised, routed, and captured at the Battle of San Jacinto, where he signed away Texas to save his life. Two years later he lost a leg fighting the French and had the amputated limb buried with military honours.

Recalled to power again and again, he led Mexico in its disastrous war against the United States, suffering defeat after defeat until the enemy took Mexico City and stripped away half the national territory. Later he even sold a further strip of land in the Gadsden Purchase.

Overthrown for the last time by the liberal revolution that ushered in the era of Benito Juárez, he spent long years in exile and died, poor and largely forgotten, in Mexico City in 1876. His war against the United States had been fought against the expansion driven by James K. Polk, and his greatest humiliation had come at the hands of the Texan commander Sam Houston.
