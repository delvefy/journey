Hugo Chávez

President of Venezuela, 1999–2013 (1954–2013)

Hugo Chávez was the firebrand soldier-president of Venezuela who built a populist socialist movement on the country's oil wealth and made himself the loudest anti-American voice in Latin America.

Born to a poor family and trained as an army paratrooper, he first seized the national stage in 1992 with a failed military coup; jailed and then pardoned, he returned to win the presidency at the ballot box in 1998. He named his program the “Bolivarian Revolution” after the liberator Simón Bolívar, rewrote the constitution, and poured petroleum revenues into health and literacy “missions” that won him the fierce devotion of the poor and the equally fierce hatred of his opponents.

He took the aging Fidel Castro as his mentor and model, forging a close Havana–Caracas axis, and styled himself the heir of revolutionary heroes. Abroad he cultivated rivals of Washington, leaning on the friendship of Vladimir Putin's Russia, and his anti-imperial theatrics reached their peak at the United Nations, where—a day after the American president had spoken—he declared that the devil had stood at the podium and that it still smelled of sulfur. Even with Barack Obama, whom he met with a famous handshake and a pointed gift of a book on Latin America's grievances, he kept up his combative stance.

Stricken with cancer and treated in Cuba, he died in 2013, leaving a divided and faltering nation to his chosen successor. The American president he had so theatrically denounced as “the devil” was George W. Bush.
