Anaxagoras

Greek philosopher (c. 500–428 BC)

Anaxagoras came from Ionia to Athens and became the first great philosopher to make his home in the city, bringing the bold natural science of the east to the heart of Greece. He taught that in the beginning all things lay mingled together in an infinite mixture, in which a portion of everything is present in everything, until a pure and separate intelligence—Nous, or Mind—set the whole in rotation and began to sort the chaos into an ordered world. He was the first to place such a cosmic Mind at the origin of things.

His explanations of nature were startlingly physical. The sun, he declared, is not a god but a vast red-hot stone, larger than the Peloponnese; the moon is earth-like and shines by reflected light, which is why eclipses occur. Such teachings were thrilling to some and blasphemous to others.

For decades he was the friend and teacher of Pericles, lending the statesman his serene rationalism. But his enemies, unable to strike Pericles directly, attacked his circle, and Anaxagoras was charged with impiety for denying the divinity of the heavens. Pericles spirited him away, and he ended his days honored in exile at Lampsacus.

His idea of Mind fascinated the young Socrates, his natural philosophy colored the plays of Euripides, and Aristotle weighed his doctrines with care. He moved within the famous society of thinkers around Pericles, whose most brilliant member—the learned woman of Miletus who shared the statesman's life—was Aspasia.
