Ananda

Cousin and attendant of the Buddha, foremost in memory (5th century BC)

Ananda was a first cousin of Gautama Buddha and became, in the last decades of the master's life, his constant personal attendant. The traditions remember him as the most devoted of the disciples and the one gifted with an extraordinary memory: he heard and retained the Buddha's discourses so faithfully that he was called the guardian of the teaching.

It was Ananda who, according to the accounts, pleaded with the Buddha to allow women to enter the monastic life, so that the Buddha's own foster-mother became the first nun and the order of bhikkhunis was founded. He attended the Buddha through his final illness and was present at his death at Kushinagar, where he is shown grieving as the master passed into final nirvana.

By tradition Ananda had not yet attained the full awakening of an arhat while the Buddha lived, in part because his very closeness left him bound by affection. When the community gathered for its first great council to preserve the teachings, he was at first excluded, then—having reached enlightenment on the eve of the assembly—admitted to recite the discourses from memory. The suttas that open with the words “Thus have I heard” are presented as his recitation.

He is said to have lived to a great age, transmitting the word of the Buddha to the next generation. The senior monk who convened and presided over that first council, and who led the community after the Buddha's death, was Mahakasyapa.
