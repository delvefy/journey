al-Biruni

Persian polymath, astronomer and indologist (973–1048)

Abu Rayhan al-Biruni, born in Khwarezm on the Central Asian frontier of the Islamic world in 973, was among the greatest scholars of the medieval age—an astronomer, mathematician, geographer, historian, and student of religions whose curiosity reached into nearly every science known to his time. He is said to have written some one hundred and forty works.

In his youth he corresponded with the philosopher Avicenna, pressing him with sharp questions that challenged the physics of Aristotle on the nature of the heavens, the void, and motion. His own astronomy stood in the great tradition of Ptolemy, which he refined in the encyclopaedic Canon Mas'udi, and his mathematics carried forward the work of al-Khwarizmi.

After Mahmud of Ghazni conquered Khwarezm, al-Biruni was carried into the sultan's service and travelled with his campaigns into India. There he learned Sanskrit and immersed himself in Hindu learning, producing his masterpiece, the "India," a remarkably fair-minded account of the subcontinent's religion, science, and customs. In it he weighed the astronomy of Brahmagupta and other Indian sages against that of the Greeks.

His achievements in measurement were extraordinary. Using observations from a single mountain and careful trigonometry, he estimated the radius of the Earth with an accuracy unmatched for centuries. He discussed the possibility that the Earth rotated and even moved about the Sun, while withholding final judgement; he compiled tables of latitude and longitude and measured the specific gravities of metals and gems.

Writing in Arabic but Persian by culture, al-Biruni embodied the empirical spirit at its medieval height, insisting on observation, measurement, and the honest comparison of sources. He is honoured today as a founder of geodesy and of the comparative study of religion alike.
