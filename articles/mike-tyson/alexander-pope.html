Alexander Pope

English poet (1688–1744)

Alexander Pope was the foremost English poet of his age and the supreme master of the heroic couplet, whose polished, biting verse made him the most quoted poet in the language after Shakespeare.

Born to a Catholic family at a time when that faith barred a man from the universities and public office, and crippled from boyhood by a wasting disease, Pope made his way by genius and industry alone. He announced himself with An Essay on Criticism and the glittering mock-epic The Rape of the Lock, and won a rare independence through his immensely successful translations of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey—the labour that freed him to live by his pen.

His range was wide: the philosophical Essay on Man, with its famous line that “the proper study of mankind is man”; an edition of Shakespeare; and the ferocious Dunciad, in which he pilloried the dull and venal writers of the town. Wit, polish, and moral force met in everything he wrote.

He moved among the great satirists, closest of all to Jonathan Swift, with whom he shared the Scriblerus Club and a withering view of human folly. In later years his reputation would be measured by Samuel Johnson, who gave him a place of honour among the Lives of the Poets.

Above all his forerunners Pope honoured John Dryden, whom he took as the model of his art.
