Aphra Behn

English playwright, poet, and novelist (1640–1689)

Aphra Behn was among the first Englishwomen to make her living by writing, a bold and prolific author of the Restoration who broke open a profession that had been closed to her sex.

Her early life is shadowy, but in the 1660s she served as a spy for King Charles II in Antwerp during the war with the Dutch, working under the code-name Astrea. Ill-paid and left in debt by the crown, she turned to her pen for survival and made it her trade.

She became one of the most successful playwrights of the London stage, pouring out witty comedies such as The Rover, and she wrote poetry and prose with equal daring. Her short novel Oroonoko, telling of an enslaved African prince, stands as an early landmark of English fiction and of the literature of conscience against slavery.

Frank about love and desire, and unafraid of controversy, she was praised and reviled in her own day for writing as freely as any man. She was honoured at her death with burial in Westminster Abbey.

Long afterward she became a banner for later women: Virginia Woolf bade all women lay flowers on her tomb, and the writer Vita Sackville-West told her story anew in a devoted biography.
