James Clerk Maxwell

Scottish physicist (1831–1879)

James Clerk Maxwell was born in Edinburgh in 1831 and revealed his genius early, publishing a mathematical paper while still a schoolboy. He studied at Edinburgh and at Cambridge, where his powers as a theorist quickly marked him out among the physicists of his age.

His supreme achievement was to unite electricity, magnetism, and light. Taking the experimental field ideas of Michael Faraday and casting them into precise mathematics, he produced the set of equations that bear his name and showed that light itself is an electromagnetic wave, travelling at a speed his theory predicted. He foretold the existence of other such waves, later confirmed by Heinrich Hertz. It was the greatest unification in physics since Isaac Newton.

Maxwell ranged far beyond electromagnetism. He developed the kinetic theory of gases and the statistical distribution of molecular speeds, posed the thought experiment of the demon that bears his name, proved that the rings of Saturn must be swarms of tiny bodies, and made the first durable color photograph.

As the first Cavendish Professor at Cambridge he founded and equipped the Cavendish Laboratory and edited the neglected papers of Henry Cavendish. He died of cancer in 1879, only forty-eight, and his electromagnetic theory became the foundation on which Albert Einstein would later build the theory of relativity.
