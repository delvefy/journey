Oliver Cromwell

English general and Lord Protector (1599–1658)

Oliver Cromwell rose from obscure country gentleman to ruler of England, the soldier-statesman who overthrew a king and governed three kingdoms as Lord Protector. Born at Huntingdon and sitting as a member of Parliament, he was an intense Puritan who believed himself an instrument of divine providence. When civil war broke out between King Charles I and Parliament, the middle-aged Cromwell discovered an extraordinary gift for war.

He forged his cavalry, the famed "Ironsides," into a disciplined and devout fighting force, and was a chief architect of the New Model Army. His generalship proved decisive at Marston Moor and Naseby, shattering the royalist cause. Convinced the king could never be trusted, Cromwell pressed for his trial and was among those who signed the death warrant that sent Charles I to the scaffold in 1649.

With the monarchy abolished, Cromwell crushed resistance in brutal campaigns in Ireland—the storming of Drogheda still darkens his name—and in Scotland. Impatient with the squabbling politicians, he forcibly dissolved Parliament and in 1653 accepted the title of Lord Protector, ruling as a king in all but name. He declined the crown itself, defended a measure of religious toleration, and readmitted the Jews to England.

Cromwell died of fever in 1658. His son could not hold power, and within two years the monarchy was restored; the dead Protector's body was dug up and hanged in symbolic vengeance. Admired as a champion of liberty and reviled as a regicide and tyrant, he remains one of history's most divisive figures. The anointed king he defeated, judged, and executed was Charles I.
