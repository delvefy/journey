Ignatius of Loyola

Founder of the Jesuits (1491–1556)

Ignatius of Loyola, born in 1491 into a noble Basque family, was the founder of the Society of Jesus—the Jesuits—and one of the central figures of the Catholic Counter-Reformation. He began life as a worldly soldier, but in 1521, while defending the fortress of Pamplona, his leg was shattered by a cannonball. During his long, painful convalescence, with only lives of Christ and the saints to read, he underwent a profound spiritual conversion.

Renouncing his military career, Ignatius devoted himself to God. He spent months in prayer and asceticism at Manresa, where he drafted the Spiritual Exercises, a systematic programme of meditation and self-examination that became one of the most influential works of Christian spirituality. He then studied at the University of Paris, where he gathered a small group of devoted companions, among them Francis Xavier.

In 1540 Pope Paul III formally approved the new order, with Ignatius as its first Superior General. The Jesuits were unlike older orders: disciplined, mobile, and bound by a special vow of obedience to the pope. Under Ignatius's direction from Rome they became the shock troops of the Counter-Reformation, combating the spread of the Protestantism launched by Martin Luther, founding schools and colleges across Europe, and sending missionaries to the ends of the earth.

By the time Ignatius died in 1556, the order numbered around a thousand members and was transforming Catholic education and missionary work. Canonised in 1622, he left a religious movement of immense and lasting influence—one that would, centuries later, give the Church its first pope of the order, Pope Francis.
