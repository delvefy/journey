Nelson Mandela

South African revolutionary and president (1918–2013)

Nelson Rolihlahla Mandela, born in 1918 into a Thembu royal family in the village of Mvezo, became the central figure in the struggle against South African apartheid and the country's first democratically elected president. Trained as a lawyer, he opened the nation's first Black law practice and joined the African National Congress, helping found its Youth League and driving its campaign of mass defiance against racial segregation.

After the Sharpeville massacre and the banning of the ANC, Mandela abandoned strict nonviolence and co-founded its armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe. Arrested in 1962 and convicted of sabotage at the 1964 Rivonia Trial, he was sentenced to life imprisonment. He spent twenty-seven years in prison, eighteen of them in the harsh limestone quarries of Robben Island, where he became the world's most famous political prisoner and a global symbol of resistance.

Mounting domestic unrest and international sanctions pushed the apartheid state toward negotiation. Released in 1990, Mandela worked with President F.W. de Klerk to dismantle apartheid peacefully; the two shared the 1993 Nobel Peace Prize. In 1994 he won the country's first multiracial election and was inaugurated president.

As head of state he prioritized national reconciliation over revenge, establishing the Truth and Reconciliation Commission and reassuring the white minority while extending rights to the Black majority. He served a single term and stepped down in 1999, devoting his later years to humanitarian and anti-AIDS work. Revered worldwide as "Madiba," he died in 2013, remembered as an icon of forgiveness and freedom.
