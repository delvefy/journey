Bhaskara II

Indian mathematician and astronomer (1114–1185)

Bhaskara II, called Bhaskaracharya, "Bhaskara the Teacher," was the greatest mathematician and astronomer of medieval India. Born in 1114 in the Deccan, he became head of the astronomical observatory at Ujjain, long the centre of Indian mathematical science, and in 1150 completed his masterwork, the Siddhanta Shiromani, the "Crown of Treatises."

The work gathers the whole science of his day into four parts. The Lilavati, on arithmetic and measurement, and the Bijaganita, on algebra, became the standard textbooks of India for centuries; the remaining books treat the mathematics of the heavens and the sphere. Through them Bhaskara carried forward and perfected the tradition of Aryabhata and Brahmagupta.

His originality was profound. He gave a full account of the "chakravala," a cyclic method that solved difficult indeterminate equations of a kind Brahmagupta had first attacked, cracking the case that would resist European mathematicians until the eighteenth century. He grasped that division by zero yields the infinite, and handled positive and negative numbers and surds with complete assurance.

Most remarkably, in studying the instantaneous motion of the planets he reached toward ideas that would mature only in the calculus of Newton and Leibniz: an early notion of the derivative, and the insight that a quantity's rate of change vanishes at its greatest value.

Bhaskara's books were copied, memorized, and at last rendered into Persian; his union of rigorous computation and graceful exposition marked the high summit of classical Indian mathematics. The infinite-series methods of the later Kerala school, under Madhava of Sangamagrama, would build upon the foundation he left.
