Eugène Delacroix

French Romantic painter (1798–1863)

Eugène Delacroix was the leader of the French Romantic movement in painting, the great colorist who broke with the cold precision of the Neoclassical school in which he had been raised. Where his forebears prized line and restraint, Delacroix poured energy, passion, and movement onto the canvas, taking as his masters the Venetian colorists and above all the surging vitality of Rubens.

He announced himself in 1822 with The Barque of Dante and caused a sensation with The Massacre at Chios, a searing depiction of Ottoman atrocities in the Greek war of independence. His literary imagination was fired by the poetry of Lord Byron, whose verse inspired the wild, doomed splendor of The Death of Sardanapalus. When revolution returned to Paris in 1830, Delacroix answered it with Liberty Leading the People, the bare-breasted figure of Liberty striding over the barricades—an image that became the very emblem of revolution.

A journey to Morocco and Algeria in 1832 opened a new world of light and color, yielding luminous Orientalist scenes such as Women of Algiers. Throughout his life he kept a brilliant journal, decorated great public buildings, and counted among his friends the composer Chopin, whose portrait he painted.

Delacroix's liberation of color and brushwork would echo through Impressionism and beyond. Yet the strongest single source of his imagination remained the brooding, defiant poetry of the English Romantic he revered above all other writers—Lord Byron.
