Heraclius

Byzantine emperor (c. 575–641)

Heraclius, born around 575, was one of the greatest and most tragic of the Byzantine emperors, whose long reign from 610 to 641 saw the empire rise to a last brilliant triumph and then suffer a catastrophe from which it never fully recovered. He came to power by overthrowing the tyrant Phocas, inheriting a state on the brink of collapse.

The crisis was the climactic war with the Sasanian Persian Empire of Khosrow II. The Persians had overrun the eastern provinces, capturing Jerusalem in 614 and carrying off the relic of the True Cross, conquering Egypt, and even threatening Constantinople itself. For years Heraclius husbanded his resources; then, in a series of daring campaigns deep into Persian territory, he turned the tide, shattering the Persian armies and bringing Khosrow's empire to ruin by 628. He recovered the True Cross and restored it to Jerusalem in a moment of supreme triumph.

But the victory was hollow. Both great empires, Roman and Persian, had bled themselves white in the long war, and a new power was rising in Arabia. In the last decade of Heraclius's reign the armies of Islam, unleashed under the caliph Umar, swept out of the desert. They crushed the Byzantines at the Battle of Yarmouk in 636 and tore away Syria, Palestine and Egypt—the very provinces Heraclius had just reconquered.

Heraclius, old and ailing, could only watch his hard-won gains slip away. He died in 641, his empire reduced to a fraction of its former extent. By tradition he had even received a letter from the Prophet Muhammad inviting him to Islam. He left a transformed, embattled Byzantium to face the centuries ahead.
