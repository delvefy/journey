Marcellus

Roman general, “the Sword of Rome” (c. 270–208 BC)

Marcus Claudius Marcellus was the most aggressive of Rome's commanders in the dark years of the war with Hannibal, the bold Sword to set against the cautious Shield of Fabius. Five times consul, he had won undying glory in his prime by killing a Gallic chieftain in single combat and dedicating the rarest of all Roman honors, the spolia opima—the last Roman ever to earn it.

When Hannibal seemed invincible, Marcellus dared to face him, checking his advance in southern Italy and lifting Roman spirits after the catastrophe of Cannae. But his most famous campaign was in Sicily, where he laid siege to the great city of Syracuse, which had turned against Rome.

That siege became legendary because of one man within the walls. The aged mathematician Archimedes had devised cranes, catapults, and grappling engines so terrible that the Roman fleet and army were held off for two long years; the soldiers came to dread any rope or beam that appeared above the ramparts. When the city finally fell, Archimedes was struck down by a soldier despite Marcellus's express order to spare him, and the conqueror mourned his death.

Marcellus himself fell at last in an ambush set by Hannibal in 208 BC. Plutarch told his life and matched it with a daring Greek of an earlier age, the liberator of Thebes who created its famed Sacred Band—Pelopidas.
