Ernst Mach

Austrian physicist and philosopher (1838–1916)

Ernst Mach was born in Moravia in 1838 and became professor of physics at Prague and later of philosophy at Vienna. A careful experimentalist, he made pioneering studies of how bodies move faster than sound, photographing for the first time the shock wave thrown off by a speeding bullet; the ratio of a speed to the speed of sound is still called its Mach number in his honor.

His deeper influence was philosophical. Mach held that science should speak only of what can be sensed and measured, and should banish from its foundations anything unobservable. On this ground he refused to believe in atoms, setting himself against Ludwig Boltzmann, and he attacked the absolute space and time of Isaac Newton, arguing that motion and inertia have meaning only in relation to the other matter of the universe.

That critique of Newton echoed powerfully in the work of Albert Einstein, who acknowledged Mach as a forerunner of relativity, even coining the term “Mach's principle” for the idea. His insistence on rooting knowledge in immediate experience also shaped the rise of logical positivism in Vienna.

Mach's analysis of sensation drew the admiration of the American philosopher and psychologist William James, who visited him and counted him among the most brilliant men he had ever met. Mach died near Munich in 1916.
