Lorenzo de' Medici

Ruler of Florence, "the Magnificent" (1449–1492)

Lorenzo de' Medici, called the Magnificent, presided over the golden age of the Florentine Renaissance and ranks among the most celebrated patrons in the history of art. Grandson of the Medici founder Cosimo the Elder, he became the effective ruler of the Florentine Republic in 1469, governing behind its republican forms by charm, wealth, and consummate political skill, even as the family bank that funded it all began to falter.

His grip on power was tested in 1478 by the Pazzi Conspiracy, a plot that struck down his brother Giuliano at the altar of the cathedral; Lorenzo fought his way clear and avenged the attack ferociously. Abroad he became the great peacemaker of Italy, balancing its quarrelsome states so deftly that he was called the needle of the Italian compass.

But it is as a patron that Lorenzo is immortal. He supported Botticelli, Ghirlandaio, and Verrocchio, encouraged the young Leonardo da Vinci, and—most famously—took the boy Michelangelo into his own household to study among the Medici sculptures. He gathered poets and Platonist philosophers about him and wrote graceful verse of his own.

His later years darkened under the fiery sermons of the friar Savonarola, and he died in 1492, the year the New World was found; his sons would include a pope. The painter whose luminous mythologies most perfectly capture the spirit of Lorenzo's Florence was Sandro Botticelli.
