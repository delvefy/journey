Klemens von Metternich

Austrian statesman and chancellor (1773–1859)

Klemens von Metternich was the dominant statesman of post-Napoleonic Europe, so commanding that the decades after 1815 are called the Age of Metternich. Born in the Rhineland to a family in imperial service, he rose through Austrian diplomacy to become ambassador to the France of Napoleon, where he arranged the emperor's marriage to an Austrian archduchess in a bid to bind the two powers.

As Austria's foreign minister he played a careful double game, holding to the French alliance until the moment was ripe, then swinging his country into the coalition that finally crushed Napoleon. At the Congress of Vienna in 1814–15 he was the central figure, redrawing the map of Europe and building a balance of power designed to contain France and preserve the old order.

For more than thirty years he upheld that conservative system, deploying censorship, secret police, and concerted action among the great powers to stamp out the liberalism and nationalism unleashed by the Revolution. His name became a synonym for reaction.

The wave of revolutions in 1848 at last swept him from power; he fled Vienna for England before returning to a quieter retirement, dying in 1859. He had matched wits at Vienna with the French envoy Talleyrand and with the victorious Duke of Wellington, and his great partner and rival in shaping the peace had been the Russian emperor Alexander I.
