Jean-Baptiste Lamarck

French naturalist and evolutionary theorist (1744–1829)

Jean-Baptiste Lamarck was born in Picardy in 1744, the youngest of many children in a family of minor nobility. He served briefly as a soldier before turning to botany, and his Flore française of 1778, with its practical key for identifying plants, brought him to notice. The patronage of Buffon, keeper of the royal garden, secured him a place among the naturalists of Paris and a botanical post.

When the Revolution reorganized the royal garden into the Museum of Natural History, Lamarck was assigned the unfashionable chair of insects and worms. He embraced it, devoting himself to the vast and neglected world of creatures without backbones, which he was the first to call invertebrates, and producing a great classification of them. He also helped give the new science its very name, biology.

In his Zoological Philosophy of 1809 he set out the first comprehensive theory of evolution, proposing that species are not fixed but change over generations, driven by the use and disuse of organs and the inheritance of characteristics acquired in life. His mechanism was later rejected, and his fixed-species rival Georges Cuvier ridiculed it, but the idea that life had a history of transformation was momentous.

Lamarck died poor and blind in 1829. Cuvier's cold eulogy long dimmed his reputation, yet Charles Darwin would acknowledge him as a forerunner, and his name endures in the doctrine of Lamarckism.
