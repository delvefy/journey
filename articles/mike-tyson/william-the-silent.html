William the Silent

Prince of Orange, leader of the Dutch Revolt (1533–1584)

William of Nassau was born in 1533 in the German county of Dillenburg and, while still a boy, inherited the principality of Orange and great estates in the Low Countries. Educated at the Brussels court of the Emperor Charles V, he became a favourite of the old emperor and rose under his son, Philip II of Spain, to be stadtholder of Holland, Zeeland and Utrecht, one of the foremost nobles of the land.

Yet he came to detest Philip's centralising rule, his crushing taxes, and above all the brutal persecution of Protestants by the Inquisition and the Duke of Alba's "Council of Blood." From 1568 William placed himself at the head of the revolt of the Netherlands against Spain, a struggle that would last eighty years. Cautious and reserved—hence his nickname—he held the rebellion together through defeats and exile, and embraced Calvinism as its cause.

Under his leadership the northern provinces bound themselves together by the Union of Utrecht in 1579 and renounced their allegiance to Philip in 1581. The king outlawed him and set a price on his head.

In 1584 an assassin, Balthasar Gérard, shot him dead in his house at Delft. Though he did not live to see it, the Dutch Republic he had founded endured and prospered, and he is honoured as the father of his country, ancestor of the House of Orange and of the later William III of England.
