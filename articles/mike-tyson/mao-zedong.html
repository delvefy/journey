Mao Zedong

Chinese communist revolutionary and founder of the People's Republic (1893–1976)

Mao Zedong rose from a Hunan peasant family to become the founder of the People's Republic of China and one of the most consequential—and deadliest—figures of the twentieth century. A founding member of the Chinese Communist Party, he broke with orthodox Marxism by basing his revolution on the vast Chinese peasantry rather than the industrial workers, forging a doctrine of guerrilla war and protracted struggle that bears his name.

His long contest with the Nationalist leader Chiang Kai-shek defined the era. Driven from the cities, Mao led his followers on the epic Long March that sealed his command of the party; the communists and nationalists fought, paused to resist the Japanese invasion, and then fought again, until in 1949 Mao's armies triumphed and Chiang fled to Taiwan. On the first of October that year Mao proclaimed the new republic from the gate of Tiananmen.

In power he pursued utopian transformation with catastrophic results. The Great Leap Forward sought to industrialise overnight and instead produced the deadliest famine in history; the Cultural Revolution unleashed the Red Guards and his "Little Red Book" in a decade of persecution and chaos. Abroad he broke with the Soviet Union and opened a wary door to the West.

Revered as a liberator and condemned as a tyrant, he reshaped a fifth of humanity before his death in 1976. He built his creed on the foundations of Karl Marx, Lenin, and Stalin. His lifelong rival for the soul of China was Chiang Kai-shek.
