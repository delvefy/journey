Federico da Montefeltro

Duke of Urbino and condottiero (1422–1482)

Federico da Montefeltro united in one man the two faces of the Italian Renaissance, the ruthless soldier and the refined patron of learning. Born illegitimate, he made his fortune as the most admired mercenary captain of the age, selling his generalship to popes and princes and seldom losing a campaign. A tournament cost him his right eye and the bridge of his nose, which is why his portraits show only his left profile, sharp against a pale sky.

With the wealth his sword earned, he transformed the little hill-state of Urbino into one of the most cultivated courts in Europe. He raised a Ducal Palace of serene beauty, fitted with a famous little study paneled in illusionistic inlay, and gathered a library so splendid that it rivaled any in Italy. To his court he drew artists and scholars, foremost among them the painter Piero della Francesca.

His reputation for courtesy, justice, and learning was such that his household became, in memory, the very model of the perfect princely court, later enshrined in a celebrated book of manners. In 1474 his eminence was crowned when the Pope raised him from count to Duke of Urbino and named him a captain of the Church.

That elevation tied him to Rome's politics, and he led the armies of the papal alliance against Florence in the war that followed the Pazzi conspiracy, set against Lorenzo de' Medici. The pope who made him a duke and whose wars he fought, builder of the chapel that bears his name, was Pope Sixtus IV.
