Simón Bolívar

Liberator of South America (1783–1830)

Simón Bolívar, known across a continent simply as the Liberator, led the wars that freed much of South America from Spanish rule. Born to a wealthy Creole family in Caracas and orphaned young, he was shaped by a tutor steeped in the ideas of Jean-Jacques Rousseau and by long travels in Europe, where he witnessed the coronation of Napoleon and, in Rome, is said to have vowed to liberate his homeland.

Returning to a Venezuela in revolt, he emerged as the boldest of the patriot commanders. After early defeats and exile he wrote the visionary Jamaica Letter and staged an audacious march across the flooded plains and frozen Andes to surprise the Spanish at Boyacá in 1819, freeing New Granada. Victories at Carabobo and, through his lieutenants, at Pichincha and Ayacucho completed the liberation of Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru; a grateful new nation took the name Bolivia.

He founded the union of Gran Colombia and dreamed of a confederation of Spanish America, drawing on the Enlightenment of Voltaire and consciously measuring himself against the republican example of George Washington. Yet the union fractured under regional rivalries, and his turn to dictatorial powers cost him support.

Disillusioned—he lamented that those who serve a revolution plough the sea—he resigned power and died of tuberculosis near Santa Marta in 1830, only forty-seven. The decisive turning point of the southern campaign had come at his 1822 meeting in Guayaquil with the other great liberator, José de San Martín.
