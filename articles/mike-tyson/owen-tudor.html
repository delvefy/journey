Owen Tudor

Welsh founder of the Tudor dynasty (c. 1400–1461)

Owen Tudor, born around 1400, was a Welsh gentleman of ancient but reduced lineage who entered royal service in the years after Agincourt. By the late 1420s he held a place in the household of the dowager queen Catherine of Valois, the young widow of Henry V. There, against every expectation of rank, a romance grew between the Welsh servant and the queen of England, and the two married in secret.

The union was perilous and, when discovered, scandalous; the council that governed for the boy-king Henry VI disapproved of a queen-mother wedding a commoner. Owen was imprisoned more than once, and after Catherine's death in 1437 he lived under a cloud. But the marriage had produced sons—Edmund and Jasper Tudor—who, as half-brothers of King Henry VI, were raised to earldoms and brought into the heart of Lancastrian politics.

It was through Edmund's marriage to Margaret Beaufort that Owen's blood acquired a claim to the throne, for their son would be the future Henry VII. Owen himself remained a staunch Lancastrian to the end. When the Wars of the Roses turned against his house, the old man took up arms; captured after the Yorkist victory at Mortimer's Cross in 1461, he was beheaded in the marketplace at Hereford. To the last, it was said, he could scarcely believe his fate.

A minor figure in his own lifetime, Owen Tudor became the founding patriarch of the most famous of English dynasties: from his improbable marriage descended Henry VII, Henry VIII, and the whole royal house of Tudor.
