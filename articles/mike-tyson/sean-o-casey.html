Sean O'Casey

Irish dramatist (1880–1964)

Sean O'Casey was the great dramatist of the Dublin tenements, who turned the poverty and political violence he had lived through into tragicomedies of enduring power.

Born into a poor Protestant family in Dublin and largely self-taught, he laboured as a manual worker while immersing himself in nationalist and socialist causes. He served as the first secretary of the Irish Citizen Army and wrote its early history, but grew disillusioned with militant politics and turned instead to the stage, drawing on the slum world he knew intimately.

The Abbey Theatre staged his breakthrough works in quick succession: The Shadow of a Gunman, the acclaimed Juno and the Paycock, and in 1926 The Plough and the Stars, whose unsentimental view of the 1916 Rising touched off nationalist riots in the theatre—quelled only when W. B. Yeats rose to rebuke the crowd for disgracing themselves. O'Casey found a warm admirer and friend in George Bernard Shaw, the elder statesman of Irish letters, and his early plays drew on the same labour ferment that James Connolly had led.

His bond with the Abbey broke in 1928 when the theatre rejected his ambitious anti-war drama The Silver Tassie; stung, he settled permanently in England, where he wrote further plays and six vivid volumes of autobiography, remaining a defiant socialist to the end. He died in Devon in 1964. The patron who had first welcomed him to the Abbey and remained his loyal champion and friend was its co-founder, Lady Gregory.
