Philip the Good

Duke of Burgundy (1396–1467)

Philip the Good, born in 1396, became Duke of Burgundy in 1419 after his father, John the Fearless, was murdered on a bridge during a parley with the French Dauphin. Vowing revenge, Philip threw Burgundy into alliance with England, recognising the English claim to France and joining the war against the Dauphin. It was Burgundian soldiers who captured Joan of Arc in 1430 and handed her to the English to be burned.

Yet Philip was a statesman as much as an avenger. In 1435, judging that the English cause was failing, he made his peace with the French king Charles VII at the Treaty of Arras, abandoning the alliance and winning rich concessions for himself. He even sheltered the rebellious French Dauphin—the future Louis XI—at his court for years, never guessing what an ungrateful guest he harboured.

Under Philip, Burgundy reached its zenith. By inheritance, purchase and marriage he gathered the prosperous provinces of the Low Countries—Flanders, Brabant, Holland—into one of the wealthiest realms in Europe, its great cloth towns the engine of its riches. His court became the most magnificent in Christendom, a model of chivalric splendour. In 1430 he founded the Order of the Golden Fleece, and he lavished patronage on artists; the painter Jan van Eyck served him as valet and confidant.

Ruling for nearly half a century, Philip earned his epithet "the Good" and made the Burgundian state a great power in all but name. He left to his proud son Charles the Bold an inheritance of unmatched wealth—and the dangerous ambition to turn a duchy into a kingdom.
