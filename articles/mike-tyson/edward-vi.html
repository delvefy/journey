Edward VI

King of England, the boy king (1537–1553)

Edward VI was the long-awaited son of Henry VIII, the only legitimate male heir for whom his father had remade the kingdom's religion—and a king who ruled for just six years before dying as a boy. Born to Jane Seymour in 1537, he came to the throne in 1547 at the age of nine, on his father's death. A precociously intelligent and intensely earnest child, he had been raised in the new Protestant learning and embraced it with conviction.

Because the king was a minor, real power lay with the council and a succession of regents—first his uncle Edward Seymour, the Duke of Somerset, who governed as Lord Protector, and then the more ruthless John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland. Under Edward the English Reformation took on a firmly Protestant shape, as the Latin mass gave way to an English liturgy and the doctrines of the reformed faith were written into law.

Always frail, Edward fell mortally ill, probably of tuberculosis, and died in 1553, not yet sixteen. On his deathbed, fearing the undoing of his religious work by his Catholic half-sister Mary, he was persuaded to set aside the order of succession and name his Protestant cousin, Lady Jane Grey, as his heir—a desperate device that collapsed within days when Mary I claimed her throne.

The reign's lasting monument was its English prayer book, the work of the reforming Archbishop of Canterbury who guided the young king's church and would later die for his faith—Thomas Cranmer.
