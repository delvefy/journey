Epictetus

Stoic philosopher (c. 50–135 AD)

Epictetus was a Stoic philosopher who, remarkably, began life as a slave and rose to become one of antiquity's most admired teachers of wisdom. Born around 50 AD at Hierapolis in Asia Minor, he was enslaved in Rome to a powerful freedman of the emperor Nero's household. Permitted to study under the Stoic Musonius Rufus, and lamed—by tradition through harsh treatment—he won his freedom after Nero's fall.

When the emperor Domitian banished philosophers from Rome, Epictetus withdrew to Nicopolis in Greece and there founded a celebrated school. His core teaching was disarmingly simple and bracingly hard: some things are within our power—our own judgments, desires, and choices—and some are not, namely everything external, from health and wealth to the actions of others. Freedom and peace come from concerning ourselves only with what is truly ours and accepting the rest as it comes.

He held up Socrates and the Cynic Diogenes as his models of the sage who needs nothing the world can give or take away, and he lived with corresponding simplicity. Like Socrates, he wrote nothing; his words survive because his devoted pupil Arrian recorded them in the Discourses and distilled them into the famous handbook, the Enchiridion.

His influence was immense and immediate: the emperor Marcus Aurelius shaped his own Meditations around Epictetus's teaching. Epictetus died around 135 AD, leaving Stoicism its most practical and enduring voice on inner freedom.
