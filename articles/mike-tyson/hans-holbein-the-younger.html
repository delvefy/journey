Hans Holbein the Younger

German painter of the Tudor court (c. 1497–1543)

Hans Holbein the Younger was one of the supreme portraitists in the history of art, the painter who fixed forever the faces of the age of Henry VIII. Born in Augsburg into a family of painters, he made his early career in Basel, where he produced religious works, designs for printers, and his haunting woodcut series the Dance of Death. There he also met Erasmus, whose portrait he painted and whose recommendation opened the door to England.

Carrying a letter from Erasmus, Holbein entered the household of Thomas More, painting More and his family in a group portrait of unprecedented intimacy. After returning briefly to Basel he settled in England for good and rose to become court painter to Henry VIII. His image of the king—massive, bejeweled, and immovable—became the definitive likeness of a monarch, copied and remembered for centuries.

Holbein's genius lay in a cool, exact realism that captured both the surface splendor and the inner character of his sitters. His masterpiece The Ambassadors, with its mysterious distorted skull, is among the most studied paintings in the world, and his portrait of Thomas Cromwell preserves the minister at the height of his power.

His art even shaped the king's marriages: it was Holbein's flattering portrait of Anne of Cleves that helped lure Henry into a match he came to loathe. He died in London in 1543, probably of the plague. Among the queens whose features he recorded for posterity was the one who gave Henry his only surviving son—Jane Seymour.
