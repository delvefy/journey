Tulsidas

Poet-saint, author of the Ramcharitmanas (c. 1532–1623)

Tulsidas was the great poet-saint of the devotional, or bhakti, age of northern India, and the supreme singer of the god Rama. His towering work, the Ramcharitmanas—the “Sacred Lake of the Deeds of Rama”—retold the ancient epic of Valmiki not in the Sanskrit of the learned but in Awadhi, the living speech of the people of the Ganges plain. Composed in the later sixteenth century, it became at once, and has remained, the most beloved and widely recited text of Hindi-speaking India.

Tradition tells that Tulsidas was born to hardship, abandoned in infancy, and turned to the worship of Rama after a wandering and devout life. He settled at the holy city of Varanasi, where he is said to have founded a temple to Hanuman, Rama's faithful servant, and where he composed not only the Ramcharitmanas but also the Hanuman Chalisa, a forty-verse hymn still on the lips of millions, and other works of praise and supplication.

By rendering the story of Rama into the vernacular, he opened the highest devotion to the unlettered and the poor, and his epic shaped the religious imagination, festivals, and folk theatre of the whole region; the annual Ramlila pageants enact his verses to this day.

He lived in the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar, in an age rich with saints and singers of God. The most renowned of the earlier mystics of that movement, the weaver of Varanasi honoured by Hindus and Muslims alike, was Kabir.
