John Keats

English Romantic poet (1795–1821)

John Keats was among the greatest of all English poets, though he died at twenty-five believing himself a failure. Born in London, the son of a stable-keeper, he trained as an apothecary and surgeon before abandoning medicine for poetry, encouraged by the writer Leigh Hunt. His early work drew savage reviews, but Keats pressed on with a devotion to beauty and a craftsmanship that ripened with astonishing speed.

In a single miraculous year, 1819, he produced the poems on which his fame rests: the great odes—To a Nightingale, On a Grecian Urn with its famous close "Beauty is truth, truth beauty," To Autumn—along with the romances The Eve of St. Agnes and La Belle Dame sans Merci, and the unfinished, Miltonic epic Hyperion. He read Shakespeare and Spenser as scripture and gave criticism the haunting idea of "negative capability," the poet's power to dwell in uncertainty and mystery.

His brief life was shadowed by loss and by a deepening love for Fanny Brawne that his poverty and illness denied. Stricken with tuberculosis, he sailed to Italy in hope of recovery and died in Rome in 1821, asking that his grave bear only the words, "Here lies one whose name was writ in water."

The senior poet of the Romantic age whom Keats revered, wrestled with, and measured his own ambition against was William Wordsworth.
