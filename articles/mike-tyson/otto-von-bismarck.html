Otto von Bismarck

German statesman, the "Iron Chancellor" (1815–1898)

Otto von Bismarck, the "Iron Chancellor," was the master statesman who forged a united Germany and dominated European politics for nearly three decades. Born in 1815 into the Prussian landed aristocracy, this arch-conservative monarchist was appointed Minister President of Prussia by King Wilhelm I in 1862, and soon declared that the great questions of the age would be decided not by speeches and majority votes but "by blood and iron."

He made good on the threat through three deliberately engineered wars. He defeated Denmark, then in 1866 crushed Austria to exclude it from German affairs, and finally, in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, defeated and captured the Emperor Napoleon III. In the wake of that victory the German Empire was proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, with Wilhelm I as Kaiser and Bismarck as its first Chancellor.

Thereafter Bismarck became Europe's supreme practitioner of Realpolitik, weaving a web of alliances to keep the peace and isolate a vengeful France. At home he battled the Catholic Church and the socialists, yet outflanked the latter by pioneering the world's first modern welfare state, with insurance for sickness, accident, and old age.

His system depended on his own subtle hand. When the brash young Kaiser Wilhelm II resolved to rule for himself, he dismissed the aging chancellor in 1890. Bismarck retired in bitterness and died in 1898, having reshaped the map and balance of Europe.
