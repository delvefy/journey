Antonie van Leeuwenhoek

Dutch microscopist, father of microbiology (1632–1723)

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was the Delft draper who, with no university learning, opened the door to an entire invisible world and became the father of microbiology. A tradesman and minor town official, he taught himself to grind tiny glass lenses of extraordinary power, mounting them in small brass frames to make microscopes that magnified far beyond anything else of his day—by methods he guarded as a secret.

He turned these instruments on everything within reach: rainwater, the scrapings of his own teeth, blood, muscle, and insects. What he saw astonished him and, in time, the world. He was the first to observe and describe the teeming microorganisms he called "animalcules"—the protozoa and bacteria—along with spermatozoa, red blood cells, and the fine structure of living tissue.

From 1673 he reported his discoveries in a long stream of letters to the Royal Society in London. At first his claims seemed incredible, but when Robert Hooke—whose own Micrographia had launched the microscopic age—confirmed the observations, van Leeuwenhoek was vindicated and elected a Fellow, his fame spreading across Europe.

He went on making discoveries into great old age, dying at Delft in 1723. The other founder of the microscopic study of living things, the Italian who first traced the hidden anatomy of plants and animals, was Marcello Malpighi.
