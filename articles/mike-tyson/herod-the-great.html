Herod the Great

King of Judaea (c. 72–4 BC)

Herod, called the Great, ruled Judaea as a client king of Rome for more than three decades. Of Idumean rather than Jewish royal blood, he owed his throne to Roman power: when the Parthians overran the country, he fled to Rome, where Mark Antony and Octavian persuaded the Senate to proclaim him King of the Jews, and he won his kingdom back by force of arms. To bolster his legitimacy he married the Hasmonean princess Mariamne—then, consumed by jealousy and fear, had her and several of his own sons put to death, prompting the jibe of Augustus that it was safer to be Herod's pig than his son.

A consummate survivor, Herod abandoned the losing Antony after the battle of Actium and threw himself on the mercy of Augustus, who confirmed and enlarged his realm; he weathered, too, the hostility of Cleopatra, who had coveted his richest lands.

He was, above all, a builder of genius and megalomania. He doubled the size of the Temple Mount and raised a magnificent new Temple in Jerusalem, founded the great harbour city of Caesarea, and crowned the desert with the fortress-palaces of Masada and the Herodium.

Yet his name is darkened in memory by the Gospel of Matthew, which sets in his last years the slaughter of the infants of Bethlehem and the flight of the Holy Family into Egypt, in his attempt to destroy the newborn Jesus Christ. He died in 4 BC, leaving his kingdom to be divided among his sons. The Roman who had first raised him to the throne was Mark Antony.
