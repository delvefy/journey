Mu'awiya I

Founder of the Umayyad Caliphate (c. 602–680)

Mu'awiya I, born around 602, was the founder of the Umayyad Caliphate and one of the most skilful and controversial statesmen of early Islam. He was the son of Abu Sufyan, the Meccan chieftain who had led the city's long resistance to Muhammad before submitting at the conquest of Mecca; Mu'awiya himself then became a companion and secretary of the Prophet.

Under the caliphs Umar and Uthman, Mu'awiya was appointed governor of Syria, which he ruled for some twenty years, building a loyal army and a strong provincial base centred on Damascus. When his kinsman, the caliph Uthman, was murdered in 656, Mu'awiya refused to accept the new caliph, Ali, demanding vengeance for Uthman's blood. Their armies clashed in the great, inconclusive Battle of Siffin in 657, a central episode of the first Muslim civil war.

After Ali's assassination in 661, Ali's elder son Hasan abdicated his claim in return for a settlement, and Mu'awiya became the undisputed caliph, founding the Umayyad dynasty and shifting the centre of the Islamic world from Arabia to Syria. A pragmatic and tolerant ruler, he governed with shrewdness and consolidated the vast empire.

His most fateful act was to break with tradition by naming his own son, Yazid, as his heir, transforming the caliphate into a hereditary monarchy. That decision set the stage for tragedy: within months of Mu'awiya's death in 680, Yazid's forces would slaughter the Prophet's grandson Husayn at Karbala. Admired by many Sunnis as an able founder-statesman and reviled in Shia tradition, Mu'awiya remains a pivotal and divisive figure at the parting of the ways in early Islamic history.
