Edward the Confessor

Anglo-Saxon king of England (c. 1003–1066)

Edward the Confessor, born around 1003, was among the last kings of Anglo-Saxon England and one of its most venerated. The son of King Æthelred the Unready and Emma of Normandy, he spent much of his youth in exile in Normandy while Danish kings, including the mighty Cnut, ruled England. In 1042 the old English line was restored and Edward came to the throne.

His reign of nearly a quarter-century was, by the violent standards of the age, relatively peaceful. A devout and gentle king—his epithet "the Confessor" reflects his reputation for holiness—he was more interested in religion than war, and real power often lay with the great earls, above all the powerful house of Godwin, whose daughter Edith he married. He founded and rebuilt Westminster Abbey, consecrated only days before his death, and it became his lasting monument and burial place.

The great tragedy of his reign was that he left no child. As he lay dying in January 1066, the succession was hopelessly disputed. He seemed to entrust the kingdom to his brother-in-law Harold Godwinson, who was duly crowned—but William, Duke of Normandy, claimed that Edward had long before promised the throne to him. That contested inheritance unleashed the cataclysm of 1066: Harold's victory over the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge, and his death weeks later before William's Normans at Hastings.

Edward's death thus ended Anglo-Saxon England. Remembered as a saintly, peace-loving king, he was canonised in 1161 and became a patron saint of England, venerated above all by Henry III, who raised his great shrine at Westminster.
