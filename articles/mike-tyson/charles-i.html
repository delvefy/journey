Charles I

King of England, Scotland, and Ireland (1600–1649)

Charles I was the king whose belief in his own God-given authority brought England to civil war and himself to the scaffold. The second son of James VI and I, he came to the throne in 1625 convinced that a monarch ruled by divine right and answered to God alone. His marriage to the Catholic French princess Henrietta Maria, and his high-church religious policy, deepened the distrust of his largely Puritan subjects.

Repeatedly at odds with Parliament over money and prerogative, Charles dissolved it and from 1629 governed alone for eleven years, raising revenue by disputed means such as "ship money." His attempt to force a new prayer book on Presbyterian Scotland sparked rebellion and forced him to recall Parliament, which now moved to curb his power. In 1642 king and Parliament went to war.

Defeated in the field by the armies of Parliament and Oliver Cromwell, Charles refused every compromise, intriguing even in captivity. His enemies, despairing of any settlement, at last put their anointed king on trial for treason against his own people. On 30 January 1649 he was beheaded outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall, dying with a composure that won him many sympathizers and the later cult of a royal martyr.

For all his political failures, Charles was the most discerning art collector ever to sit on the English throne, assembling a magnificent gallery and employing Van Dyck as his court painter. The greatest master he drew to his service—who painted the glorious ceiling of the very hall beneath which the king would step to his death—was the Flemish prince of painters, Peter Paul Rubens.
