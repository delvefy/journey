Heraclitus

Greek philosopher of Ephesus (c. 535–475 BC)

Heraclitus of Ephesus was the most enigmatic of the early Greek philosophers, so given to riddling utterance that antiquity called him the Obscure, and so grave in temper that later ages made him the Weeping Philosopher. Of aristocratic birth, he scorned public life and the crowd, and set down his thoughts in a cryptic book that he is said to have placed in the great temple of Artemis.

His central insight was that all things are in ceaseless flux. “You cannot step twice into the same river,” he taught, for fresh waters are ever flowing on; the world is an ever-living fire, kindling and dying away by measure. Beneath the restless change, however, runs a hidden order and reason—the Logos—which holds the warring opposites in a tense harmony, for “war is the father of all.”

He had little patience for the famous and the learned. He mocked the wide knowledge of Pythagoras as no true wisdom, and declared that Homer deserved to be whipped and turned out of the contests—contempt that only deepened his reputation for proud obscurity.

His doctrine of universal becoming would echo for centuries, shaping the Stoics' idea of the Logos and giving Plato the image of a sensible world forever in flux. Against him stood the opposite pole of Greek thought, the philosopher who denied change altogether and argued that what truly is must be one, eternal, and unmoving—Parmenides.
