Thomas Cromwell

English statesman, chief minister to Henry VIII (c. 1485–1540)

Thomas Cromwell was the brilliant and ruthless minister who engineered the English Reformation and built the machinery of the modern English state. The son of a Putney blacksmith, he made his own way in the world as a soldier, merchant, and self-taught lawyer in Italy and the Low Countries before entering the service of Cardinal Wolsey. Alone among Wolsey's men, he survived his master's downfall and rose to become the indispensable servant of Henry VIII.

Where Wolsey had failed, Cromwell succeeded by a bolder path. Through a string of statutes driven through Parliament, he severed England from Rome, made the king Supreme Head of the Church, and dissolved the monasteries, pouring their vast wealth into the royal treasury. A administrative genius, he reshaped the king's government and the power of Parliament itself.

His power was built on the destruction of others. He assembled the case that sent Anne Boleyn to the block in 1536, and earlier he had prosecuted Sir Thomas More to his death. For a time no one stood higher in the realm. But Cromwell's enemies among the old nobility waited for a misstep.

It came with the marriage he arranged between Henry and the German princess Anne of Cleves, meant to seal a Protestant alliance. The king detested his new bride, the alliance crumbled, and Cromwell's foes pounced. In 1540 he was condemned without trial and beheaded on Tower Hill. The portrait that had flattered Anne of Cleves and helped lure the king into that fatal match was the work of the master painter Cromwell himself had patronized—Hans Holbein the Younger.
