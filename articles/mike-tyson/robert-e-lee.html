Robert E. Lee

Confederate general (1807–1870)

Robert E. Lee was the most celebrated soldier of the Confederacy and, for better and worse, the central figure of the South's memory of the Civil War. Born in Virginia to a Revolutionary War hero, he graduated near the top of his West Point class, served brilliantly as an engineer and as an officer under Winfield Scott in Mexico, and married into the family of George Washington, whose Arlington estate became his home and whose example he revered.

When Virginia seceded in 1861, Abraham Lincoln offered Lee command of the Union armies. Torn but unwilling to draw his sword against his native state, Lee declined and joined the Confederacy. As commander of the Army of Northern Virginia he won a series of audacious victories—Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville—against larger Union forces, his daring magnified by the partnership of his finest subordinate.

Twice he carried the war north, and twice he was turned back, decisively at Gettysburg in 1863, where the failure of Pickett's Charge marked the high-water mark of the rebellion. Worn down at last by the relentless campaigns of Ulysses S. Grant, he surrendered at Appomattox Court House in April 1865.

After the war he accepted the presidency of a small Virginia college and counselled reconciliation until his death in 1870. He had served the government of Jefferson Davis, and his boldest triumphs had been won at the side of Stonewall Jackson.
