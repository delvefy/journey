Thomas Jefferson

Author of the Declaration of Independence, third U.S. president (1743–1826)

Thomas Jefferson, born in Virginia in 1743, was the principal author of the Declaration of Independence and the third President of the United States. A lawyer, planter, and Enlightenment polymath steeped in the writings of Locke and Montesquieu, he was just thirty-three when he drafted the document that announced a new nation, with its ringing claim that all men are created equal and endowed with rights to "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness."

He served the young republic in nearly every high capacity—as minister to France, as Washington's first Secretary of State, and as Vice President—all the while founding, with Madison, the party that opposed Alexander Hamilton's vision of strong central power. Elected President in 1800, he doubled the size of the country in 1803 with the Louisiana Purchase, bought from Napoleon, and dispatched Lewis and Clark to explore the vast new lands.

Jefferson championed religious liberty, public education, and a republic of independent farmers. His curiosity ranged over architecture, science, and invention; he designed his beloved home at Monticello and founded the University of Virginia.

Yet the apostle of liberty was also a lifelong slaveholder, and his relationship with the enslaved Sally Hemings lays bare the deep contradictions of his ideals. In a stroke of remarkable symmetry, he died on the Fourth of July, 1826—the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration—within hours of his old friend and rival John Adams.
