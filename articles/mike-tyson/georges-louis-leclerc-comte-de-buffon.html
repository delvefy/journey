Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon

French naturalist (1707–1788)

Georges-Louis Leclerc, later Comte de Buffon, was born at Montbard in 1707 to a prosperous family. Drawn first to mathematics and the new physics, he translated into French Isaac Newton's work on fluxions before turning to the study of nature. In 1739 he was appointed keeper of the Jardin du Roi, the royal botanical garden in Paris, under Louis XV, a post he held and enlarged for nearly fifty years.

From that base he produced his life's monument, the Histoire Naturelle, an encyclopedia of the natural world that eventually ran to dozens of volumes. Written in a grand and graceful prose that made him one of the most widely read authors of the century, it surveyed the animals, minerals, and the history of the Earth itself.

Buffon was a bold speculator. He argued that the Earth was vastly older than scripture allowed, measuring its age by the cooling of heated globes, and suggested that living forms could change and degenerate over time, ideas that pointed toward later theories of evolution. His claim that the animals of the New World were smaller and feebler than those of the Old provoked a famous rebuttal from Thomas Jefferson, who had a great moose shipped to Paris to refute him. With his rival Linnaeus he disagreed sharply over how nature should be classified.

He gave early support to the naturalist Lamarck and was elected to the French Academy. He died in 1788, and Charles Darwin would later name him among the forerunners of evolutionary thought.
