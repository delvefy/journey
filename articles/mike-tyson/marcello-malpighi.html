Marcello Malpighi

Italian biologist and physician (1628–1694)

Marcello Malpighi was the founder of microscopic anatomy, the scientist who first used the microscope to reveal the hidden architecture of living things. Born near Bologna and trained in medicine, he taught at the great Italian universities and turned his lenses upon tissues and organs that the naked eye could never resolve, opening the studies we now call histology and embryology.

His most celebrated discovery came in 1661, when he observed the capillaries—the minute vessels that carry blood from the arteries into the veins. This was the missing piece in William Harvey's revolutionary theory of the circulation of the blood, which had described a closed loop without being able to show how it joined. Malpighi supplied the proof.

He went on to describe the fine structure of the lungs, the kidney, the spleen, and the skin—so many parts of the body still bear his name—and he extended his microscope to the development of the chick embryo, the anatomy of plants, and the silkworm. A Fellow of the Royal Society and physician to a pope, he was honored across Europe.

Malpighi died in Rome in 1694. The great physician whose bold theory of the circulating blood his own discovery had at last completed was William Harvey.
