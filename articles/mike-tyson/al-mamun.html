al-Ma'mun

Abbasid caliph and patron of learning (786–833)

Al-Ma'mun, born in 786, was the seventh Abbasid caliph and the son of Harun al-Rashid, remembered above all as the great royal patron of science and learning in the Islamic golden age. He came to power in 813 only after a destructive civil war against his brother al-Amin, in which Baghdad itself was besieged—but once secure, he made his reign a high point of intellectual achievement.

Al-Ma'mun threw the resources of the caliphate behind the pursuit of knowledge. He greatly expanded the institution in Baghdad known as the House of Wisdom, gathering scholars, astronomers, mathematicians and translators of every faith. Under his sponsorship the great works of Greek antiquity were rendered into Arabic—the philosophy of Aristotle, the astronomy of Ptolemy's Almagest, the geometry of Euclid, and the medicine of Galen—preserving a heritage that might otherwise have been lost. He patronised the mathematician al-Khwarizmi, whose work would give the world the words "algebra" and "algorithm," and he commissioned astronomers to measure the circumference of the Earth.

His enthusiasm extended to theology, where he embraced the rationalist Mu'tazila doctrine and, less happily, enforced it through an inquisition, the Mihna, that persecuted scholars who would not accept it. This coercion stained his reputation for openness.

Al-Ma'mun died in 833 while campaigning against the Byzantines. The dynasty's political fortunes would soon decline, but the translation movement he had fostered transformed the intellectual life of the Islamic world—and, through later transmission into Latin Europe, of the West as well. As a sponsor of the meeting of Greek, Persian and Indian learning, al-Ma'mun ranks among history's most consequential patrons of the mind.
