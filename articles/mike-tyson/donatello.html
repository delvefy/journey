Donatello

Florentine sculptor of the early Renaissance (c. 1386–1466)

Donato di Niccolò di Betto Bardi, known as Donatello, was the sculptor who taught marble and bronze to breathe again. Trained in the workshop of Lorenzo Ghiberti and a lifelong friend of Brunelleschi—with whom, by tradition, he traveled to Rome to measure and draw the ruins of antiquity—he absorbed the lost language of classical form and made it new.

His early marble figures for Orsanmichele, the alert St. George above all, gave statues a psychological inwardness no medieval carver had attempted. For the bell tower of Florence he cut gaunt, unflattering prophets, among them the bald Zuccone he is said to have begged to speak. Then, for the Medici, he cast the bronze David, the first freestanding nude since the ancient world, a work of startling sensuous calm.

Summoned to Padua, he raised the equestrian monument to the mercenary captain Gattamelata, the first great bronze horseman since Roman times, a model for every ruler's statue that followed. In old age his art turned harrowing: the wooden Penitent Magdalene, worn to bone by fasting, and the grim bronze Judith and Holofernes.

Through it all his closest patron was Cosimo de' Medici, who valued him so highly that Donatello was buried near him in San Lorenzo. His mastery of perspective in shallow relief and of raw human emotion reshaped sculpture for a century and reached its heir in the man who would carve the marble David of a later age, Michelangelo. The friend and rival who had unlocked antiquity with him, and who crowned Florence with its dome, was Filippo Brunelleschi.
