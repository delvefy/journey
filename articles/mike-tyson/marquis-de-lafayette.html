Marquis de Lafayette

French aristocrat, hero of two revolutions (1757–1834)

The Marquis de Lafayette was the “Hero of Two Worlds,” a French nobleman who fought for liberty on both sides of the Atlantic. At just nineteen he defied his king and sailed to join the American Revolution, where he became a major general and the devoted, almost filial companion of George Washington. Wounded at Brandywine and decisive in the Virginia campaign that trapped the British at Yorktown, he returned to France a celebrated champion of the American cause.

When revolution came to France, Lafayette stood at its center as a moderate liberal. He presented the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, shaped in part by his friend Thomas Jefferson, then the American minister in Paris, and he commanded the National Guard, devising the tricolor cockade. He sought to steer a middle course of constitutional monarchy, guarding the royal family of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette even as the queen distrusted him and the radicals despised him.

As the Revolution turned to terror, the faction of Maximilien Robespierre branded him a traitor; he fled, was captured by the Austrians, and languished for years in prison. Freed at last, he refused to bend to the autocracy of Napoleon, whom he steadily opposed.

His long life had a final act. During the July Revolution of 1830 he again took up the National Guard and lent his immense prestige to place a new constitutional monarch on the throne. Six years earlier he had toured the United States in triumph as the guest of President James Monroe, and at his death in 1834 he was buried under soil brought from Bunker Hill. The king he had helped to crown was Louis Philippe I.
