Samuel Beckett

Irish-born writer and dramatist (1906–1989)

Samuel Beckett was the great minimalist of modern literature, who stripped the stage and the novel down to their bones and won the Nobel Prize for a vision of comic, stubborn despair.

Born near Dublin and educated at Trinity College, he went to Paris in 1928 and fell into the orbit of James Joyce, reading to the half-blind master and helping with the work that became Finnegans Wake. His own early writing was steeped in his lifelong companions on the page: he carried Dante Alighieri with him always, naming an early hero after a figure from the Purgatorio, and his first separately published poem put its words into the mouth of the philosopher René Descartes, whose riddle of mind and body haunted everything he wrote.

He settled permanently in France, served bravely in a Resistance network during the Second World War, and after it poured out a torrent of work in French: the novels Molloy, Malone Dies and The Unnamable, and the play that made him famous, Waiting for Godot, in which two tramps wait endlessly for someone who never comes. Endgame, Krapp's Last Tape and Happy Days followed, each barer than the last, founding works of what critics called the Theatre of the Absurd.

Awarded the Nobel Prize in 1969—an honour he found an agony to his privacy—he went on writing ever more compressed fragments until his death in Paris in 1989. Long before any of it, his very first published book had been a probing essay on the great novelist of memory and lost time, Marcel Proust.
