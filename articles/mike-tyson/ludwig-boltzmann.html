Ludwig Boltzmann

Austrian physicist (1844–1906)

Ludwig Boltzmann was born in Vienna in 1844 and held a succession of professorships across Austria and Germany. Taking up the kinetic theory of gases begun by James Clerk Maxwell, he worked out how the speeds of countless molecules are distributed—the law now named for them both—and built from it a new science of statistical mechanics.

His deepest insight linked the unseen motions of atoms to the everyday flow of heat. He showed that entropy, and with it the irreversible direction of time, is a matter of probability: the world moves toward its more numerous, more disordered arrangements. The relation he found, entropy as the logarithm of the number of microscopic states, is carved on his tombstone.

All of this rested on the reality of atoms, and there Boltzmann fought a wearing battle. Influential thinkers, above all the physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach, dismissed atoms as unobservable fictions, and Boltzmann felt his life's work doubted and besieged.

Prone to deep swings of mood, he took his own life in 1906 while on holiday near Trieste—only a short time before the work of Albert Einstein and others on the jostling of tiny particles in fluids proved beyond dispute that atoms were real, vindicating everything for which he had argued.
