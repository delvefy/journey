Montesquieu

French Enlightenment political philosopher (1689–1755)

Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu, born near Bordeaux in 1689, was one of the founders of political science and the most influential constitutional theorist of the Enlightenment. Inheriting wealth and a high judicial office in the parlement of Bordeaux, he won early fame with the Persian Letters, a witty epistolary novel in which two Persian travellers cast a satirical eye over the manners, religion, and politics of France.

Turning to history, he wrote a penetrating study of the rise and decline of Rome that sought natural causes rather than fortune or divine will for the fate of empires, and that would help shape later historians of the ancient world. But his enduring monument is The Spirit of the Laws, published in 1748 after some twenty years of labour.

In it Montesquieu compared the forms of government—republic, monarchy, and despotism—each sustained, he argued, by its own animating principle, and he insisted that good laws must suit a people's climate, history, and customs. Above all he set out the doctrine of the separation of powers: that liberty is best preserved when legislative, executive, and judicial authority are lodged in distinct hands that check one another. Developing the constitutionalism of John Locke and the realism of Niccolò Machiavelli, and reaching back to Aristotle's classification of states, the idea would be written into the constitution of the new United States.

Admired and debated across Europe, his work was carried into the salons by fellow philosophes such as Voltaire and reached even the throne of Russia, where it guided the reforming ambitions of Catherine the Great.
