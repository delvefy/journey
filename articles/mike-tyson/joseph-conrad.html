Joseph Conrad

Polish-British novelist (1857–1924)

Joseph Conrad was born Józef Teodor Konrad Korzeniowski in 1857 in the Polish lands under Russian rule, the son of patriots who died when he was a boy. Restless and orphaned, he went to sea, serving some twenty years in the French and then the British merchant marine, rising to master mariner and taking British nationality. English was his third language, learned as a grown man, yet he would become one of its supreme stylists.

Only in his late thirties did he turn seriously to writing. Out of his voyages he made fiction of rare moral depth: Lord Jim, Nostromo, The Secret Agent, and above all Heart of Darkness, drawn from his own journey up the Congo, a pitiless study of the greed and savagery beneath the mask of empire. He revered the prose of Gustave Flaubert and the art of Ivan Turgenev, and forged from them a manner all his own.

Conrad pioneered the modern novel's restless techniques—framed tales, unreliable narrators such as his Marlow, and broken chronology—to probe isolation, guilt, and the testing of honor.

He settled in Kent within the brilliant Edwardian literary circle that included his friend Henry James, and the younger H.G. Wells, who had championed his early work. He died in 1924, his influence reaching deep into the literature and even the cinema of the century that followed.
