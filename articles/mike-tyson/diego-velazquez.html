Diego Velázquez

Spanish painter to Philip IV (1599–1660)

Diego Rodríguez de Silva y Velázquez was the supreme painter of the Spanish Golden Age and, for many, the most purely gifted brush in the history of European art. Born in Seville, he trained under Francisco Pacheco, whose daughter he married, and made his early name with sober, intensely observed scenes of ordinary life such as The Water Seller of Seville.

In 1623, still in his early twenties, he was summoned to Madrid and appointed painter to the young Philip IV, whose features he would record across nearly four decades. He befriended the visiting Peter Paul Rubens, who encouraged him to study in Italy; on two long Italian journeys he absorbed the Venetians and painted the startling portrait of Pope Innocent X.

His range was vast: the historical Surrender of Breda, the mythological Rokeby Venus, the equestrian and hunting portraits of the royal family, and unforgettable studies of the court's jesters and dwarfs, painted with grave dignity. Above all stands Las Meninas, an enigmatic group portrait of the royal household that folds the painter, the king and queen, and the viewer into a single illusionistic space.

Rising to high court office, he was made a knight of Santiago near the end of his life and died in Madrid in 1660. The painter he revered above all was the Venetian Titian; centuries later Pablo Picasso would pay him the homage of fifty-eight variations on Las Meninas. His most direct Spanish heir was Francisco Goya.
