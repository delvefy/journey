Genghis Khan

Founder of the Mongol Empire (c. 1162–1227)

Genghis Khan, born Temüjin around 1162, founded the Mongol Empire, the largest contiguous land empire in history. Born into a nomadic clan on the harsh Mongolian steppe, he endured a brutal childhood—his father poisoned, his family cast out to scrape for survival—before rising through cunning, courage, and force of will to dominate the feuding tribes around him. In 1206 a great assembly proclaimed him Genghis Khan, "universal ruler," over a united Mongol nation.

He remade that nation into a war machine of unprecedented efficiency, organizing his armies by tens and hundreds, promoting by merit, and binding the empire with a written script, a law code, and a swift relay-post system. Then he turned outward. His horsemen overran the Western Xia and the Jin of northern China and swept through the wealthy Khwarazmian realm of Central Asia and Persia, where great cities such as Samarkand and Nishapur were laid waste with appalling slaughter.

His campaigns combined dazzling mobility, careful intelligence, and calculated terror; resistance could mean annihilation. Yet within his domains he enforced order and tolerated all religions. He died in 1227, still on campaign.

His sons and grandsons—Ögedei, Batu, Hulagu, and Kublai—carried the conquests from Korea to the gates of Europe, and his bloodline would echo through the dynasties of Asia, claimed by later conquerors as the very fountainhead of legitimacy.
