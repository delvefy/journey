Alessandro Volta

Italian physicist, inventor of the battery (1745–1827)

Alessandro Volta was born in Como in 1745 and made his early name in the study of electricity, inventing the electrophorus for generating static charge and, turning to chemistry, identifying and isolating methane from the gas of marshes. He became professor of physics at Pavia and a respected figure across scientific Europe, building in part on the electrical theories of Benjamin Franklin.

His career turned on a famous dispute. His countryman Luigi Galvani had found that a frog's legs twitched when touched with two different metals and concluded that the tissue held a vital “animal electricity.” Volta disagreed, insisting that the electricity arose simply from the contact of dissimilar metals, with the moist tissue serving only as a conductor.

To settle the matter he built, in 1800, a stack of alternating zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked cloth—the voltaic pile, the first device to deliver a steady electric current. It was one of the most consequential inventions in the history of science, opening the way to electrochemistry, to Humphry Davy's discovery of new elements, and to the electromagnetism of Michael Faraday.

Volta demonstrated his pile before Napoleon Bonaparte, who was so impressed that he made him a count and a senator of the Kingdom of Italy. He retired to Como and died there in 1827. The volt, the unit of electric potential, preserves his name.
