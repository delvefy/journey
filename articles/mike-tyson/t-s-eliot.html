T. S. Eliot

Modernist poet and critic (1888–1965)

T. S. Eliot, born Thomas Stearns Eliot in St. Louis, Missouri, in 1888, became the most influential poet and critic writing in English in the twentieth century. Educated at Harvard and later settling permanently in England, he abandoned an academic career for poetry, banking and then publishing, eventually becoming a director of the firm of Faber and Faber.

His early poems, beginning with The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock, broke radically with Victorian verse in their fragmented, ironic, allusive modern voice. In 1922 he published The Waste Land, a dense, despairing panorama of a civilisation in ruins after the Great War. Edited and sharpened by his friend Ezra Pound, to whom it is dedicated, it became the defining poem of literary Modernism and reshaped English poetry.

Eliot's conversion to Anglo-Catholic Christianity in 1927 transformed his later work. The meditative, religious Four Quartets is widely held to be his masterpiece. He also turned to the stage, and his verse drama Murder in the Cathedral, on the martyrdom of Thomas Becket, remains his most performed play. Late in life his light verse about cats would even be reborn as one of the most successful musicals ever staged.

As a critic he was equally commanding, his essays redefining the canon and championing Dante—whom he revered above all poets—while his editorial influence at Faber shaped a generation, not least through his admiration for James Joyce. Awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1948, Eliot was by then a towering literary authority. He died in London in 1965, the poet who had given the modern age its most enduring voice of doubt and faith.
