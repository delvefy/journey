Ezra

Jewish priest and scribe (5th century BC)

Ezra was the priest and scholar who, more than any other, refounded the religious life of the Jewish people after the long catastrophe of exile. Of priestly descent and deeply learned in the Law, he lived among the Jews of Babylon until the Persian king Artaxerxes gave him a royal commission to lead a body of exiles home to Jerusalem and to re-establish there the Law of Moses.

Arriving in the holy city, he found a community grown lax in observance, and he set about a stern reform. He gathered the people and read the Torah aloud to them from morning to midday, with interpreters explaining its meaning, and bound them to keep it. Most controversially, he demanded that the men send away the foreign wives they had married, fearing that intermarriage would dissolve the small community into the surrounding peoples.

So central was he to the restoration that later tradition almost set him beside the lawgiver himself, saying that Ezra was worthy to have received the Torah had Moses not come first. He was remembered as the founder of the Great Assembly, the fixer of the sacred scriptures, and the father of the scribal learning that would shape Judaism for ages—a second Moses for a second beginning.

His work of rebuilding was shared with another leader sent from the Persian court, a layman and royal official who restored the city's shattered defenses while Ezra restored its soul. That governor, who rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem, was Nehemiah.
