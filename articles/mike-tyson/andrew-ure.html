Andrew Ure

Scottish chemist and writer on manufactures (1778–1857)

Andrew Ure was born in Glasgow in 1778 and trained as a physician before becoming a professor at the Andersonian Institution, an early school of practical science for working people. A tireless compiler, he produced widely used dictionaries of chemistry and of the arts and manufactures that spread technical knowledge to industry.

In 1818 he gained notoriety with a galvanic experiment in the manner of Giovanni Aldini, applying electric currents to the body of the executed murderer Matthew Clydesdale before a Glasgow audience. The corpse heaved its chest, its limbs jerked, and its face worked into hideous expressions, and Ure speculated that life itself might in some cases be restored by such means.

His most influential work was The Philosophy of Manufactures of 1835, a confident defense of the factory system and its machinery, which he praised as a triumph of order, discipline, and productive power over the disorder of handicraft. The book became a touchstone of the debate over industrialization.

It also drew the scorn of its critics. Karl Marx quoted Ure again and again in Capital, mocking him as the “Pindar of the automatic factory,” while Friedrich Engels mined his pages for evidence of the conditions of the workers. Ure died in London in 1857, his rosy account of the mills having become, against his intention, a chief source for their fiercest enemies.
