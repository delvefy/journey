Albert Einstein

Theoretical physicist (1879–1955)

Albert Einstein, born in Ulm, Germany, in 1879, was the theoretical physicist whose revolution in our understanding of space, time, and gravity made him the twentieth century's icon of scientific genius. An indifferent fit for conventional schooling, he took a humble post as a patent clerk in Bern, and it was there, in his "miracle year" of 1905, that he transformed physics in a series of papers written in his spare time.

That year he introduced special relativity, with its startling conclusions that time and length depend on motion and that mass and energy are equivalent, expressed as E=mc². He also explained the photoelectric effect in terms of light quanta—work that helped found quantum theory and earned him the 1921 Nobel Prize—and accounted for the jittering of microscopic particles in Brownian motion.

A decade later he completed his masterpiece, the general theory of relativity, which recast gravity as the curvature of spacetime by matter and energy. When a 1919 solar eclipse confirmed its prediction that starlight bends near the sun, Einstein became an overnight global celebrity. Yet he never accepted the randomness at the heart of quantum mechanics, sparring for years with Niels Bohr and insisting that "God does not play dice."

A Jew and a pacifist, Einstein fled Nazi Germany in 1933 for Princeton. In 1939 he signed a letter urging President Roosevelt to pursue an atomic bomb, a step he later regretted. An outspoken advocate for civil rights and world peace, he declined the presidency of Israel and spent his final years seeking a unified theory. He died in 1955.
