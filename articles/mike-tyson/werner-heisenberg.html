Werner Heisenberg

German physicist (1901–1976)

Werner Heisenberg was born in Würzburg in 1901 and trained under the finest physicists of Germany, studying with Arnold Sommerfeld in Munich and Max Born in Göttingen before joining Niels Bohr in Copenhagen. In 1925, while recovering from hay fever on the island of Helgoland, he conceived a wholly new mechanics that abandoned the picture of electrons in orbit and dealt only with observable quantities. Worked out with Born and Pascual Jordan, this matrix mechanics was the first complete quantum theory.

Two years later he stated the principle for which he is best known: that the position and the momentum of a particle can never both be known with perfect precision, the uncertainty being woven into nature itself. With Bohr's complementarity it formed the heart of the Copenhagen interpretation, and it won Heisenberg the Nobel Prize in 1932.

During the Second World War he stayed in Germany and became the leading figure of its atomic research, the subject of his fraught and much-debated 1941 visit to Bohr. The German bomb was never built, and historians still argue whether this owed to scientific failure or to reluctance.

After the war Heisenberg helped rebuild German science and pursued a unified theory of matter until his death in 1976. His matrix mechanics had found its mirror image in the wave mechanics of Erwin Schrödinger.
