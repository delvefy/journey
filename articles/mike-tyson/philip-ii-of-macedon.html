Philip II of Macedon

King of Macedon (382–336 BC)

Philip II of Macedon took a poor and divided kingdom on the northern edge of the Greek world and forged it into the master of Greece. As a youth he had been held hostage in Thebes during its years of greatness, and there he studied the tactics of Epaminondas; on his return he applied those lessons to remake the Macedonian army into the most formidable fighting force of the age.

He armed his infantry with the long pike, or sarissa, drilled them into a deep, bristling phalanx, and paired them with the shock of his noble Companion Cavalry and a corps of siege engineers. With this army, and with the gold of newly won mines that he spent freely on bribery as well as war—boasting that no city was safe from an ass laden with gold—he expanded in every direction, swallowing Thessaly, Thrace, and the cities of the north.

His rise alarmed the Greek states, above all Athens, where the orator Demosthenes thundered against him in speech after speech. The reckoning came at Chaeronea in 338 BC, where Philip crushed a coalition of Athens and Thebes; his teenaged son led the charge that destroyed the Theban Sacred Band. Master now of Greece, he bound the cities into a league under his command and prepared a great invasion of Persia.

For his son's education he had summoned the philosopher Aristotle. But in 336 BC, before he could march east, Philip was struck down by an assassin at a wedding feast, leaving his crown, his army, and his Persian war to Alexander the Great.
