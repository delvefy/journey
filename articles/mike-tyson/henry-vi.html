Henry VI

King of England and disputed King of France (1421–1471)

Henry VI, born in 1421, became king of England before his first birthday when his father, the conquering Henry V, died in 1422. Two months later the death of his grandfather Charles VI of France made the infant, by the Treaty of Troyes, the claimant king of France as well. No man ever inherited grander titles to so little purpose.

A long minority left power in the hands of quarrelling magnates, and the boy who emerged was gentle, devout and wholly unsuited to rule. While he prayed and founded his great colleges at Eton and at Cambridge, the English position in France collapsed. The tide had already turned with the rise of Joan of Arc, who broke the siege of Orléans and saw his rival Charles VII crowned; by 1453 the English had lost everything in France but Calais, and the Hundred Years' War was over in defeat.

That humiliation, and Henry's descent into spells of catatonic madness, opened the way to civil war. The Wars of the Roses pitted his Lancastrian house, championed by his fierce queen Margaret of Anjou, against the Yorkists led by Richard, Duke of York, and then by York's son Edward. In 1461 Edward seized the crown as Edward IV, and Henry became a hunted fugitive.

He was captured and imprisoned, briefly restored to the throne in 1470 by the Earl of Warwick, then deposed again when Edward returned in 1471. After the Yorkist victory at Tewkesbury, where Henry's only son was killed, the captive king was put to death in the Tower of London. Pious and pitiable, Henry VI was soon venerated as a holy martyr, and Shakespeare made his tragic, ineffectual reign the subject of three plays.
