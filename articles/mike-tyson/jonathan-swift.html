Jonathan Swift

Anglo-Irish satirist and clergyman (1667–1745)

Jonathan Swift was the greatest satirist in the English language, a clergyman whose savage wit was turned against the cruelty, vanity, and folly of his age.

Born in Dublin of English parents, he served in his youth as secretary to a noted statesman, and in that household began both his writing and his ordination in the Church of Ireland. He rose to be Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral in Dublin, and from that pulpit and pen became the fierce champion of the Irish poor against English misrule—rousing the country with the Drapier's Letters, and shaming it with A Modest Proposal, in which he calmly suggested the Irish sell their children as food.

In London he had been a power among writers, joining Pope, Gay, and Arbuthnot in the Scriblerus Club to mock the pedantry of the day. His masterpiece, Gulliver's Travels, sends its traveller among the tiny Lilliputians, the giants of Brobdingnag, and the rational horses, all to hold a pitiless mirror to mankind—a fantastical voyage squarely in the tradition of Lucian.

His last years were clouded by illness and despair, and he left his fortune to found a hospital for the insane. His self-written epitaph speaks of a heart torn by “savage indignation.”

His dearest companion in letters, and his match in wit, was the poet Alexander Pope.
