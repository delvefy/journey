Andy Warhol

American pop artist (1928–1987)

Andy Warhol was the foremost figure of Pop Art and one of the most influential artists of the twentieth century, a man who turned the images of consumer culture and celebrity into high art.

The son of Slovak immigrants in Pittsburgh, he made his name first as a commercial illustrator before transforming the art world in the early 1960s. He painted what surrounded everyone—Campbell's soup cans, Coca-Cola bottles—and produced silkscreen portraits in repeating grids of the famous and the infamous: Marilyn Monroe, Elizabeth Taylor, Elvis, and Chairman Mao. The Marilyns, made after her death, became among the most recognised images of modern art.

His studio, the Factory, was a glittering, chaotic salon of artists, musicians, and hangers-on; there he made experimental films and championed the rock band the Velvet Underground. He coined the prophecy that everyone would one day be famous for fifteen minutes, and he himself became as celebrated as any of his subjects. In 1968 he was shot and gravely wounded by a disturbed acquaintance.

He erased the old line between art and commerce, founding a magazine and courting the rich and notorious. In the 1980s he took up with a brilliant young painter of the new generation, Jean-Michel Basquiat, with whom he collaborated. He died in 1987.
