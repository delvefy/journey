Richard III

Last Yorkist king of England (1452–1485)

Richard III, born in 1452, was the youngest son of Richard, Duke of York, and the last king of the Plantagenet line. Through the turbulent reign of his elder brother Edward IV he was the most loyal of the Yorkist princes, rewarded with the dukedom of Gloucester and vast power in the north of England, where he won a reputation as a capable soldier and administrator.

Everything changed when Edward IV died suddenly in 1483, leaving a twelve-year-old heir, Edward V. Named Lord Protector, Richard moved swiftly: he intercepted the young king, confined him and his younger brother in the Tower of London, and had Parliament declare the boys illegitimate. In June 1483 Richard took the crown himself. The two princes were never seen again, and the suspicion that he had murdered his own nephews blackened his name forever.

His reign lasted barely two years. Though he passed sound laws and tried to govern justly, the manner of his accession had cost him trust. The Duke of Buckingham rebelled, and the exiled Lancastrian claimant Henry Tudor gathered support abroad. In August 1485 the two met at Bosworth Field in Leicestershire. Deserted by key followers at the crucial moment, Richard charged headlong at Henry and was cut down—the last English king to die in battle.

His death ended the Wars of the Roses and the Plantagenet dynasty, and the victorious Tudors ensured that history remembered him as a deformed and murderous tyrant. Thomas More's hostile history and, above all, Shakespeare's brilliant stage villain fixed that image for posterity, though modern scholars argue how much is propaganda. The discovery of his battle-scarred skeleton beneath a Leicester car park in 2012—confirming the curved spine of legend—reopened the long debate over England's most controversial king.
