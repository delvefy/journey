Mark Antony

Roman general and triumvir (83–30 BC)

Mark Antony was the brave, charismatic, and dissolute soldier who stood at the centre of Rome's final descent into autocracy. A kinsman and devoted lieutenant of Julius Caesar, he fought at his side through the conquest of Gaul and the civil war, commanded a wing of the army at Pharsalus, and served as his fellow consul. At the festival of the Lupercalia he famously offered Caesar a crown, which the dictator made a show of refusing.

When Caesar was struck down on the Ides of March, Antony seized the moment with his funeral oration, turning the Roman people in fury against the assassins. He then joined Octavian and Lepidus in the Second Triumvirate, whose bloody proscriptions claimed the life of his old enemy Cicero, and he avenged Caesar by crushing the conspirators at Philippi.

Taking the wealthy East as his domain, Antony allied himself with—and was captivated by—Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, with whom he had children and shared a court of legendary splendour. His liaison and his slights to his Roman wife gave Octavian the weapons for a propaganda war that ended in open conflict.

At the sea battle of Actium in 31 BC his cause was lost; he fled with Cleopatra to Egypt and, as Octavian closed in the next year, fell upon his own sword. His death left Octavian—soon Augustus—the master of the Roman world. Among the men Antony had defeated at Philippi was the noblest of Caesar's assassins, Marcus Junius Brutus.
