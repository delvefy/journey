Miyamoto Musashi

Japanese swordsman and strategist (c. 1584–1645)

Miyamoto Musashi, born around 1584, was the most famous swordsman in Japanese history—a duelist, strategist, artist and philosopher whose life passed swiftly into legend. Living in the turbulent years when the long civil wars ended and the Tokugawa shogunate was established, he claimed in his own writings to have fought his first lethal duel at the age of thirteen and to have been undefeated in more than sixty encounters.

By tradition he took part as a youth in the great battles of the age, including the climactic clash at Sekigahara in 1600 and the later Siege of Osaka, fighting on the losing side against the forces of Tokugawa Ieyasu. But it was as a wandering swordsman, perfecting his art through single combat, that he made his name. He developed a distinctive two-sword fighting style, the Niten Ichi-ryū, wielding the long and short blades together.

His most celebrated duel, in 1612, was against the master swordsman Sasaki Kojirō on the island of Ganryūjima. According to legend Musashi arrived deliberately late to unnerve his rival and struck him down with a wooden sword he had carved from an oar on the way over. After such exploits he gradually gave up dueling.

In his last years, living in a cave, Musashi set down his philosophy of strategy in The Book of Five Rings, a treatise on swordsmanship and tactics still read today by martial artists and businessmen alike. He was also an accomplished ink painter and calligrapher. He died around 1645. Much of his story is embroidered by later tradition—above all by modern novels and films—but the historical Musashi remains the supreme emblem of the samurai warrior.
