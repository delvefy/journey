Friedrich Engels

German philosopher, co-founder of Marxism (1820–1895)

Friedrich Engels was the co-founder, with Karl Marx, of the body of thought that bears Marx's name. Born in 1820 to a wealthy Rhineland textile family, he was sent to help run the family cotton business in Manchester, then the workshop of the industrial world. What he saw there—the squalor and misery of the mill workers—moved him to write The Condition of the Working Class in England, a searing portrait of industrial capitalism.

In 1844 he met Marx, and the two formed one of the most famous partnerships in the history of ideas. Together they wrote The Communist Manifesto and other founding works, fusing the dialectic of Hegel and the materialism of Feuerbach into a new theory of history driven by class struggle. For decades Engels used the income of the hated business to support his impoverished friend, so that Marx might labour at his masterpiece.

When Marx died in 1883, leaving Das Kapital unfinished, it was Engels who deciphered the manuscripts and brought the later volumes to print. In his own right he wrote widely on philosophy, economics, anthropology, and the natural sciences, and helped lead the international socialist movement until his death in 1895.

The ideas he and Marx set down became the creed of revolutions across the globe—taken up by Lenin and Rosa Luxemburg, and carried into the largest nation on earth by Mao Zedong.
