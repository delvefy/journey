Avicenna

Persian polymath and physician (c. 980–1037)

Ibn Sina, known in the West as Avicenna, was born around 980 near Bukhara in Central Asia and became the most influential thinker of the Islamic Golden Age. A prodigy who is said to have memorized the Quran by the age of ten, he was largely self-taught, mastering law, medicine, and the sciences, and famously struggling with Aristotle's Metaphysics until a commentary by al-Farabi unlocked it for him.

He led a restless life, serving as physician and minister to a succession of Persian princes amid political upheaval, often writing through the night. From this turmoil came two monumental works. The Canon of Medicine gathered the Greek learning of Hippocrates and Galen together with Persian and Arabic knowledge into a vast, systematic encyclopedia that remained the standard medical textbook in both Europe and the Islamic world for some six hundred years.

His Book of Healing, despite its title a philosophical and scientific summa, set out an ambitious system blending Aristotle's logic and physics with a Neoplatonic vision of all things flowing from a single Necessary Existent—his name for God. He distinguished a thing's essence from its existence, a distinction that would prove crucial to later philosophy, and devised the "floating man" thought experiment to argue that the soul is aware of itself independent of the body.

Avicenna died in 1037. His medicine and metaphysics shaped the Islamic world for centuries and, in Latin translation, deeply influenced the Scholastics, including Thomas Aquinas.
