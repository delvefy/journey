Wanli Emperor

Ming emperor of China (1563–1620)

The Wanli Emperor, born in 1563, reigned from 1572 to 1620 and was the longest-ruling emperor of China's Ming dynasty—a reign that began in vigour and ended in a long, fateful paralysis. He came to the throne as a boy of nine, and for his first decade the government was ably run by the brilliant, autocratic Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng, who reformed the finances and strengthened the state.

After Zhang's death the young emperor took the reins himself and for a time ruled energetically. The crowning effort of his active years was the great intervention in Korea: when the Japanese warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded in the 1590s, the Wanli Emperor sent huge Ming armies to aid his Korean vassals, and the long, costly Imjin War ended in the repulse of the Japanese—though it drained the Ming treasury.

His later reign, however, became infamous. Frustrated by his officials, especially over the choice of his heir, the emperor withdrew almost entirely from government. For decades he refused to hold audiences, left thousands of offices unfilled, and let memorials pile up unanswered, governing through eunuchs from the depths of the Forbidden City. The bureaucracy drifted and factions festered.

It was during his reign that the Jesuit Matteo Ricci reached Beijing, opening a remarkable channel between China and Europe. But on the northern frontier a Manchu chieftain, Nurhaci, was forging the power that would one day overthrow the dynasty. Historians often date the beginning of the Ming decline to the Wanli Emperor's long neglect; his reign, outwardly magnificent, sowed the seeds of the dynasty's fall a generation after his death in 1620.
