Pope Sixtus IV

Pope, builder of the Sistine Chapel (1414–1484)

Francesco della Rovere rose from a poor family through the Franciscan order, where his learning won him the office of minister general, and in 1471 the cardinals elected him pope as Sixtus IV. He proved one of the great worldly pontiffs of the Renaissance, a builder and politician as much as a churchman, who left Rome and the papacy transformed for good and ill.

His most enduring monument is the chapel that bears his name. He raised the Sistine Chapel and summoned the finest painters of Italy—among them Sandro Botticelli—to cover its walls with frescoes, a generation before its ceiling was given to Michelangelo. He refounded the Vatican Library on a grand scale and rebuilt much of the city, throwing a new bridge across the Tiber and opening its crowded streets.

He was also notorious. He showered offices and principalities on his della Rovere and Riario nephews—one of whom would become the formidable Pope Julius II—and his hunger for their advancement drew him into the darkest deed of his reign: complicity in the Pazzi conspiracy of 1478, the plot to murder the Medici brothers in Florence Cathedral, which killed Giuliano and turned Lorenzo de' Medici into a lasting enemy.

In the same year he issued the bull that licensed the Spanish Inquisition at the urging of Ferdinand and Isabella, unleashing a power he could not recall. Among the masters he set to paint his great chapel, and who there created the solemn Delivery of the Keys, was Pietro Perugino.
