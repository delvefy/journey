Edmund Burke

Anglo-Irish statesman and political philosopher (1729–1797)

Edmund Burke was the greatest political thinker of his age and is often called the father of modern conservatism. Born in Dublin, he made his way in London first as a writer and then as a Whig member of Parliament, where his magnificent oratory and depth of thought soon made him a force. He moved among the finest minds of the day as a member of Samuel Johnson's literary Club.

Burke's politics were principled and often generous. He pleaded for conciliation with the rebellious American colonies, defended the persecuted Catholics of his native Ireland, and spent years prosecuting the impeachment of Warren Hastings for the misgovernment of British India. He was no mere reactionary but a champion of ordered liberty and reform within tradition.

His most famous work came late. In Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), he denounced the Revolution's violence and its faith in abstract reason, defending instead the slow growth of inherited institutions and warning, with uncanny foresight, that the upheaval would end in tyranny. His lament for the fate of Queen Marie Antoinette became one of the most quoted passages in English prose.

The book set off the greatest pamphlet war of the era. Foremost among the radicals who rose to answer Burke and defend the Revolution and the rights of man was the firebrand Thomas Paine.
