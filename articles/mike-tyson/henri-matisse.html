Henri Matisse

French painter, master of colour and leader of Fauvism (1869–1954)

Henri Matisse was, with Picasso, one of the two giants of modern art, and its supreme master of colour. He came to painting late, abandoning a career in law, and within a few years was leading the group derided as the “Fauves”—the wild beasts—for the shocking, pure, unnaturalistic colour of canvases like Woman with a Hat. Where Picasso fractured form, Matisse pursued harmony, joy, and decorative splendour.

Across half a century he produced an art of serene radiance: the whirling figures of The Dance, the saturated interiors, the languorous odalisques. He revered Cezanne as a master and treasured one of his paintings as a talisman, and he carried on with Picasso a lifelong dialogue of rivalry and respect, each pushing the other to greater daring.

In his last years, bedridden and unable to stand at an easel, he invented a wholly new art form—the cut-out, in which he “drew with scissors,” shaping brilliant sheets of painted paper into bold, simple, glowing compositions—and crowned his career by designing a chapel in the south of France.

Among the painters whose liberation of pure, emotional colour had opened the path he would follow, none blazed more intensely than the Dutch post-impressionist Vincent van Gogh.
