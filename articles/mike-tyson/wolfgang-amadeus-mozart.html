Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Austrian composer (1756–1791)

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was perhaps the most naturally gifted composer who ever lived, the very emblem of musical genius. Born in Salzburg in 1756, he was a prodigy almost beyond belief—playing the keyboard at three, composing at five, and touring the courts of Europe as a small child under the proud, exacting management of his father Leopold. The marvel of the boy grew into the mastery of the man.

In a life of only thirty-five years he produced more than six hundred works and triumphed in every form. His operas—The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, The Magic Flute, Così fan tutte—remain pillars of the repertoire; his last symphonies, especially the great G-minor and the "Jupiter," crown the Classical style; and his piano concertos, chamber music, and unfinished Requiem are treasured the world over. His music married effortless grace with surprising depth and drama.

Restless under the servitude of court patronage, Mozart broke free to live as an independent artist in Vienna—winning fame but never lasting security. He died in 1791, poor and exhausted, the Requiem left incomplete, and was buried without ceremony. Around his early death legends quickly gathered, including the lurid tale of poisoning by a jealous rival.

The older composer who befriended him, exchanged works of admiration with him, and whom Mozart regarded as the reigning master of the age was Joseph Haydn.
