Matteo Ricci

Jesuit missionary to Ming China (1552–1610)

Matteo Ricci, born in 1552 in Macerata, Italy, was a Jesuit priest and the most influential European in late-Ming China, a pioneer of cultural exchange between East and West. Trained in Rome in the humanities and in mathematics under the great Jesuit scholar Christopher Clavius, he joined the Society of Jesus and sailed east, following the missionary path opened by Francis Xavier a generation before.

Ricci's method was revolutionary. Rather than demand that the Chinese abandon their culture, he immersed himself in it: he mastered classical Chinese, dressed first as a Buddhist monk and then as a Confucian scholar, and won the respect of the literati by his learning. He presented Western marvels—mechanical clocks, prisms, and above all a great world map—as gifts and curiosities.

His scholarly achievements bridged two civilisations. With his Chinese convert and collaborator Xu Guangqi he translated the first books of Euclid's Elements into Chinese, introducing Western geometry to the empire, and he produced works on astronomy, cartography and Christian doctrine in elegant Chinese. After years of patient progress he was at last permitted, under the Wanli Emperor, to settle in Beijing in 1601, the first Jesuit to reside in the imperial capital.

Ricci died in Beijing in 1610 and, by special imperial favour, was granted a burial plot in the city—an unheard-of honour for a foreigner. He left behind a small Christian community, a body of Chinese scholarly works, and a model of respectful "accommodation" that shaped the Jesuit mission for a century. He remains a revered symbol of dialogue between China and Europe.
