Eratosthenes

Greek polymath and geographer (c. 276–194 BC)

Eratosthenes of Cyrene was the great universal scholar of the Hellenistic age and chief librarian of the famous Library of Alexandria. So broad were his interests—mathematics, astronomy, geography, poetry, history, literary criticism—that rivals nicknamed him "Beta," the second letter, jesting that he was second-best at everything and first at nothing; admirers more justly called him the all-rounder. Among his correspondents was Archimedes, who dedicated to him one of his most ingenious works.

His most celebrated feat was to measure the size of the Earth. Learning that at noon on the summer solstice the sun stood directly overhead at Syene, casting no shadow, while at Alexandria it fell at a measurable angle, he used the distance between the two cities and simple geometry to calculate the planet's circumference—and came astonishingly close to the true figure.

Eratosthenes founded the science of geography, even coining the word, and drew a map of the inhabited world ruled with lines of latitude and longitude. He also reckoned the tilt of the Earth's axis, compiled a chronology of history reaching back to the fall of Troy, and devised the elegant "sieve" still used to sift out the prime numbers, a method in the number-theoretic tradition of Euclid.

By tradition he went blind in old age and ended his own life by refusing food. The mathematical geography he created would, three centuries later, be gathered up and extended by Ptolemy.
