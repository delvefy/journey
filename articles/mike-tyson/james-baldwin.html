James Baldwin

American writer and civil-rights voice (1924–1987)

James Baldwin was one of the most eloquent American writers of the twentieth century, an essayist and novelist whose searing prose laid bare the realities of race and identity.

Born in Harlem and a boy preacher in its storefront churches, he fled both the pulpit and American racism by moving to Paris, where he found the distance to write. His early mentor Richard Wright helped him, until Baldwin broke with him in essays attacking the limits of the “protest novel.” His own fiction—Go Tell It on the Mountain and the daring Giovanni's Room—and above all his blazing essays made him a singular voice.

With The Fire Next Time he became a conscience of the civil rights era, moving between the worlds of Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X, and articulating the cost of American racism more powerfully than almost anyone. Among his close companions was the actor Marlon Brando, his friend since their struggling years in New York. His friend Maya Angelou treasured him as a fellow witness to the Black American experience.

He spent his last decades largely in France and died there in 1987, his reputation only growing in the years that followed. The Nobel-winning novelist who delivered a soaring eulogy at his funeral, and who carried his vision of Black American life into a new generation, was Toni Morrison.
