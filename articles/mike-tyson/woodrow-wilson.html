Woodrow Wilson

28th President of the United States (1856–1924)

Woodrow Wilson was the scholar-president who led the United States into the First World War and tried, and failed, to remake the world order around a League of Nations.

Born in Virginia and raised in the war-torn South, he became a distinguished political scientist and president of Princeton before entering politics as governor of New Jersey. He won the White House in 1912 only because Theodore Roosevelt's third-party candidacy split the Republican vote. His “New Freedom” reshaped the economy with the Federal Reserve, the Federal Trade Commission, and a federal income tax. Among the young men who served in his administration was the future president Franklin D. Roosevelt, his Assistant Secretary of the Navy.

Re-elected in 1916 on the boast that he had kept the country out of war, he reversed course in 1917 after German submarine attacks, taking America into the conflict to “make the world safe for democracy.” He framed the peace around his idealistic Fourteen Points and crossed the Atlantic to the Paris Peace Conference, where as one of the Big Four he wrangled with the hard-headed Georges Clemenceau over the fate of Germany and the shape of the new Europe. He won the Nobel Peace Prize and the creation of his cherished League.

At home, however, the Senate refused to ratify the treaty, and a punishing speaking tour ended in a stroke that left him an invalid for his final months in office. The economist John Maynard Keynes pilloried the whole settlement—and Wilson's part in it—in a celebrated polemic. The British half of that bruising partnership at the peace table was the Welsh wizard, David Lloyd George.
