Guru Tegh Bahadur

Ninth Sikh Guru and martyr (1621–1675)

Guru Tegh Bahadur was the ninth of the Sikh Gurus and is honoured as one of the great martyrs of conscience in Indian history. The youngest son of the sixth Guru, he was a man of deep contemplation who spent long years in meditation and travel, founding the town of Anandpur in the Punjab hills and carrying the teaching of Guru Nanak across northern and eastern India.

His martyrdom arose from a defence not of his own faith but of another's. When a delegation of Kashmiri Hindu Brahmins came to him in despair at being forced to convert to Islam under the policies of the emperor Aurangzeb, the Guru took up their cause, declaring that no one should be compelled in matters of belief. Summoned to Delhi, he refused either to convert or to renounce his stand.

In 1675 he was publicly beheaded in the imperial capital, together with his companions. For dying in defence of the religious freedom of people not his own, Sikh tradition remembers him as the “Shield of India.” His severed cause passed at once to his young son, who would answer the persecution by forging the militant Khalsa.

Guru Tegh Bahadur's own hymns, grave and meditative, were later added to the Sikh scripture. He stood in a line of sacrifice: his grandfather, the fifth Guru, who had gathered the Sikh scriptures into a single volume and become the faith's first martyr, was Guru Arjan.
