Fibonacci

Italian mathematician of Pisa (c. 1170–c. 1250)

Leonardo of Pisa, remembered as Fibonacci, was the merchant's son who taught Europe to count anew. His father served as a Pisan trade official at Bugia on the North African coast, and there the boy learned the calculation of the Arab world—above all the Hindu system of nine digits and a zero, with which sums could be written and worked on paper rather than fumbled on an abacus.

In 1202 he set down what he had learned in the Liber Abaci, the Book of Calculation, a great manual that explained the new numerals and showed merchants how to use them for prices, weights, interest, and exchange. Slowly, against the inertia of Roman numerals, it won Europe over to the decimal system the world still uses. Tucked among its problems was the puzzle of breeding rabbits whose solution is the famous sequence that now bears his name.

Fibonacci was no mere popularizer. His Book of Squares was an original and elegant work of number theory, and his reputation reached the dazzling court of the Emperor Frederick II, whose scholars set him hard problems to test his powers—challenges he met and surpassed, drawing on the Greek tradition of Euclid.

Through him the arithmetic of the Arabs and the Hindus entered the counting-houses and schools of Italy, where it would underpin the commerce of the Renaissance. The friar and mathematician who, two and a half centuries later, gathered that commercial arithmetic into a famous compendium and gave the world double-entry bookkeeping was Luca Pacioli.
