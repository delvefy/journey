Laurence Olivier

English actor, hailed as the greatest of his century (1907–1989)

Laurence Olivier was, by wide agreement, the foremost actor of the twentieth century, equally commanding on the stage and the screen. His art was rooted in Shakespeare, whom he served as no one else of his age: he directed and starred in a trio of landmark films—a heroic Henry V made to lift British spirits in wartime, a brooding Hamlet that won him Academy Awards for both acting and picture, and a chilling Richard III—that brought the plays to vast new audiences.

His range was prodigious. He could be the romantic Heathcliff of Wuthering Heights, the seedy comedian of The Entertainer, or the sadistic dentist of Marathon Man, terrifying audiences with the murmured question “Is it safe?” Off the screen he was a builder of institutions, the founder and first director of Britain's National Theatre, and the first actor ever raised to a life peerage.

He stood at the head of a brilliant generation of British actors that included Gielgud and Richardson, and he became the measure of greatness against which younger stars such as Burton were judged.

His private life drew nearly as much attention as his art, above all his passionate and troubled marriage to a beautiful and gifted actress who was his equal in fame. She had won lasting glory as Scarlett O'Hara in Gone with the Wind, and her name was Vivien Leigh.
