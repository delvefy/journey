Commodus

Roman emperor (161–192)

Commodus was the emperor whose vanity and misrule shattered the golden age of the Antonines. The son of the philosopher-emperor Marcus Aurelius, he was raised in the purple and made co-ruler while still a youth, succeeding as sole emperor at eighteen in the year 180. Unlike the run of "good emperors" who had been chosen for merit and adopted, Commodus owed his throne to birth alone—and he proved disastrously unworthy of it.

He quickly abandoned his father's grinding wars on the Danube for a hasty peace and sank into a reign of pleasure, neglect, and growing megalomania. He surrendered the business of government to corrupt favorites while indulging fantasies of divinity, identifying himself with Hercules, posing in a lion's skin, and renaming Rome, the legions, and the very months of the year after himself.

Most scandalous of all, he descended into the arena to fight as a gladiator before the Roman crowd—rigged combats he always won—degrading the dignity of the imperial office. Plots multiplied, including one led by his own sister Lucilla. At last, on the final day of 192, his attendants had him strangled in his bath by a wrestling partner.

His murder left no clear successor and plunged the empire into the chaotic Year of the Five Emperors. From that scramble of generals and pretenders, the hard, capable commander who fought his way to the throne and founded a new dynasty was Septimius Severus.
