John Lennon

Beatle, songwriter, and peace activist (1940–1980)

John Lennon was the founder and restless creative spirit of the Beatles, the most successful and influential band in the history of popular music. A working-class boy from Liverpool, he started a skiffle group as a teenager and drew into it the musicians who would conquer the world. Idolizing Elvis and the American rock-and-rollers, he and his bandmates remade their inheritance into something wholly new.

The hysteria of Beatlemania gave way, across only a few astonishing years, to the studio artistry of Rubber Soul, Revolver, and Sgt. Pepper's, as the band transformed what popular music could attempt. Lennon's own songs were the sharpest and most searching—by turns witty, caustic, surreal, and achingly honest.

In the late 1960s he married the avant-garde artist Yoko Ono and threw himself into the cause of peace, staging playful protests and writing the singalong anthem “Give Peace a Chance.” After the Beatles dissolved in 1970 he made a celebrated solo career, crowned by “Imagine,” his utopian hymn to a world without division.

In December 1980, at the height of a comeback, he was shot dead by a deranged admirer outside his New York apartment, only forty years old; the world mourned as for few others. The partner with whom he had formed the most celebrated songwriting team in the history of popular music was Paul McCartney.
