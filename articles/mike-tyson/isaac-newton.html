Isaac Newton

English physicist and mathematician (1642–1727)

Isaac Newton, born in 1642 in the Lincolnshire hamlet of Woolsthorpe, was an English mathematician and physicist whose work founded classical mechanics and ranks among the greatest achievements in the history of science. A solitary, intense student at Trinity College, Cambridge, he returned home during the plague years of 1665–66 and, in an extraordinary burst of creativity, laid the groundwork for the calculus, his theory of color, and his ideas on gravitation.

Encouraged and financed by the astronomer Edmond Halley, Newton set out his system in the Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica of 1687. There he stated the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, showing that the same force governs falling apples, the orbits of the planets, and the tides, and deriving Kepler's laws from first principles. It is among the most important books ever written.

In Opticks he analyzed light, using a prism to split white light into the spectrum, and he built the first practical reflecting telescope. His later years were marked by a bitter priority dispute with Gottfried Leibniz over the invention of calculus and an old feud with Robert Hooke. Privately he poured vast effort into alchemy and biblical chronology.

Newton also served the state, becoming Master of the Royal Mint, where he reformed the coinage and hunted counterfeiters, and long presided over the Royal Society. Knighted in 1705, he died in 1727 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. His mechanics reigned unchallenged until Albert Einstein, two centuries later, set it within a deeper theory.
