Mikhail Kutuzov

Russian field marshal (1745–1813)

Mikhail Kutuzov was the Russian commander who broke Napoleon's invasion of 1812 and became a national hero. He learned war in the service of Catherine the Great, fighting the Ottomans under the famed generalissimo who was his mentor, and surviving two terrible head wounds that cost him the use of an eye.

His reputation was mixed before his greatest hour. At Austerlitz in 1805 he had counselled caution, only to be overruled and defeated. But when Napoleon's Grande Armée poured into Russia in 1812, the aged, cunning Kutuzov was made commander-in-chief. He met the French in the colossal slaughter of Borodino, then made the agonising decision to surrender Moscow without another battle, declaring that the loss of the city was not the loss of Russia.

His patience was vindicated. As Napoleon lingered in the burning, empty capital and then began his retreat, Kutuzov's forces and the Russian winter annihilated the invading army. He was made Prince of Smolensk and pursued the French into Central Europe, where he died early in 1813.

His sovereign in these campaigns was the emperor Alexander I, and his lifelong adversary was Napoleon. Leo Tolstoy would later enshrine him in War and Peace as the very soul of Russian endurance and instinct. The brilliant general under whom he had first made his name was Alexander Suvorov.
