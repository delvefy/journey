Oda Nobunaga

First great unifier of Japan (1534–1582)

Oda Nobunaga, born in 1534, was the first of the three great unifiers of Japan and one of the most dynamic and ruthless figures in his nation's history. Inheriting a small domain in central Japan amid the chaos of the "Warring States" period, he rose through audacity and brilliance to dominate the country and shatter the old order.

His career announced itself in 1560 at the Battle of Okehazama, where with a tiny force he ambushed and destroyed a vastly larger invading army. From that triumph he expanded relentlessly, seizing the capital, Kyoto, and reducing rival warlords one by one. He embraced innovation as eagerly as war: he made devastating use of massed firearms, most famously at the Battle of Nagashino in 1575, and encouraged commerce, the tea ceremony, and the newly arrived Jesuit missionaries from Europe.

Nobunaga could be visionary and monstrous in equal measure. He broke the temporal power of the great Buddhist monasteries, notably burning the monks of Mount Hiei, and crushed all who stood in his way. He drew to his service two men of genius who would inherit his work: the brilliant commoner Toyotomi Hideyoshi and his steadfast ally Tokugawa Ieyasu.

His ascent ended in sudden betrayal. In 1582, resting at the temple of Honnō-ji in Kyoto, he was attacked by one of his own generals, Akechi Mitsuhide. Trapped and defeated, Nobunaga took his own life as the temple burned. He had unified perhaps a third of Japan and broken the back of the old feudal anarchy; Hideyoshi and Ieyasu would build the unified state upon the foundations he had so violently laid.
