Cardinal Richelieu

Chief minister of France (1585–1642)

Armand Jean du Plessis, Cardinal de Richelieu, born in 1585, was the architect of the modern French state. A young bishop who rose through the favour of the queen mother, he was raised to the cardinalate and in 1624 became chief minister to King Louis XIII, whom he served and dominated until his death. Known as the Red Eminence, he wielded the real power of the crown for nearly two decades.

His guiding aim was the supremacy of royal authority. He broke the independent military power of the Huguenots at the great siege of La Rochelle, crushed the conspiracies of the high nobility, banned private duels, and pulled down the fortresses from which over-mighty lords had defied the king. To his enemies he was a ruthless schemer—an image fixed forever in Alexandre Dumas's tale of the Musketeers—but he forged a centralised monarchy where none had stood before.

In foreign affairs he was the supreme practitioner of reason of state, subordinating religion to the interests of France. Though a prince of the Catholic Church, he set himself against the Catholic Habsburgs of Spain and Austria, subsidising the Protestant Gustavus Adolphus in the Thirty Years' War and at last bringing France openly into the conflict to shatter the Habsburg encirclement.

A patron of letters, he founded the Académie française to guard the French language. When he died in 1642 he bequeathed his power and his policies to his protégé Mazarin—and the absolute, centralised throne he had built passed, in time, to its most dazzling occupant, Louis XIV.
