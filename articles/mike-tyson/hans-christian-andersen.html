Hans Christian Andersen

Danish author of fairy tales (1805–1875)

Hans Christian Andersen was the Danish storyteller whose literary fairy tales became some of the most beloved and widely translated works in all of literature.

Born into bitter poverty in the town of Odense—his father a poor cobbler, his mother a washerwoman—he set out for Copenhagen at fourteen, dreaming of the stage. He failed as a performer, but charmed patrons who paid for his schooling, and he turned to writing travel books, novels and plays. Awkward, towering and painfully insecure, he poured his longing into his work, including an unrequited devotion to the famous singer Jenny Lind, who inspired his tale “The Nightingale” but loved him only as a brother.

From 1835 he began publishing the short tales that made him immortal: “The Little Mermaid,” “The Ugly Duckling,” “The Emperor's New Clothes,” “The Snow Queen,” “The Little Match Girl.” Unlike the folk tales gathered by the Brothers Grimm, these were original inventions, blending innocence, irony and a deep vein of melancholy. His scientist friend Hans Christian Ørsted assured him they, more than anything, would carry his name down the ages.

Celebrated across Europe, he travelled restlessly and counted famous admirers abroad, among them Charles Dickens, at whose English home he once outstayed his welcome by many weeks. He died in 1875, honoured as the pride of Denmark. His countryman and contemporary, the brooding philosopher whose very first book had been built around a sharp critique of one of Andersen's novels, was Søren Kierkegaard.
