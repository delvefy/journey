Solon

Athenian lawgiver and sage (c. 630–560 BC)

Solon was the statesman who set Athens on the long road toward democracy, and one of the Seven Sages whom Greece honored as the wisest of men. He rose to power around 594 BC, when the city was tearing itself apart: the poor, sinking ever deeper into debt, were being sold into slavery to their wealthy creditors, and revolution loomed. Granted sweeping authority to reform the state, Solon used it and then gave it back.

His great measure was the “shaking off of burdens,” which canceled the crushing debts, freed those who had been enslaved for them, and forbade the practice forever. He broke the monopoly of birth by ranking citizens according to wealth and opening office accordingly, created a council to prepare the people's business, and gave ordinary Athenians the right to sit in judgment and to appeal—institutions from which later democracy would grow.

Though he might have made himself tyrant, he refused, and once his laws were in place he left Athens to travel for ten years, that no one might pressure him to change them. A poet as well as a lawgiver, he defended his work in verse and declared that he grew old ever learning something new. After his death his own kinsman Peisistratus seized the tyranny he had declined.

Solon's name endured among the Seven Sages, that famous company of wise men whose maxims were inscribed at Delphi. The first of them, the Milesian thinker remembered as the father of philosophy, was Thales.
