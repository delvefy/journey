Luigi Galvani

Italian physician and physicist (1737–1798)

Luigi Galvani was born in Bologna in 1737 and trained in medicine, becoming professor of anatomy at the city's ancient university. A careful and devout man, he might have passed his life as a respected local physician but for a chance observation made while dissecting frogs.

In the 1780s he noticed that a frog's leg muscles twitched sharply when touched with metal instruments in the presence of electricity. After years of experiment he concluded, in his treatise of 1791, that living tissue itself held a kind of vital fluid he called animal electricity, set in motion through the nerves. The discovery caused a sensation across Europe and grew from the broader electrical science advanced by Benjamin Franklin.

It also provoked his celebrated quarrel with Alessandro Volta, who held that the electricity came not from the animal but from the contact of two different metals. The dispute drove Volta to invent the battery, while Galvani clung to his vital force; later science showed that both men had grasped a part of the truth, for the body does indeed run on electrical signals.

Galvani refused to swear loyalty to Napoleon's new Cisalpine Republic, was stripped of his chair, and died in poverty in 1798. His name survives in galvanism and the galvanometer, and his nephew Giovanni Aldini carried his experiments into a new and theatrical phase.
