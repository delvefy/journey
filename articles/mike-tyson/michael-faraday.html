Michael Faraday

English physicist and chemist (1791–1867)

Michael Faraday was born in London in 1791 to a poor blacksmith's family and had almost no formal schooling. Apprenticed to a bookbinder, he read the volumes he stitched and grew hungry for science. After attending the lectures of the great chemist Humphry Davy, he sent Davy a careful bound copy of his notes and was taken on as his laboratory assistant at the Royal Institution, where he would spend his life.

As a chemist he discovered benzene, was the first to liquefy chlorine and other gases, and established the laws of electrolysis, coining much of its vocabulary—electrode, ion, anode, cathode. But his greatest discoveries lay in electromagnetism. In 1831 he found that a changing magnetic field induces an electric current, the principle of the electric generator and transformer, and he built one of the first electric motors.

Lacking mathematics, Faraday thought in pictures, imagining space filled with lines of force and fields—an idea that James Clerk Maxwell would later translate into exact equations. A devout and modest man, he declined a knighthood and a grave in Westminster Abbey, and delighted audiences with his Christmas Lectures and his talk on the chemistry of a candle.

He died in 1867. So profound was his insight into the electromagnetic field that Albert Einstein kept his portrait on the study wall, and the electrical machines of Nikola Tesla and the modern world all descend from his discovery of induction.
