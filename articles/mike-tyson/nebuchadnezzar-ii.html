Nebuchadnezzar II

King of Babylon (reigned 605–562 BC)

Nebuchadnezzar II raised the Neo-Babylonian empire to its height and made his capital the wonder of the ancient world. The son of the king who had joined the Medes to destroy mighty Assyria, he proved his soldiership while still crown prince by crushing the Egyptians at Carchemish, a victory that left Babylon master of the lands between the rivers and the sea.

Westward his armies pressed against the small kingdom of Judah. When it defied him he came twice against Jerusalem, deporting its young king in the first assault and, after a later revolt, storming the city, burning Solomon's Temple, and carrying its people into the long sorrow of the Babylonian Exile. The destruction of Jerusalem and the captivity of the Jews are among the most fateful events of biblical history, and they were his work.

At home he was a builder without equal. He girded Babylon with vast walls, raised the brilliant blue Ishtar Gate and its processional way, restored the towering ziggurat that legend remembered as the Tower of Babel, and—so the Greeks believed—laid out the Hanging Gardens, a green mountain of terraces said to have been made to comfort his foreign queen.

To later ages he became a figure of both glory and warning, and the scriptures told of his prophetic dreams and a season of madness in which he grazed like a beast until his reason returned. The exiled Judean youth who, by the biblical account, rose in his court to read those dreams and counsel the king was Daniel.
