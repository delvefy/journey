Christiaan Huygens

Dutch mathematician, physicist and astronomer (1629–1695)

Christiaan Huygens, born at The Hague in 1629 into a distinguished family—his father Constantijn was a poet and diplomat and a friend of René Descartes—was the foremost natural philosopher of the generation between Galileo Galilei and Isaac Newton. Grinding his own superior lenses, he turned an improved telescope on Saturn and in 1655 discovered its great moon Titan, then explained the planet's puzzling appearance as a thin, flat ring, settling a question that had baffled earlier observers.

In 1657 he invented the pendulum clock, applying Galileo's insight about the regularity of a swinging weight to transform the measurement of time; for decades his clocks were the most accurate in the world, and he pursued the dream of a marine timekeeper to solve the problem of longitude at sea. He also wrote the first printed treatise on the mathematics of probability, building directly on the work of Blaise Pascal.

His greatest theoretical achievement was the wave theory of light, set out in his Treatise on Light. The principle that bears his name—that every point on a wave front is itself a source of new wavelets—allowed him to explain reflection and refraction, and stood in direct opposition to the corpuscular theory championed by Newton. He also analysed collisions and the force of circular motion.

A founding member of the French Académie des sciences, Huygens lived for years in Paris, where he tutored the young Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in mathematics. Elected to the Royal Society in London, he corresponded and contended with its leading lights, among them the restless and inventive Robert Hooke.
