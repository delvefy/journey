Jacques-Louis David

French painter, master of Neoclassicism (1748–1825)

Jacques-Louis David was the supreme painter of the French Revolution and the Napoleonic age, the artist who gave Neoclassicism its stern, heroic grandeur. Born in Paris and trained in the academic tradition, he won the coveted Prix de Rome and steeped himself in the art of antiquity. His breakthrough came with the Oath of the Horatii in 1784, a picture of Roman virtue and self-sacrifice whose austere lines and moral seriousness made it a manifesto for a new age.

David threw himself into the Revolution as fiercely as he painted. Elected to the National Convention, he voted for the death of the king, allied himself with Robespierre, and staged the great revolutionary festivals. When his friend Jean-Paul Marat was murdered in his bath, David transformed the scene into The Death of Marat, a secular pietà that remains one of the most powerful political images ever painted. The fall of Robespierre briefly landed David in prison.

He recovered to become the official painter of a new master, Napoleon Bonaparte, glorifying the emperor in vast canvases such as Napoleon Crossing the Alps and the enormous Coronation. As director of the arts he ruled French painting almost single-handedly and trained a whole generation of artists in his studio.

With Napoleon's final defeat David went into exile in Brussels, where he died in 1825. His legacy passed to his many pupils, among whom the boldest and most original—the standard-bearer of the Romantic generation that would rebel against his cold perfection—was Eugène Delacroix.
