Antoine Lavoisier

French chemist, father of modern chemistry (1743–1794)

Antoine Lavoisier was born in Paris in 1743 into a wealthy family, trained in the law, but turned his fortune and his exacting mind to science. Elected young to the Academy of Sciences, he funded his costly experiments in part through his shares in the Ferme Générale, the private company that collected royal taxes—a connection that would later cost him his life.

Lavoisier led the chemical revolution that overthrew the old phlogiston theory. By careful measurement he showed that combustion and respiration are processes of combination with a gas he named oxygen, drew hydrogen and oxygen into a new understanding of water, and established the principle that mass is conserved in chemical change. With collaborators he devised the systematic chemical nomenclature still used today and set it out in his Elementary Treatise on Chemistry of 1789. His advances built upon and competed with the discoveries of Joseph Priestley.

He worked with Pierre-Simon Laplace on the measurement of heat using an ice calorimeter, and his wife, Marie-Anne, translated foreign chemists and illustrated his books. He also served the state in the gunpowder administration and on the commission that helped create the metric system, and joined Benjamin Franklin on the royal panel that investigated and debunked Mesmer's animal magnetism.

His wealth and his role as a tax farmer made him a target in the Revolution. Denounced by radicals such as Jean-Paul Marat, he was tried and guillotined in 1794; the mathematician Lagrange remarked that it took only an instant to cut off his head, but a century might not produce another like it.
