Nathaniel Hawthorne

American novelist (1804–1864)

Nathaniel Hawthorne was born in Salem, Massachusetts, in 1804, a descendant of a stern Puritan judge of the Salem witch trials—an ancestry whose shadow he felt so keenly that he added a letter to the family name to set himself apart from it. At Bowdoin College he formed friendships that lasted his life, among his classmates the poet Longfellow and the future president Franklin Pierce.

He began with brooding short tales such as “Young Goodman Brown,” steeped in sin, guilt, and the hidden corruption of the human heart, a vein of dark Romanticism that set him apart from the sunnier Transcendentalists. In 1850 came his masterpiece, The Scarlet Letter, the tale of Hester Prynne branded for adultery in Puritan Boston, followed by The House of the Seven Gables and other romances.

Hawthorne lived for a time in Concord among Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau, though he kept a certain ironic distance from their optimism. His deepest literary friendship was with Herman Melville, his younger neighbor in the Berkshires, who poured out his admiration in letters and dedicated Moby-Dick to him.

When Franklin Pierce won the presidency, the biography Hawthorne wrote for his campaign was rewarded with a consulship in Liverpool. He died in 1864, and a generation later Henry James honored him with a full critical life.
