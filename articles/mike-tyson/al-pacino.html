Al Pacino

American actor of the New Hollywood (born 1940)

Al Pacino, a New Yorker of Italian descent trained in the Method at the Actors Studio, became one of the defining stars of the American cinema's great renaissance in the 1970s. His breakthrough was the role of Michael Corleone in The Godfather and its sequels—the chilling slow transformation of a reluctant war hero into a ruthless crime lord, played opposite the Don of Marlon Brando, from whom the family and the saga pass to him.

Through that decade he gave a run of electric performances: the honest cop of Serpico, the desperate, doomed robber of Dog Day Afternoon, the furious lawyer of And Justice for All. In 1983 he created the cocaine kingpin Tony Montana of Scarface, a role that grew into a cult of its own, and in 1992 he finally won the Academy Award for Best Actor as the blind, raging colonel of Scent of a Woman.

Famed for the volcanic intensity he could unleash on screen, he was also a serious man of the theatre, returning often to the stage and to his beloved Shakespeare.

For decades critics and fans paired him with one other actor as the two supreme talents of their generation—rivals in reputation who finally shared the screen in the crime epic Heat. That other titan was Robert De Niro.
