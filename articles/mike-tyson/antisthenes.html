Antisthenes

Greek philosopher, founder of the Cynics (c. 446–366 BC)

Antisthenes began as a student of the great rhetorician Gorgias, but on hearing Socrates he abandoned the art of fine speaking and gave himself wholly to philosophy, becoming one of the master's most devoted companions and standing by him at his death. From Socrates he took the lesson that virtue alone matters, and he pressed it to a severe extreme.

Virtue, Antisthenes taught, is the only good and is enough by itself for happiness; it shows in deeds, not words, and once won it cannot be lost. Everything else—wealth, reputation, comfort, pleasure—he despised as worthless or worse, declaring that he would rather be mad than feel pleasure. He scorned social convention and lived in studied poverty, founding by his example the tradition of the Cynics, the “dog-philosophers” who flouted the world's opinions.

His austerity went with a sharp and combative mind. He quarreled with Plato and mocked his theory of eternal Forms, saying that he could see a horse well enough but could nowhere see “horseness.” His many dialogues, now lost, ranged over ethics, language, and the figures of his day.

His greatest legacy was a pupil who carried his doctrine to its outrageous limit, the beggar-philosopher Diogenes, and through that line the Cynic spirit passed into Stoicism. Yet another follower of Socrates took the contrary path, holding that pleasure, not its renunciation, is the aim of life—the founder of the Cyrenaic school, Aristippus.
