Pico della Mirandola

Italian Renaissance philosopher (1463–1494)

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola was born into the small ruling house of Mirandola and Concordia and chose learning over his inheritance. A prodigy who studied at Bologna, Ferrara, Padua, and Paris, he mastered Latin and Greek and, unusually, Hebrew and Arabic, intending to gather all human wisdom into a single harmony. He believed that Plato and Aristotle, the scholastics and the Arabic commentators, the Hebrew Kabbalah and the Hermetic texts, all pointed toward one underlying truth.

In 1486 the young count published nine hundred theses drawn from every tradition he knew and offered to defend them in public disputation in Rome, prefacing them with the famous oration later titled On the Dignity of Man. There he imagined God telling Adam that he alone among creatures had no fixed nature, but could shape himself by free will toward the beasts or toward the divine—a declaration often read as the manifesto of Renaissance humanism.

The disputation never took place. Pope Innocent VIII appointed a commission that condemned several theses, and Pico, after writing a defiant Apologia, was briefly detained in France. Lorenzo de' Medici secured his return and settled him near Florence, where he joined the brilliant circle of humanists around the Medici court. In 1493 Pope Alexander VI absolved him.

In his last years Pico fell under the spell of the preacher Savonarola and turned toward penance, planning to give away his wealth. He died in 1494, only thirty-one, and was buried in the Dominican habit; centuries later his bones showed traces of arsenic. The friend and elder Platonist who had translated Plato into Latin and shaped Pico's whole philosophy was Marsilio Ficino.
