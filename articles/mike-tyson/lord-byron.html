Lord Byron

English Romantic poet (1788–1824)

George Gordon, the sixth Baron Byron, was the most celebrated and scandalous poet of the Romantic age, a man whose life became as famous as his verse. Born in London and lame from birth, he unexpectedly inherited his title as a boy and was educated at Harrow and Cambridge. In 1812 the first cantos of Childe Harold's Pilgrimage made him an overnight sensation—"I awoke one morning," he said, "and found myself famous."

For a few dazzling years Byron was the lion of London society, pursued and gossiped about, branded by one lover "mad, bad, and dangerous to know." He created in his poetry a new kind of figure—the brooding, defiant, guilt-haunted "Byronic hero"—and poured out work after work: The Corsair, Manfred, and the sparkling, satirical epic Don Juan. He revered Milton's Satan and worshipped the conquering genius of Napoleon.

Scandal—a broken marriage, rumors of incest, mounting debts—drove him from England forever in 1816. On Lake Geneva he summered with Percy and Mary Shelley, in the storm-bound house where Frankenstein was born, then settled in Italy to write and to love.

Restless to the end, Byron gave his fortune and finally his life to the cause of Greek independence, dying of fever at Missolonghi in 1824 at the age of thirty-six. Mourned as a hero in Greece and across Europe, he remained for the rest of the century the very image of the Romantic poet. The earlier English master whose grand verse and rebel angel haunted his imagination was John Milton.
