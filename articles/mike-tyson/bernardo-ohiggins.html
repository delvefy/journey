Bernardo O'Higgins

Chilean independence leader and first head of state (1778–1842)

Bernardo O'Higgins was the principal founder of independent Chile. The illegitimate son of Ambrosio O'Higgins, an Irish-born officer who rose to be a Spanish viceroy, he was educated in Peru and then in England. In London the young Chilean fell under the influence of the great precursor of Spanish American independence, who fired his commitment to the cause and the secret patriot lodges.

Back in Chile, O'Higgins threw himself into the revolution, surviving early defeats and the bitter rivalry of other patriot leaders. His fortunes turned when he joined forces with José de San Martín: together they brought the Army of the Andes over the mountains and crushed the royalists at Chacabuco in 1817 and decisively at Maipú the following year, sealing Chilean independence.

As Supreme Director from 1817, O'Higgins proclaimed the new republic and pressed ambitious reforms, abolishing aristocratic titles and founding national institutions. But his autocratic manner and his challenges to the landed elite and the Church turned the powerful against him, and in 1823 he was compelled to resign.

He spent his last nineteen years in exile in Peru, honoured with estates and ever ready to serve the wider cause—he offered himself to the campaigns of Simón Bolívar. He died at Lima in 1842, revered as the father of his nation. The mentor who had first set him on the patriot path was Francisco de Miranda.
