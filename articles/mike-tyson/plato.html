Plato

Greek philosopher (c. 428–348 BC)

Plato, born around 428 BC into an aristocratic Athenian family, was a philosopher whose work lies at the foundation of the entire Western intellectual tradition. As a young man he became a devoted follower of Socrates, and the execution of his master by the Athenian democracy in 399 BC marked him for life, turning him away from a political career and toward philosophy.

After years of travel, including fraught involvement with the rulers of Syracuse in Sicily, Plato founded the Academy in Athens around 387 BC, an institution of philosophy and mathematics often called the first university of the Western world. There he taught and wrote until his death.

Plato cast his philosophy as dialogues, most starring Socrates as the questioner. In works such as the Apology, Phaedo, Symposium, and above all the Republic, he explored justice, love, knowledge, and the ideal state governed by philosopher-kings. His Theory of Forms held that the changing things we perceive are mere shadows of eternal, perfect realities—an idea he illustrated with the famous allegory of the cave. He argued for the immortality of the soul and the unity of virtue and knowledge.

His pupil Aristotle would challenge and transform much of his teaching, yet Plato's questions and concepts have shaped philosophy ever since; a modern thinker quipped that all later philosophy is but "footnotes to Plato." He died around 348 BC, leaving the Academy and a body of work studied without interruption for nearly twenty-four centuries.
