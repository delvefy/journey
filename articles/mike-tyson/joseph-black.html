Joseph Black

Scottish chemist and physicist (1728–1799)

Joseph Black was born in 1728 to a Scottish family settled in Bordeaux, and was sent to study at Glasgow and Edinburgh, where he trained in medicine and chemistry under William Cullen. He went on to hold the chairs of chemistry first at Glasgow and then at Edinburgh, becoming one of the most admired lecturers of the Scottish Enlightenment.

His experiments on magnesia and limestone led him to identify a distinct gas, which he called fixed air and which is now known as carbon dioxide. By showing that this air could be weighed, released, and reabsorbed, he opened the quantitative study of gases that would feed into the chemical revolution led by Antoine Lavoisier.

Black's other great contribution lay in the study of heat. He distinguished temperature from quantity of heat, and discovered latent heat—the heat absorbed or given out when a substance melts or boils without changing temperature—and specific heat. These ideas proved crucial to his friend and colleague James Watt, helping him grasp why the steam engine wasted so much energy and how it might be improved.

A close companion of James Hutton and Adam Smith, whose estate he helped settle, Black was a central figure in Edinburgh's intellectual life. He died in 1799, his careful, measuring spirit having helped turn chemistry into an exact science, much as his contemporary Henry Cavendish was doing in England.
