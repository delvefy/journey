Luca Pacioli

Italian friar and mathematician (c. 1447–1517)

Luca Pacioli, a Franciscan friar from the Tuscan town of Borgo San Sepolcro, became the great teacher of mathematics to the Renaissance. Schooled in his youth by his townsman Piero della Francesca, he spent his life wandering from city to city—Venice, Perugia, Milan, Florence, Rome—lecturing on geometry and the practical arithmetic of merchants.

In 1494 he printed his vast Summa, a survey of all the mathematics then known. Buried within it was a short section that made him immortal in an unexpected field: the first published account of double-entry bookkeeping, the Venetian method of balancing debits against credits, which earned him the title of father of accounting and shaped commerce for five centuries.

At Milan, in the service of Ludovico Sforza, Pacioli formed a famous friendship with Leonardo da Vinci, to whom he taught mathematics and proportion. When Pacioli wrote On the Divine Proportion, his study of the golden ratio and the regular solids, it was Leonardo who drew the exquisite polyhedra that illustrate it. Pacioli also issued a printed edition of Euclid, carrying the ancient geometer to a new age of readers.

For all his learning he was dogged by the charge that he had borrowed too freely from others, especially from the mathematical writings of his old master, whose work on the five perfect solids reappeared in his own. That master—the painter whose serene, geometric pictures are among the glories of the early Renaissance—was Piero della Francesca.
