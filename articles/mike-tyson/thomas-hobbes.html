Thomas Hobbes

English philosopher of politics (1588–1679)

Thomas Hobbes, born in 1588 as the Spanish Armada approached England—he later joked that his mother "brought forth twins, myself and fear"—became the founder of modern political philosophy. Educated at Oxford and long employed as tutor to the noble Cavendish family, he travelled widely on the Continent, where he met Galileo Galilei in Florence and embraced a thoroughgoing materialism: the conviction that the world, and even thought itself, is nothing but matter in motion.

His earliest major work was a translation of the Greek historian Thucydides, whose distrust of democracy he shared. In Paris he joined the scientific circle around Mersenne and composed objections to the Meditations of René Descartes, with whom he sparred over the nature of mind and body. When civil war broke out in England he fled to France and there tutored the exiled prince who would become Charles II.

In 1651 he published Leviathan, his enduring masterpiece. Imagining human beings in a "state of nature" without government, he argued that life there would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," a war of all against all. To escape it, men covenant together to surrender their rights to a single sovereign power whose absolute authority alone can secure peace. The argument founded social-contract theory even as it justified absolute rule.

Suspected of atheism and embroiled in quarrels—with the chemist Robert Boyle over the existence of the vacuum, and with mathematicians over his claim to have squared the circle—Hobbes nonetheless lived to ninety-one. His grim vision provoked the very different social contract of John Locke.
