Francisco Goya

Spanish painter and printmaker (1746–1828)

Francisco José de Goya y Lucientes bridged the age of the Old Masters and the modern world, beginning as a decorous court painter and ending as the visionary chronicler of cruelty, folly, and nightmare. Born in the Aragonese village of Fuendetodos, he rose through the design of tapestry cartoons to become first court painter to Charles IV, whose family he portrayed with merciless candour.

A grave illness around 1793 left him stone deaf, and his art grew darker and more inward. In the etched series Los Caprichos he lashed superstition and vice, framing them with the famous caption that the sleep of reason produces monsters. He named his masters as Velázquez, Rembrandt, and nature itself.

Napoleon's invasion of Spain gave him his most searing subjects. The Third of May 1808 shows a faceless firing squad executing helpless Madrileños, and the private etchings of The Disasters of War record atrocity without consolation. Amid the same upheaval he painted a portrait of the British commander, the Duke of Wellington, who had entered Madrid in 1812.

In his last Spanish years, isolated and embittered, he covered the walls of his house with the haunted Black Paintings, including the devouring Saturn. Fleeing the repression of Ferdinand VII, he settled in Bordeaux and died there in 1828. His shadow fell across modern art—on his countryman Pablo Picasso, and on the French painter who took up his frank realism, Édouard Manet.
