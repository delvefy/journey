René Descartes

French philosopher and mathematician (1596–1650)

René Descartes, born in 1596 in the Touraine region of France and educated by the Jesuits at the college of La Flèche, is commonly called the father of modern philosophy. As a young man he travelled and served in the armies of the Thirty Years' War; by his own account, a day of solitary reflection in a stove-heated room in 1619, followed by three vivid dreams, set him on the search for a single method that could unify all the sciences on certain foundations.

That ambition produced his Discourse on the Method (1637) and the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641). Resolving to doubt everything that could be doubted, Descartes found one thing he could not deny—that he was thinking—and so reached his famous first principle, "I think, therefore I am" (cogito, ergo sum). From this fixed point he rebuilt knowledge, arguing for the existence of God and for a sharp division between mind and body that would frame debate for centuries. In doing so he turned decisively away from the Scholastic Aristotelianism in which he had been schooled.

Descartes was also a mathematician of the first rank. He fused algebra with geometry to create analytic geometry, and the Cartesian coordinate system still bears his name. His mechanistic physics, including a theory of planetary vortices, dominated the Continent until it was overturned by the work of Isaac Newton. Wary of the fate of his contemporary Galileo Galilei, he settled in the tolerant Dutch Republic to think and write in freedom.

In 1649 Queen Christina of Sweden summoned him to Stockholm; the harsh winter and pre-dawn philosophy lessons broke his health, and he died of pneumonia in 1650. His rationalism shaped Spinoza and Leibniz and provoked the later critiques of Immanuel Kant, leaving "Cartesian" a permanent word in the language of thought.
