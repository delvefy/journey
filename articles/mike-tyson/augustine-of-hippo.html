Augustine of Hippo

Christian theologian and Church Father (354–430)

Augustine, born in 354 in the North African town of Thagaste, was the most influential theologian of the early Christian Church and one of the towering minds of late antiquity. Son of a devout Christian mother, Monica, and a pagan father, he trained as a teacher of rhetoric and pursued worldly ambition and pleasure—he later prayed, with rueful honesty, "Lord, make me chaste, but not yet." For years he followed the dualist Manichaean sect and then the philosophy of the Neoplatonists.

Teaching in Milan, he fell under the influence of its bishop, Ambrose, and the writings of the Platonists prepared his mind for faith. His celebrated conversion in a garden in 386, prompted by a child's voice chanting "take up and read," led him to Scripture and to baptism the next year.

Returning to Africa, Augustine became bishop of Hippo Regius and poured out a vast body of writing. His Confessions, an intimate account of his sins and his turn to God, founded the tradition of spiritual autobiography. His City of God, written after the sack of Rome in 410, framed a Christian understanding of history. He defended orthodox doctrine against the Manichaeans, Donatists, and Pelagians, hammering out enduring teachings on grace, original sin, and the will.

Augustine died in 430 as the Vandals besieged his city. His thought shaped medieval Christianity and, a thousand years later, the Reformers Luther and Calvin alike, making him a father of both Catholic and Protestant theology.
