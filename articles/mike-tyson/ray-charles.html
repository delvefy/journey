Ray Charles

American musician, “the Genius” (1930–2004)

Ray Charles was the blind American musician who all but invented soul music, fusing the fervor of gospel with rhythm and blues, jazz and country into something wholly new.

Born in rural Georgia and blind from the age of seven, orphaned young and trained at a school for the blind, he turned hardship into an art of astonishing range. With recordings like “I Got a Woman,” “What'd I Say,” “Georgia on My Mind” and “Hit the Road Jack” he broke down the walls between sacred and secular music, and his country-music album of 1962 shocked and delighted the world by crossing yet another boundary. Lifelong friends like the producer Quincy Jones, whom he had met as a teenager in Seattle, marvelled at his gifts.

He won seventeen Grammys and was simply called “the Genius”—a title endorsed by Frank Sinatra, who pronounced him the only true genius in show business. He overcame a long heroin addiction, and his story reached new audiences through an acclaimed film after his death in 2004. He shared the stage and the soul tradition with the reigning queen of the music, Aretha Franklin.

The blind musical genius who inherited his mantle in the next generation—another boundless talent who turned the joining of genres into pop magic—was Stevie Wonder.
