Charlemagne

King of the Franks, first Holy Roman Emperor (c. 747–814)

Charlemagne, or Charles the Great, born around 747, was king of the Franks, conqueror of much of Western Europe, and the first emperor in the West since the fall of Rome—a figure so towering that he is remembered as the "Father of Europe." The son of Pepin the Short and grandson of Charles Martel, the victor of Tours, he inherited the rising power of the Carolingian dynasty and made it the dominant force of the early Middle Ages.

In a reign of more than forty years, Charlemagne waged near-constant war. He conquered the Lombard kingdom of Italy, subdued and forcibly Christianised the pagan Saxons in decades of brutal campaigns, crushed the Avars of the Danube, and pushed his frontiers across central Europe. A famous rearguard disaster in the Pyrenees, the death of his commander Roland at Roncevaux, would later be immortalised in the great epic the Song of Roland.

On Christmas Day in the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned him "Emperor of the Romans" in St Peter's, reviving the Western imperial title and binding the Frankish monarchy to the Roman Church—an act whose echoes would reach down through Otto the Great to the Holy Roman Empire. His prestige was such that the distant Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid exchanged embassies and gifts with him, including a celebrated elephant.

Charlemagne was more than a warrior. He gathered scholars to his court, promoted learning and the copying of manuscripts, and reformed script and education in the movement known as the Carolingian Renaissance. He ruled from Aachen and was buried there in 814. His empire soon fragmented among his grandsons, but his memory endured as the model of Christian kingship—emulated by emperors and, a thousand years later, by Napoleon himself.
