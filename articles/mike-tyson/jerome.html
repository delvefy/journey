Jerome

Church Father and translator of the Bible (c. 342–420)

Jerome was the greatest biblical scholar of the early Church and the author of the Latin Bible that shaped Western Christianity for more than a thousand years. Born in the Roman province of Dalmatia and educated in Rome, he combined deep classical learning with fierce religious zeal. After a spell as a hermit in the Syrian desert, where he mastered Hebrew, he served as secretary to Pope Damasus, who set him the task that would define his life.

Settling at last in a monastery at Bethlehem, Jerome translated the scriptures afresh into Latin, working directly from the original Hebrew and Greek. The result, later known as the Vulgate, became the standard Bible of the Western Church and one of the most influential books in history. Around this labor he produced a vast body of commentaries, letters, and learned controversy.

He was as combative as he was brilliant. He quarreled bitterly with former friends over the orthodoxy of Origen, whose scholarship he had once revered, and exchanged sharp letters with his great contemporary Augustine of Hippo. His irritable genius and ascetic devotion made him a legend; in art he is shown with his books, a skull, and a faithful lion.

Honored ever after as one of the four great Latin Doctors of the Church, Jerome died at Bethlehem in 420. Among the towering churchmen of his own generation, the bishop of Milan who humbled emperors and won souls by his preaching was Ambrose.
