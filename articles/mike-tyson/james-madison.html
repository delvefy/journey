James Madison

Fourth President of the United States, Father of the Constitution (1751–1836)

James Madison was the chief architect of the United States Constitution and the fourth president. A slight, scholarly Virginian of immense learning, he came to the Constitutional Convention of 1787 with a plan of government already worked out, steeped in the political philosophy of Montesquieu and his theory of the separation of powers. His Virginia Plan framed the debates, and the detailed notes he kept remain the great record of the Convention.

To win ratification he joined Alexander Hamilton in writing The Federalist Papers, contributing the famous essays on faction and on checks and balances, and in the first Congress he drafted the Bill of Rights. Yet he soon broke with Hamilton's vision, and with his closest friend Thomas Jefferson he founded the opposition party that would dominate the early republic. He had also been a trusted ally of George Washington in the new nation's founding.

As Jefferson's Secretary of State and then as president, he guided the country into the War of 1812 against Britain—a conflict of early humiliations, including the burning of Washington, redeemed by a late surge of national pride.

The last of the Founding Fathers, he retired to his Montpelier estate and died in 1836. The war he led had been won in part by Jean Lafitte's guns at New Orleans, and his chosen successor in the presidency was his fellow Virginian James Monroe.
