Christopher Wren

English architect and scientist (1632–1723)

Christopher Wren was born in Wiltshire in 1632 and first made his name not as an architect but as a scientist. A dazzling student at Oxford, he became professor of astronomy while still young, pursued mathematics, anatomy and optics, and stood among the founders of the Royal Society, of which he later served as president. He worked closely with Robert Hooke and was esteemed by Isaac Newton, who credited him among those who had grasped the laws of planetary motion.

In the 1660s he turned increasingly to building. A journey to Paris brought him into contact with the great Gian Lorenzo Bernini and the splendours of continental design, and he absorbed the classical manner that Inigo Jones had first brought to England.

The Great Fire of London in 1666 gave him his life's work. Appointed to oversee the rebuilding, he designed more than fifty city churches and, crowning them all, the vast new St Paul's Cathedral, raised between 1675 and 1710, with its majestic dome dominating the skyline. He added the Royal Hospitals at Chelsea and Greenwich, the Sheldonian Theatre and much more.

Knighted and laden with honours as Surveyor of the King's Works under Charles II, Wren lived to the age of ninety and was buried in his own cathedral. His epitaph there invites the visitor, if he seeks the architect's monument, simply to look around him.
