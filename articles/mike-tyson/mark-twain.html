Mark Twain

American writer and humorist (1835–1910)

Mark Twain—the pen name of Samuel Langhorne Clemens, taken from a riverboat call—is often honoured as the father of American literature. Raised in the river town of Hannibal, Missouri, he tried his hand as a printer, a Mississippi steamboat pilot, a silver miner, and a frontier journalist before a comic tale of a jumping frog brought him sudden fame.

From the world of his boyhood he drew his masterpieces, The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, the latter written in the living American vernacular and reaching, through the friendship of a white boy and a runaway slave, a depth of moral seriousness beneath its humour that later writers judged the wellspring of all modern American fiction. In a stream of other books and in his razor-sharp lectures he satirised greed, cruelty, racism, and empire, and gave his era its name when he called it the Gilded Age.

The most celebrated American of his day, Twain moved among the great. His publishing house brought out the memoirs of his friend Ulysses S. Grant, and his own fortunes swung wildly between wealth and ruin, deepened by private grief. Born under Halley's Comet, he foretold he would "go out with it," and died as it returned in 1910.

Endlessly curious about the new age of machines, Twain became a close friend of the electrical genius of the day, often visiting his laboratory—the inventor Nikola Tesla.
