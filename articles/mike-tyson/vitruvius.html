Vitruvius

Roman architect and engineer (1st century BC)

Marcus Vitruvius Pollio is little known as a man but immortal as an author, for he wrote the only treatise on architecture to survive from the ancient world. By his own account he served as a military engineer, building and maintaining war machines—ballistae and siege engines—during the campaigns of Julius Caesar, and lived on into the long peace that followed.

In old age he composed On Architecture, ten books dedicated to the emperor Augustus, who, Vitruvius gratefully records, granted him a pension that freed him to write. The work ranges far beyond temples and palaces: it treats town planning, the training of the architect, building materials, the orders of columns, water supply and aqueducts, sundials, and machines, preserving a whole technical civilization in a single text.

From it comes the famous formula that every building must possess firmitas, utilitas, venustas—firmness, usefulness, and beauty. From it too comes the idea that the proportions of a well-made building should mirror the proportions of the human body, the man inscribed in circle and square that Leonardo da Vinci would later draw and make the most famous image of Renaissance science.

For centuries the manuscript slept, until humanists rediscovered it in 1414 and made it the indispensable authority for every architect of the rebirth—for Alberti, for Raphael at St. Peter's, and most faithfully of all for the man who translated its principles into a living style and a book of his own, the Venetian master Andrea Palladio.
