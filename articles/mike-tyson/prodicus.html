Prodicus

Greek Sophist of Ceos (c. 465–c. 395 BC)

Prodicus, born on the small island of Ceos, was among the most respected of the Sophists who flocked to Athens in its golden age. He came first as an envoy for his native city and stayed to teach, charging steep fees for his lessons in language and persuasion, and growing rich on the appetite of young Athenians for the new learning.

He is remembered above all for a single moral fable, the Choice of Heracles, in which the young hero, at a crossroads, is approached by two women—Virtue, plain and earnest, and Vice, soft and alluring—and chooses the steep, hard road of Virtue. The story so charmed antiquity that Xenophon preserved it, and it has echoed through art and literature ever since.

Prodicus was also a careful student of words, insisting on fine distinctions between near-synonyms in a way that anticipated later semantics, and he offered a strikingly naturalistic account of religion, suggesting that men had first worshipped the sun, rivers, and other useful things, and then the mortals who discovered them.

Socrates spoke of him with affectionate irony and even claimed to have been his pupil, while Plato gave him a comic turn, deep-voiced and self-important, in his dialogues; tradition counted the tragedian Euripides among those he taught. Another of the great wandering Sophists of the day, a self-styled master of every art who boasted he had made with his own hands everything he wore, was Hippias of Elis.
