Gertrude Stein

American writer and art collector (1874–1946)

Gertrude Stein was the American writer and collector whose Paris salon became the crucible of literary and artistic modernism.

She studied psychology under William James at Radcliffe—an education that shaped her experiments with repetition and the stream of consciousness—before settling in Paris in 1903. There, with her brother, she assembled one of the earliest great collections of modern art, buying the radical canvases of Paul Cézanne and Henri Matisse when almost no one else would, and forming a deep friendship with Pablo Picasso, who painted her famously masklike portrait.

Her apartment at 27 rue de Fleurus, its walls crowded with masterpieces, drew the whole avant-garde, and after the war it drew the young American writers too. It was Stein who told Ernest Hemingway that his was a “lost generation,” and who took the measure of newcomers like F. Scott Fitzgerald. Her own writing was wilfully experimental—“a rose is a rose is a rose”—though her greatest popular success came with a memoir cast as the autobiography of her lifelong companion, Alice B. Toklas.

She presided over modern art and letters for four decades until her death in 1946. Among the painters of her circle, the artist who with Picasso invented Cubism—the movement she did so much to champion—was Georges Braque.
