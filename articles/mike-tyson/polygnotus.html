Polygnotus

Greek painter of Thasos (5th century BC)

Polygnotus, born on the island of Thasos, was reckoned by the Greeks the first great painter of their classical age. Drawn to Athens in the years of its rising glory, he attached himself to the circle of the statesman Cimon and was honored with Athenian citizenship—a rare distinction that marked the esteem in which his art was held.

He worked on a grand public scale, covering the walls of porticoes and shrines with crowded scenes of myth and history. In the Painted Porch of Athens he set the Battle of Marathon among legendary combats, and at Delphi, in the clubhouse of the Cnidians, he painted his two acknowledged masterpieces: the sack of Troy and the visit of Odysseus to the land of the dead, vast compositions whose every figure the traveler Pausanias later described.

His innovations changed the art. He gave his figures a new inwardness and nobility of character, lightened and varied their drapery, and arranged them at different levels across the field, feeling his way toward the representation of depth. Aristotle would hold him up as the painter who showed men better than they are.

Not a line of his work survives; it is known only through the words of those who saw it. By tradition his heart was given to a celebrated Athenian noblewoman, sister of his patron Cimon, whose face he is said to have painted among the captive women of Troy at Delphi—Elpinice.
