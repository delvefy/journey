Maurice Ravel

French composer (1875–1937)

Maurice Ravel was, with Debussy, the supreme master of French music in the early twentieth century, though he prized clarity and precision so highly that Stravinsky called him the most perfect of Swiss watchmakers.

A peerless orchestrator, he wrote music of jewelled exactness and sensuous colour. His best-known piece, the hypnotic Boléro, grew from a single repeated theme into an orchestral juggernaut—a popularity that half-embarrassed him. He also gave the world the radiant ballet Daphnis et Chloé, the wistful Pavane pour une infante défunte, two dazzling piano concertos, and a famous orchestration of Mussorgsky's Pictures at an Exhibition.

He drew on Spanish rhythms, on the new sounds of jazz, and on the poise of older French forms. When the young American George Gershwin asked to study with him, Ravel is said to have refused, telling him not to become a second-rate Ravel when he could be a first-rate Gershwin.

He was a contemporary and occasional collaborator of Stravinsky, and the lifelong companion-rival of Debussy in the story of French music. Among the admirers he gently turned away was George Gershwin.
