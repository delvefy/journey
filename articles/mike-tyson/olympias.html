Olympias

Queen of Macedon, mother of Alexander the Great (c. 375–316 BC)

Olympias was a princess of the royal house of Epirus, which traced its descent from Achilles, and she brought that fierce heroic pride to the court of Macedon when she married King Philip II. Their union produced Alexander the Great, and Olympias devoted herself utterly to her son, nurturing in him the belief that he was born for more than mortal greatness—whispering, it was said, that his true father was a god.

She was a woman of overpowering will and intense religion, initiated into ecstatic rites and reputed to handle sacred serpents. Her marriage to Philip soured amid the rivalries of his other wives, and when Philip was struck down by an assassin in 336 BC, rumor—probably unjust—laid some of the blame at her door.

While Alexander lived and conquered Asia she guarded his interests at home, quarreling with his regent. But his death unleashed her ferocity. Championing her infant grandson as the rightful king, she invaded Macedonia, seized her enemies, and put to death the rival king and his followers in a bloody purge.

Her triumph was brief. Cassander marched against her, besieged her, and, once she surrendered, had the old queen condemned and killed—though she is said to have faced her executioners with unbroken royal defiance. The greatest warrior-king her ancestral house of Epirus would ever produce, a kinsman of her line who carried war even to Rome, was Pyrrhus.
