Christina, Queen of Sweden

Queen of Sweden (1626–1689)

Christina of Sweden was one of the most learned and unconventional monarchs of her age, a queen who astonished Europe by giving up her crown. The only surviving child of the warrior-king Gustavus Adolphus, who fell in battle when she was six, she was raised and schooled as if she were a prince—mastering languages, philosophy, and statecraft, and developing a lifelong passion for art and ideas.

When she came to rule, she made her court at Stockholm a magnet for European learning. Most famously, she summoned the philosopher René Descartes to instruct her; the great thinker, forced to give lessons in the icy Swedish dawn, caught a chill and died there in 1650. Brilliant, restless, and fiercely independent, Christina refused to marry and chafed against the duties of her throne.

In 1654 she did the unthinkable: she abdicated, secretly embraced Roman Catholicism, and left Sweden for Rome. There she lived for decades as a dazzling and eccentric figure, a patron of music and art who supported composers and artists and founded an academy, keeping all Europe gossiping about her.

She died in Rome in 1689 and was laid to rest in St. Peter's Basilica, a rare honor for a woman. The supreme artist of the papal Rome she had made her home—sculptor, architect, and impresario of the Baroque—was Gian Lorenzo Bernini.
