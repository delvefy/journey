Gabriel García Márquez

Colombian novelist (1927–2014)

Gabriel García Márquez—“Gabo” to a continent that adored him—was the Colombian master of magical realism and one of the most widely read novelists of the twentieth century.

Born in the sleepy town of Aracataca and raised among the tall tales of his grandparents, he worked for years as a journalist before finding the voice that would make him famous. The decisive spark, he often said, came from reading the opening of Kafka's Metamorphosis, which showed him that a writer could simply declare the impossible to be true. Steeped also in the mythical county of William Faulkner, he transformed his grandmother's deadpan way of relating marvels into a whole literary universe.

That universe found its home in One Hundred Years of Solitude, the saga of the Buendía family in the imaginary town of Macondo, where the miraculous and the everyday flow together as one. Published in 1967, it became a global phenomenon and the defining work of the Latin American “Boom.” Love in the Time of Cholera, Chronicle of a Death Foretold and others followed, and in 1982 he won the Nobel Prize.

A man of the left all his life, he poured his political passion into a long and controversial personal friendship—one that drew much criticism from fellow writers—with the Cuban revolutionary who ruled the island for half a century, Fidel Castro.
