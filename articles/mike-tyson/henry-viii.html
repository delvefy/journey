Henry VIII

King of England (1491–1547)

Henry VIII, born in 1491, was King of England for nearly four decades and one of the most consequential and notorious monarchs in the nation's history. A younger son of Henry VII, he became heir when his elder brother Arthur died, and on taking the throne in 1509 he married Arthur's widow, Catherine of Aragon. Handsome, athletic, and learned, the young king cut the figure of a Renaissance prince and even wrote a treatise defending Catholic doctrine against Martin Luther, earning the papal title "Defender of the Faith."

His reign turned on his desperate desire for a male heir. When Catherine bore him only a surviving daughter, Mary, Henry sought to annul the marriage so he could wed Anne Boleyn. The Pope refused, and Henry, advised by Thomas Cromwell, broke with Rome. The Act of Supremacy of 1534 made him Supreme Head of the Church of England, launching the English Reformation, and he dissolved the monasteries, seizing their vast wealth.

Henry married six times—the wives remembered by the rhyme "divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived." Anne Boleyn, mother of the future Elizabeth I, was executed in 1536; Jane Seymour gave him his son Edward. He dealt ruthlessly with opponents, sending his former chancellor Thomas More and many others to the block for refusing to accept his supremacy.

Growing corpulent, suspicious, and tyrannical in his later years, Henry died in 1547. He left England a Protestant realm and a strengthened monarchy, and three children who would each rule in turn.
