Nikola Tesla

Serbian-American inventor and electrical engineer (1856–1943)

Nikola Tesla, born in 1856 in the Austrian borderlands and trained as an engineer, emigrated to America and changed the way the world is powered. After a brief, bitter stint working for Thomas Edison, he struck out on his own and became the champion of alternating current. His polyphase system and induction motor, backed by the industrialist George Westinghouse, defeated Edison's direct current in the "War of the Currents," and alternating current became the standard for transmitting electricity across great distances—lighting the Chicago World's Fair and harnessing the power of Niagara Falls.

A torrent of invention poured from him: the Tesla coil, the rotating magnetic field, fluorescent lamps, remote control, and pioneering experiments in the wireless transmission of signals and even power, which entangled him in a long priority dispute with Marconi over the invention of radio.

His grandest dream, a great tower to broadcast energy and information around the globe, collapsed when his financier withdrew, and Tesla—brilliant, eccentric, and unworldly—slid gradually into poverty and obscurity, dying alone in a New York hotel in 1943 amid the flock of pigeons he loved. Only later did the world fully honour him; the very unit of magnetic field now bears his name.

Endlessly fascinated by the future, he counted among his friends the writer Mark Twain, who delighted in his laboratory. His great antagonist, the rival from whom he had parted in his first American years, was Thomas Edison.
