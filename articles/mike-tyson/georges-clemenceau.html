Georges Clemenceau

French premier, the “Father of Victory” (1841–1929)

Georges Clemenceau was the ferocious republican statesman who led France to victory in the First World War and dominated the peace that followed.

Trained as a physician in the Vendée, he threw himself instead into radical politics, serving as mayor of Montmartre and rising as a journalist and orator so feared for toppling ministries that he was nicknamed “the Tiger.” A passionate defender of the wronged officer Alfred Dreyfus, he ran the newspaper L'Aurore and gave Émile Zola's famous open letter its thunderous title, “J'accuse…!”, in 1898. He served a first term as prime minister before the war, combative toward strikers and the Church alike.

His greatest hour came in November 1917, when France was exhausted and demoralized and he was recalled, at seventy-six, to lead the government. “I wage war,” he declared, and through sheer will he stiffened the nation, backed his generals, and saw the conflict through to the armistice that made him the “Father of Victory.” Among the commanders who served under his government was Philippe Pétain, the defender of Verdun.

At the Paris Peace Conference of 1919 he was one of the Big Four, fighting for harsh guarantees against a future German revival and clashing repeatedly with the idealism of the American president. Outside politics his closest friendship was with the painter Claude Monet, whom he comforted in old age and persuaded to give his great cycle of water-lily canvases to the French state. Defeated in his bid for the presidency, he retired and died in 1929, his fiercest adversary at the peace table having been the architect of the League of Nations, Woodrow Wilson.
