Melissus of Samos

Greek philosopher and admiral (5th century BC)

Melissus of Samos was the last of the three great Eleatic philosophers and the only one who was also a man of action. In thought he was the faithful heir of Parmenides, defending and refining the doctrine that what truly is must be one, eternal, and unchanging. Yet he made a striking departure: where Parmenides had pictured Being as finite and sphere-like, Melissus argued that it must be unlimited and infinite, for there could be nothing beyond it to bound it.

He set out these arguments in a prose treatise on the nature of what is, reasoning with a rigor that even Aristotle, who thought him mistaken, felt obliged to answer. Through Melissus the bold logic of Elea reached its fullest systematic form.

But Melissus did not spend his life in argument alone. When his native Samos revolted against the empire of Athens, he took command of its fleet, and in 440 BC he won a naval victory over the Athenians—a rare triumph of a philosopher in war. Only the return of Pericles with a great armament, and a long siege, brought proud Samos to heel.

His was an age crowded with bold minds wrestling over whether the world is one or many. In Athens itself, at the very center of Pericles's circle, the philosopher who taught that all things were once mingled together until Mind set them in order was Anaxagoras.
