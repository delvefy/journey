Marguerite de Navarre

Queen of Navarre, author and patron of the Renaissance (1492–1549)

Marguerite de Navarre was the most brilliant woman of the early French Renaissance—a queen, a writer, and the great protector of its scholars and reformers.

Elder sister of King Francis I and daughter of Louise of Savoy, she stood close to the throne and used her place to shelter the new learning. Humanists, poets, and evangelical reformers found refuge under her wing when the doctors of the Sorbonne would have silenced them; among those she shielded was the satirist François Rabelais, who dedicated a book of his great comic epic to her.

She was herself a writer of power. Her devotional poem, the Mirror of the Sinful Soul, was condemned by the theologians of Paris and saved only by her brother's protection. Her enduring masterpiece is the Heptaméron, a collection of tales told by travellers sheltering at an abbey, consciously modelled on the Decameron of Giovanni Boccaccio and published only after her death.

Deeply pious and drawn to reform, she nonetheless never left the Catholic Church, holding a delicate middle path through the religious storms of her time.

Her blood would shape the future of France: through her daughter she was the grandmother of Henry IV, the first Bourbon king. But in the history of letters her name is joined above all to the writer she befriended and defended, François Rabelais.
