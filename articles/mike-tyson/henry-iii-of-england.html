Henry III of England

King of England (1207–1272)

Henry III, born in 1207, was the eldest son of King John and reigned for fifty-six years, one of the longest reigns in English history. He came to the throne in 1216 as a boy of nine, in the midst of the civil war his father had provoked, while a French army under Louis the Lion held much of the country. Loyal barons rallied to the child king, drove out the invaders, and reissued Magna Carta in his name.

Henry grew into a pious, cultured, but politically inept ruler. He was extravagant and trusted foreign favourites and his wife's relatives, alienating his barons; his costly, failed schemes—including a fantastical plan to win the crown of Sicily for his son—drained the treasury. His great passion was religion and art: he lavished a fortune on rebuilding Westminster Abbey in the Gothic style, as a shrine to his revered patron, the Anglo-Saxon saint-king Edward the Confessor, after whom he named his heir.

His misrule provoked a constitutional crisis. In 1258 the barons, led by Simon de Montfort, forced on him the Provisions of Oxford. The conflict escalated into the Second Barons' War; de Montfort captured Henry at the Battle of Lewes in 1264 and ruled in his name, summoning the famous parliament of 1265. But Henry's son, the future Edward I, rallied the royalists and destroyed de Montfort at Evesham.

Restored to power but old and weary, Henry left the governing to Edward and died in 1272. His long reign is remembered less for his own deeds than for the rise of Parliament and the glories of Westminster Abbey.
