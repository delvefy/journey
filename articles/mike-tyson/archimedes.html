Archimedes

Greek mathematician and inventor of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BC)

Archimedes of Syracuse is reckoned the greatest mathematician of antiquity and one of the greatest of any age. Building on the geometry of Euclid, he devised a "method of exhaustion" that anticipated the integral calculus by nearly two thousand years, using it to find the area of the circle and of curved figures, to bound the value of pi, and to prove that a sphere has two-thirds the volume of the cylinder enclosing it—a result he prized so highly that he asked for the figure to be carved upon his tomb.

He was as much physicist and engineer as geometer. He established the law of the lever, boasting that with a place to stand he could move the Earth, and he founded the science of hydrostatics with the principle of buoyancy that bears his name—the discovery that, by tradition, sent him leaping from his bath with the cry of "Eureka!" when he saw how to test the purity of his king's golden crown. To him are credited the water-raising screw, compound pulleys with which he is said to have drawn a laden ship ashore single-handed, and engines of war.

When Rome besieged Syracuse, his catapults and ship-grappling machines held the great fleet at bay for two years. But in 212 BC the city fell, and a Roman soldier cut him down—absorbed, the story goes, in a diagram in the sand, bidding the intruder not to disturb his circles.

His tomb, long neglected, was found and restored by Cicero; his work inspired Galileo, who called him "superhuman," and the mathematics of Isaac Newton; and his death was recorded by Plutarch. His contemporary and correspondent in Alexandria was the polymath Eratosthenes.
