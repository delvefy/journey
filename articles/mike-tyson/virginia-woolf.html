Virginia Woolf

English modernist writer (1882–1941)

Virginia Woolf was born Adeline Virginia Stephen in London in 1882, the daughter of the eminent man of letters Leslie Stephen. Largely educated in her father's enormous library, she suffered the early deaths of her mother and father and the first of the breakdowns that would recur throughout her life. After her father's death the Stephen children settled in the Bloomsbury district, where their circle of friends grew into the celebrated Bloomsbury Group.

In 1912 she married the writer and political thinker Leonard Woolf, and in 1917 the couple founded the Hogarth Press, which printed her own work and that of contemporaries. Freed to experiment, she became one of the great innovators of the modern novel, dissolving conventional plot in favour of the inner flow of consciousness.

Her major novels—Mrs Dalloway (1925), To the Lighthouse (1927), the time-leaping fantasy Orlando (1928), and The Waves (1931)—trace the texture of thought and memory across ordinary days. She was equally important as an essayist and critic, and her extended essay A Room of One's Own (1929), with its argument that a woman needs money and a room of her own to write, became a founding text of modern feminism.

Plagued throughout her life by mental illness, and dreading another collapse as war engulfed Europe, she drowned herself in the River Ouse in 1941. Her novels and essays remain central to twentieth-century literature.
