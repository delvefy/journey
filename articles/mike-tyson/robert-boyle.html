Robert Boyle

Anglo-Irish natural philosopher and founder of modern chemistry (1627–1691)

Robert Boyle, born in 1627 at Lismore Castle in Ireland as a younger son of the wealthy Earl of Cork, became one of the founders of modern chemistry and a leading figure of the Scientific Revolution. A devout and serious-minded man of great fortune, he devoted his life to experiment and was among the circle of natural philosophers—the "Invisible College"—whose meetings gave rise to the Royal Society of London.

In his most famous book, The Sceptical Chymist, Boyle attacked the old doctrine of four elements and the principles of the alchemists, arguing instead that matter is composed of tiny "corpuscles" in motion. By insisting that chemistry be built on careful experiment rather than tradition, he helped raise it from alchemy into a rigorous science, even as he never wholly abandoned the search for transmutation himself.

With his brilliant assistant Robert Hooke he built an improved air pump, and from their experiments came the law that still bears his name: that the volume of a gas varies inversely with its pressure when temperature is held fixed. The work championed the experimental, mechanical philosophy of nature and drew on the corpuscular vision of René Descartes, while his alchemical investigations were shared with the young Isaac Newton.

Boyle was also a deeply religious writer who endowed a lecture series to defend Christianity and supported the translation of the Bible into many tongues. His friend the philosopher John Locke absorbed his ideas, and his experimental creed descended directly from the new method preached a generation earlier by Francis Bacon.
