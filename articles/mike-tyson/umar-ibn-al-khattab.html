Umar ibn al-Khattab

Second Rashidun caliph (c. 584–644)

Umar ibn al-Khattab, born around 584 in Mecca, was the second of the Rashidun caliphs and one of the most powerful and consequential rulers in the history of Islam. A formidable, imposing man who had at first bitterly opposed Muhammad's new faith, he underwent a dramatic conversion that turned him into one of its staunchest champions and a close companion of the Prophet, whose wife Hafsa was Umar's daughter.

On Muhammad's death Umar was instrumental in securing the succession of Abu Bakr, and when Abu Bakr died in 634, Umar became caliph. His reign was a decade of astonishing expansion. Under his command the Arab armies, led by brilliant generals such as Khalid ibn al-Walid, shattered the two great empires of the age: they overran the Sassanid Persian Empire and stripped the Byzantine Empire of Heraclius of Syria, Palestine and Egypt. Jerusalem surrendered to Umar in person, and he entered the holy city humbly, guaranteeing the safety of its Christians.

Umar was as great an administrator as a conqueror. He organised the conquered lands into provinces, founded garrison cities, instituted the diwan to distribute stipends, and established the Islamic Hijri calendar. Famed for his austerity, justice and accessibility, he set a lasting standard for the office of caliph.

In 644 Umar was assassinated in Medina by a Persian captive, stabbed while leading prayers. He left behind an empire transformed into a world power and a reputation, especially among Sunnis, as the model of the just ruler.
