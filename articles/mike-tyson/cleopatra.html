Cleopatra

Last queen of Ptolemaic Egypt (69–30 BC)

Cleopatra VII, born in 69 BC, was the last active ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and one of the most celebrated women of antiquity. Though queen of Egypt, she belonged to a Greek-Macedonian dynasty descended from Ptolemy, a general of Alexander the Great, and she was reputedly the first of that line to learn the Egyptian language. Clever, cultivated, and politically astute, she sought to preserve her kingdom's independence amid the overwhelming power of Rome.

Locked in a dynastic struggle with her younger brother, she allied herself with Julius Caesar when he arrived in Egypt in 48 BC. He restored her to the throne, and she bore him a son, Caesarion. After Caesar's assassination, Cleopatra attached her fortunes to Mark Antony, one of the most powerful Romans of the day. The two became lovers and allies, had three children, and ruled the eastern Mediterranean in open rivalry with Caesar's heir, Octavian.

That rivalry ended in war. In 31 BC the fleets of Antony and Cleopatra were defeated by Octavian's forces at the naval Battle of Actium, and the couple withdrew to Egypt. As Octavian's army closed in the following year, Antony fell on his sword, and Cleopatra, refusing to be paraded in a Roman triumph, took her own life—by tradition through the bite of an asp.

Her death in 30 BC ended both the Ptolemaic dynasty and the independence of Egypt, which became a Roman province. Her dramatic life has fascinated posterity ever since, retold by the biographer Plutarch and immortalized on the stage by William Shakespeare.
