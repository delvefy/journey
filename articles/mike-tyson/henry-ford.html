Henry Ford

American industrialist (1863–1947)

Henry Ford did not invent the automobile, but he put the world on wheels. A Michigan farm boy who became chief engineer at Thomas Edison's Detroit power company—and was urged on by Edison himself—he founded the Ford Motor Company and in 1908 brought out the Model T, a car simple, sturdy, and cheap enough for ordinary people to own.

To build it in the numbers he imagined, Ford perfected the moving assembly line, slashing the time and cost of manufacture and pioneering the mass production that bears his name. In 1914 he stunned industry by doubling his workers' pay to five dollars a day, reasoning that the men who built his cars should be able to buy them—a stroke that helped create the modern consumer economy. He became one of the richest and most influential men on earth.

His record is darkened by a virulent antisemitism. Through his newspaper he circulated the notorious forgery known as the Protocols and a series of tracts blaming the world's ills on the Jews—writings admired in Germany, where Adolf Hitler praised Ford by name and later honoured him with a Nazi decoration. He was also fiercely anti-union and, in two world wars, an isolationist.

In the Second World War his vast Willow Run plant poured out bombers by the thousand, aided by the celebrated aviator who advised on its production—Charles Lindbergh.
