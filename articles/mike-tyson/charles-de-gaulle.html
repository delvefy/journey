Charles de Gaulle

Leader of Free France and founder of the Fifth Republic (1890–1970)

Charles de Gaulle was the soldier-statesman who rallied Free France against Nazi occupation and, two decades later, refounded the French republic in his own image.

Born in Lille into a patriotic Catholic family, he became a career army officer, was wounded and captured at Verdun in the First World War, and between the wars argued—largely in vain—for a modern army built around tanks. When France collapsed before Adolf Hitler's armies in 1940 and the government sought an armistice, de Gaulle escaped to London and on 18 June broadcast a defiant appeal to keep fighting, an act that made the obscure brigadier the embodiment of national resistance.

As leader of the Free French he proved a difficult ally: Winston Churchill, who sheltered his movement, found him maddening yet indispensable, while Franklin D. Roosevelt distrusted him and tried to sideline him in favour of others. He quarrelled with the Allied command of Dwight D. Eisenhower over the fate of France, yet insisted on leading the parade into liberated Paris in 1944. After heading a provisional government he withdrew in frustration from a fractured politics.

Recalled to power in 1958 amid the agony of the Algerian war, he founded the Fifth Republic with a powerful presidency, granted Algeria independence, built an independent French nuclear deterrent, vetoed British entry into the Common Market, and pursued reconciliation with Germany before resigning in 1969. His own career had begun under the patronage of the aged hero of Verdun, Philippe Pétain—the man who would later head the collaborationist Vichy regime that de Gaulle spent the war opposing.
