Max Planck

German physicist, founder of quantum theory (1858–1947)

Max Planck was born in Kiel in 1858 and became, by temperament, a conservative master of classical thermodynamics at the University of Berlin. Yet it was he who, in 1900, opened the quantum age. Struggling to explain the spectrum of light radiated by a heated body, he found that the formula worked only if energy were emitted not continuously but in discrete packets, or quanta, each proportional to its frequency through a new constant of nature now called Planck's constant.

He reached the result by adopting, almost against his will, the statistical methods of Ludwig Boltzmann, and he long hoped the quantum might be reconciled with older physics. It could not: his reluctant discovery overturned it. The work brought him the Nobel Prize in 1918.

Planck was also a generous judge of others' genius. He was among the first to grasp the significance of Albert Einstein's relativity and drew him to Berlin, and he saw the quantum he had founded carried far beyond his intentions by Einstein, by Niels Bohr, and by the younger generation.

His later life was marked by terrible loss: two daughters died in childbirth, one son fell in the First World War, and another, Erwin, was executed in 1945 for his part in the plot against Hitler. Planck stayed in Germany and strove to shield its science through the catastrophe. He died in 1947, honored as the reluctant father of the quantum.
