Hannibal

Carthaginian general (247–183 BC)

Hannibal Barca, son of the Carthaginian commander Hamilcar, was made as a boy to swear eternal enmity to Rome—an oath he kept for life. Building up Carthage's power in Spain, he provoked the Second Punic War and then astonished the ancient world by leading an army, war elephants and all, over the Pyrenees and across the snowbound passes of the Alps to fall upon Italy from the north.

There he won a string of victories that have become legend. At the Trebia and at Lake Trasimene he destroyed Roman armies; at Cannae in 216 BC he enveloped and annihilated a far larger force in a maneuver still studied as the perfect battle of encirclement. For some fifteen years he ranged up and down Italy, never beaten in the open field.

Yet he could not take Rome itself nor shatter its web of alliances, and the Romans learned to refuse him the pitched battles he craved, wearing his army down through caution and time. When at last a Roman army threatened Carthage, Hannibal was recalled to Africa, and there, in 202 BC, he met his only defeat, at the hands of Scipio Africanus on the field of Zama.

His later years were those of a hunted man—a reforming statesman driven into exile, advising Rome's eastern enemies, and at last taking poison in Bithynia rather than be handed over. He ranked the conquering Macedonian first among all generals and modeled his own art of war upon him; that supreme commander he admired was Alexander the Great. The cautious Roman who first found the way to blunt Hannibal's genius, by refusing to fight him, was Fabius Maximus.
