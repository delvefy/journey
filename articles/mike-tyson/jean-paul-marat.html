Jean-Paul Marat

French revolutionary and journalist (1743–1793)

Jean-Paul Marat was the most ferocious voice of the radical press during the French Revolution, a man whose pen called openly for the blood of the Republic's enemies. Born in Boudry, in the principality of Neuchâtel, he trained as a physician and practiced in London and Paris, where he also pursued ambitious experiments in optics, fire, and electricity. Bitter at the Academy of Sciences for spurning his theories, he carried into politics a deep resentment of established authority.

When revolution came, Marat found his true vocation. In 1789 he founded the newspaper L'Ami du peuple—"The Friend of the People"—which thundered against ministers, generals, and moderates alike, denouncing traitors real and imagined and demanding ever harsher measures. Hunted for his attacks, he hid in cellars and sewers, where he aggravated the painful skin disease that forced him to spend his days soaking in a medicinal bath.

Elected to the National Convention, he sat with the radical Montagnards and helped bring down the moderate Girondins, sealing his place among the Revolution's most uncompromising figures. He had pressed, with Robespierre and Danton, for the trial and execution of King Louis XVI.

On 13 July 1793 a young Girondin sympathizer, Charlotte Corday, gained entry to his rooms and stabbed him to death as he sat in his bath. In an instant Marat became a martyr of the Revolution, his murder immortalized in one of the most famous political paintings ever made—The Death of Marat, by his friend and fellow Jacobin Jacques-Louis David.
