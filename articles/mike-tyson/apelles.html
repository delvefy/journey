Apelles

Greek painter (4th century BC)

Apelles was the most famous painter of the ancient world, the supreme master of an art now wholly lost to us. Trained in the Sicyonian school, he became court painter to Philip and then to Alexander the Great, who—so the story went—forbade any other artist to paint his portrait. He depicted the conqueror grasping the thunderbolt of Zeus, and when, while painting Alexander's beautiful mistress Campaspe, the painter fell in love with her, the king generously gave her to him.

Around his name clustered the famous sayings of his craft. He worked at his drawing every day—"no day without a line"—and when a cobbler presumed to criticise more of a picture than the sandals, Apelles told him to judge no higher than the shoe, giving the world the proverb to stick to one's last. With his rival Protogenes he held a celebrated contest to draw the finest possible line.

His masterpieces were spoken of with awe: an Aphrodite rising from the sea and wringing the water from her hair, and an allegory of Calumny so vivid that the painters of the Renaissance tried to recreate it from the ancient description alone. He was praised above all for grace, and for the wisdom of knowing when to lift his hand from the work.

Though not a line of his painting survives, his fame as the perfect artist never died. The great painter of the generation before him, whose grapes were said to have deceived the very birds, was Zeuxis.
