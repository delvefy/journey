Thomas Cranmer

First Protestant Archbishop of Canterbury (1489–1556)

Thomas Cranmer was the chief architect of the English Reformation and one of the great makers of the English language. A Cambridge scholar of quiet brilliance, he rose to notice by suggesting that Henry VIII consult the universities of Europe on the lawfulness of his marriage. The advice pleased the king, and in 1533 Cranmer was raised to the highest office in the English Church, Archbishop of Canterbury.

He at once pronounced Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon void and validated his union with Anne Boleyn, setting his hand to the whole revolution that followed. Cautious under Henry, he came into his own under the Protestant boy-king Edward VI, when he composed the Book of Common Prayer. Its sonorous English—phrases still spoken at weddings and funerals—gave the reformed faith a liturgy of surpassing beauty and shaped Anglican worship for centuries.

When the Catholic Mary I came to the throne, Cranmer was doomed. Imprisoned and tried, worn down by interrogation, he signed several recantations of his Protestant beliefs. Yet at the stake in Oxford in 1556 he found his courage, repudiated his recantations, and thrust into the flames first the right hand that had betrayed his conscience.

Cranmer looked beyond England to the broader Protestant movement on the continent, corresponding with its leaders and hoping to unite the reformed churches. The greatest of those continental reformers, whose Geneva became the capital of the international Protestant cause, was John Calvin.
