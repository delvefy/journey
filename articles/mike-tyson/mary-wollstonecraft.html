Mary Wollstonecraft

English writer and pioneer of feminism (1759–1797)

Mary Wollstonecraft was a pioneering writer and philosopher whose work laid a foundation stone of modern feminism. Born in London to an unhappy household, she made her own precarious way as a governess, teacher, and schoolmistress before turning to her pen. She became a valued author for the radical publisher Joseph Johnson, entering a circle of reformers and freethinkers that included Thomas Paine and the philosopher William Godwin.

When Edmund Burke condemned the French Revolution, Wollstonecraft was among the first to answer, publishing A Vindication of the Rights of Men. But her enduring achievement came two years later with A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792). In it she argued that women only appeared inferior to men because they were denied education, and she demanded that they be treated as rational beings entitled to learning and independence—a revolutionary claim that resonates to this day.

Her own life was turbulent and unconventional. She lived through the Revolution in Paris, bore a daughter outside marriage, survived heartbreak and a suicide attempt, and at last found stability in marriage to Godwin, a union of two famous radical minds.

In 1797 she died at thirty-eight, only days after giving birth to a second daughter. That child, raised in the shadow of her mother's fame, would grow up to write one of the most famous novels in the language and to marry a great Romantic poet—Mary Shelley.
