Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Genevan philosopher of the Enlightenment (1712–1778)

Jean-Jacques Rousseau, born in Geneva in 1712, was a philosopher and writer whose ideas reshaped politics, education, and the life of feeling. Self-taught and restless, he burst into fame with essays arguing that civilization, far from improving humanity, had corrupted a species naturally good—a provocation that set him against the optimistic, reason-worshiping mainstream of the Enlightenment.

His Social Contract of 1762 opens with the thunderclap "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains," and argues that legitimate authority can rest only on the consent of the people and the "general will." It became a founding text of modern democracy and republicanism. In Émile he reimagined education as the nurturing of natural development, and in his Confessions he pioneered a startlingly intimate form of autobiography. His exaltation of nature, emotion, and authenticity made him a father of Romanticism.

Brilliant but tormented and suspicious, Rousseau quarreled with nearly everyone—estranged from Diderot, at war with his great rival Voltaire, and embroiled in a famous, bitter public falling-out with David Hume, who had sheltered him in England. His banned and burned books kept him on the move for much of his life.

He died in 1778, but his influence only grew: he set the philosopher Immanuel Kant on a new path, and his doctrine of the general will was invoked by the revolutionaries who, a decade later, overturned France. He too came to rest in the Panthéon.
