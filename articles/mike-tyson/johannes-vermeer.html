Johannes Vermeer

Dutch Golden Age painter (1632–1675)

Johannes Vermeer was the quiet master of light, a painter who in a mere handful of canvases achieved a serene perfection unmatched in art. He spent his whole life in the Dutch town of Delft, working slowly as both painter and art dealer, and left behind only some three dozen pictures—yet each is a marvel. In luminous domestic scenes he caught the fall of daylight through a window, the gleam of pearl and porcelain, and the stillness of a private moment.

Girl with a Pearl Earring, The Milkmaid, the radiant View of Delft, and his interiors of women reading, pouring milk, or making music are treasured among the supreme achievements of painting. He rendered texture and color with an almost magical exactness, and may have studied the images cast by the camera obscura to school his eye.

In his own day Vermeer was little known beyond Delft and far from rich; the French invasion and economic collapse of 1672 ruined his trade, and he died in debt in 1675. For nearly two centuries he was all but forgotten, until nineteenth-century critics rediscovered him and raised him to the first rank of artists.

In Delft he shared his streets, and his very year of birth, with a tradesman of insatiable curiosity who turned hand-ground lenses upon the unseen world—the father of microscopy, who served as executor of Vermeer's estate, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek.
