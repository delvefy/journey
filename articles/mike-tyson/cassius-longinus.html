Cassius Longinus

Greek philosopher and rhetorician (c. 213–273)

Cassius Longinus was one of the most learned men of the late antique world, a Platonist philosopher and master of rhetoric whose erudition became proverbial—admirers called him "a living library" and "a walking museum." Schooled in Alexandria under Ammonius Saccas, the same teacher who trained the founder of Neoplatonism, he went on to teach philosophy and literature in Athens for many years, numbering among his pupils the scholar Porphyry.

His reputation as a critic was immense. For centuries the brilliant treatise On the Sublime, a study of grandeur and power in literature, was read under his name, and though scholars now doubt the attribution, it kept his fame alive among the lovers of great writing.

In his later years Longinus left the schools for the dangerous world of politics, becoming the counselor and chief minister of Queen Zenobia of Palmyra. It was widely believed that his was the eloquent and defiant hand behind the letters with which Zenobia rejected the demands of Rome.

When the emperor Aurelian crushed Palmyra, he made the philosopher pay for that defiance: Longinus was condemned and executed in 273, meeting his death, it was said, with serene composure. The fellow student of his Alexandrian school days, who became the supreme philosopher of the age and the founder of Neoplatonism, was Plotinus.
