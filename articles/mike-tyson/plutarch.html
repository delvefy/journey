Plutarch

Greek biographer and philosopher (c. 46–120 AD)

Plutarch, born around 46 AD in the small Boeotian town of Chaeronea, was a Greek biographer, essayist, and philosopher whose writings shaped how later ages remembered the classical world. A Roman citizen and a devoted Platonist, he traveled and lectured but spent much of his life near home, serving for decades as a priest of Apollo at the sanctuary of Delphi under a succession of emperors.

His fame rests above all on the Parallel Lives, a series of paired biographies setting a famous Greek beside a famous Roman—Alexander the Great with Julius Caesar, Demosthenes with Cicero, and many more—and drawing out the character of each. Plutarch insisted he wrote lives, not histories, caring less for great battles than for the revealing anecdote or telling phrase that exposed a man's soul. His subjects ranged from statesmen like Pericles to the doomed lovers of his Life of Antony, which preserves the legend of Cleopatra.

He also composed the Moralia, a vast and varied collection of essays on ethics, religion, education, and custom, marked throughout by humane good sense and Platonic conviction.

Plutarch died around 120 AD, but his influence only grew. Translated and retranslated, the Lives became a wellspring for Renaissance writers; through Thomas North's English version they gave William Shakespeare the plots of his Roman plays. For centuries Plutarch was among the most widely read of all ancient authors, the chief portraitist of antiquity's great men.
