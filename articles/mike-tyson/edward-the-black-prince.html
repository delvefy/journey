Edward the Black Prince

Prince of Wales, hero of Crécy and Poitiers (1330–1376)

Edward of Woodstock, known to later ages as the Black Prince, was born in 1330, the eldest son of Edward III and heir to the English throne. He grew up amid his father's wars against France and became the very model of the chivalric warrior. At sixteen he commanded the vanguard at the Battle of Crécy in 1346, where the English longbow shattered the French nobility, and his father reputedly refused to send him aid, bidding the boy "win his spurs."

His masterpiece came ten years later. In 1356, leading a raiding army deep into France, the prince was cornered near Poitiers by a far larger force under the French king John II. Once again English archery and discipline triumphed; the prince not only won the battle but captured King John himself, whom he treated with elaborate courtesy and brought as a prize to London. The ransom and prestige were immense.

Made Prince of Aquitaine, Edward ruled the English lands in south-western France and intervened in Spain, winning another hard victory at Nájera in 1367 to restore his ally Pedro of Castile. But the Spanish campaign ruined his health and his finances. His harsh taxation turned Gascon opinion against him, war with France resumed, and in 1370 his sack of Limoges stained his chivalrous name.

Broken by a wasting illness, the Black Prince returned to England and died in 1376, a year before his father, so that the crown passed instead to his young son, who became Richard II. Mourned as the flower of English chivalry, the prince was celebrated in the chronicles of Jean Froissart as the greatest soldier of his age.
