Baruch Spinoza

Dutch rationalist philosopher (1632–1677)

Baruch Spinoza, born in Amsterdam in 1632 to a family of Portuguese Jewish refugees, was among the most original and courageous philosophers of the early modern age and a fountainhead of the Enlightenment. His radical ideas about God and scripture led, when he was only twenty-three, to his harsh excommunication from the Jewish community of Amsterdam. Unbowed, he lived the rest of his life quietly and modestly, grinding optical lenses for his living and declining a university chair so as to keep his freedom of thought.

In his masterwork, the Ethics, published only after his death and written in the austere form of geometric proofs, Spinoza argued that there exists but one infinite substance, which he called God or Nature—Deus sive Natura—of which all things are aspects. This identification of God with the whole of reality denied a personal, intervening creator and struck his contemporaries as atheism, yet for Spinoza it pointed the way to human freedom and blessedness through understanding and the "intellectual love of God."

His Theological-Political Treatise pioneered the critical study of the Bible and made a powerful early case for freedom of thought and speech. Condemned and banned in his own time, his work would later be revered: Hegel declared that to be a philosopher one must first be a Spinozist, and centuries on Albert Einstein professed belief in "Spinoza's God."

Spinoza died in 1677, at only forty-four, his lungs ruined by glass dust—a serene life that drew its deepest debt from the modern philosophy founded by René Descartes.
