Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

German idealist philosopher (1770–1831)

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel, born in Stuttgart in 1770, was the towering figure of German Idealism and one of the most influential—and famously difficult—philosophers in history. Building on and reaching beyond Kant, he constructed a vast system of "absolute idealism" in which thought and reality are ultimately one, and all of history is the gradual self-unfolding of Spirit toward freedom and self-knowledge.

His dialectical method—the way contradictions are driven forward and resolved at ever higher levels—became one of the most powerful ideas in modern thought. He set it out in the Phenomenology of Spirit, which he is said to have finished in 1806 as Napoleon's cannon thundered at the Battle of Jena; catching sight of the conqueror riding through the town, Hegel called him "the world-soul on horseback." Further works, the Science of Logic and the Encyclopedia, completed a system that ranked Aristotle and Spinoza among its great ancestors.

As a celebrated professor in Berlin—where the embittered Schopenhauer railed against him—Hegel dominated German philosophy. He taught that "the rational is the real," and that the state and history embody the progress of reason and liberty.

After his sudden death from cholera in 1831, his disciples split into rival camps. From the radical "Young Hegelians" emerged the young Karl Marx, who would invert Hegel's idealist dialectic into a materialist one and change the world. Hegel's reach across philosophy, politics, and history has few equals.
