Afonso de Albuquerque

Portuguese admiral and governor of India (1453–1515)

Afonso de Albuquerque was the chief architect of the Portuguese empire in the East, an admiral and statesman whose conquests gave a small Atlantic kingdom command of the seas and trade of half the world.

Sent eastward by King Manuel I to make Portugal master of the Indian Ocean, he conceived a bold strategy: rather than attempt to rule vast lands, he would seize the narrow choke-points through which all the great trade had to pass. In 1510 he stormed and took Goa, which became the capital of Portuguese India and remained so for four and a half centuries. In 1511 he captured Malacca, the gateway to the spice islands of the East; and in 1515 he seized Hormuz at the mouth of the Persian Gulf.

Within these strongholds he built fortresses, established arsenals, and encouraged his men to marry local women and settle, laying the human foundations of a lasting presence. He sought alliances with Indian princes, among them the emperor Krishnadevaraya of Vijayanagara, whose cavalry depended on the war-horses the Portuguese could supply.

Feared by his enemies and stern with his own men, he was called the Lion of the Seas. Yet court intrigue undid him at the last: as he lay dying aboard ship off Goa in 1515, word reached him that the king had replaced him with a rival.

He had completed what Vasco da Gama began when he first opened the sea-road to India, and the young Ferdinand Magellan had served in his ranks at the taking of Malacca.
