Alexander Hamilton

American founding father and first Treasury Secretary (1755–1804)

Alexander Hamilton rose from obscure and illegitimate beginnings in the West Indies to become one of the principal architects of the United States. Orphaned young, he was sent to New York to study and threw himself into the revolutionary cause, serving for years as the trusted aide-de-camp of General George Washington and winning glory in a charge at the climactic siege of Yorktown.

His genius lay in statecraft and finance. With James Madison and John Jay he wrote the bulk of The Federalist Papers, the classic defence of the new Constitution. As the first Secretary of the Treasury under President Washington, he built the financial machinery of the nation—assuming the states' war debts, founding a national bank and a mint, and setting the country on a commercial, manufacturing path.

His vision of strong central authority made him the founder of the Federalist Party and the bitter antagonist of Thomas Jefferson, whose agrarian, states'-rights philosophy clashed with his at every turn within the cabinet. The rivalry split the early republic into its first political parties.

His later years were marked by feuds, including a damaging quarrel with the Federalist president John Adams. In 1804 his long enmity with Vice President Aaron Burr ended in a duel at Weehawken, where Hamilton was mortally wounded. He had earlier corresponded on the liberation of Spanish America with Francisco de Miranda.
