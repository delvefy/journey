Pliny the Elder

Roman naturalist and author (23/24–79 AD)

Pliny the Elder was the great encyclopedist of the Roman world, a man of ferocious industry who, between military commands and high administrative posts, read, excerpted, and wrote without pause—his nephew tells us he begrudged every hour not spent studying. A trusted friend of the emperor Vespasian, he served the empire by day and filled his nights and journeys with note-taking.

His monument is the Natural History, a vast work in thirty-seven books that set out to gather the whole of human knowledge about the natural world—the heavens, geography, the races of men, animals, plants, medicine, metals and stones, and the arts. Dedicated to the future emperor Titus and drawing on thousands of earlier authors, it carried forward the great tradition of natural inquiry founded by Aristotle and remained an authority for some fifteen centuries. Its books on painting and sculpture are very nearly all we possess of the history of Greek art.

His death matched his life. In 79 AD, commanding the fleet at Misenum when Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried Pompeii, Pliny sailed straight toward the disaster, partly to study the strange cloud and partly to rescue those trapped beneath it. He came ashore amid the falling ash and died there, overcome by the fumes—a scientist and a Roman officer to the end. The account of it, written by his nephew, is one of the most famous letters of antiquity.

His patron and friend, who raised the Flavian dynasty to the purple, was the emperor Vespasian.
