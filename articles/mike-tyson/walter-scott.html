Walter Scott

Scottish novelist and poet, father of the historical novel (1771–1832)

Sir Walter Scott was the most popular and influential writer of his age, and effectively the inventor of the historical novel. A lawyer by training and an antiquarian by passion, he steeped himself in the history, legend, and ballads of his native Scotland, which he first collected and then transmuted into literature. He won early fame with sweeping narrative poems—The Lay of the Last Minstrel, Marmion, The Lady of the Lake.

When the dazzling success of Byron's verse overshadowed his own, Scott turned to prose, publishing Waverley anonymously in 1814. There followed a torrent of historical romances—Rob Roy, Ivanhoe, The Heart of Midlothian—that brought the past alive with unprecedented vividness and sympathy for all parties in old quarrels. They made him the most read author in Europe and the model for a whole generation of novelists, from Dumas in France to Manzoni in Italy and Cooper in America.

He all but invented the romantic image of the Scottish Highlands, and he loved to play the laird at his beloved estate of Abbotsford. Then disaster struck: the collapse of his publishers in 1826 left him crushed by enormous debts. Refusing bankruptcy, he drove himself to write without rest to repay every creditor, a labour that broke his health and hastened his death in 1832.

The most memorable encounter of his youth had been a single meeting, at an Edinburgh gathering, with Scotland's beloved national poet, Robert Burns.
