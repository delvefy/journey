Laozi

Founder of Daoism, reputed author of the Tao Te Ching (6th century BC, by tradition)

Laozi—“the Old Master”—is the shadowy sage revered as the founder of Daoism. Tradition makes him a contemporary of Confucius named Li Er, a keeper of the archives at the royal court of Zhou, but his very existence is uncertain, and many scholars regard him as a legend or a composite of several teachers. What is certain is the slender, cryptic book of some five thousand characters that bears his name, the Tao Te Ching, one of the most translated and influential texts ever written.

Its teaching turns on the Dao, the nameless Way that underlies and flows through all things, and on wu wei, action through non-action—accomplishing without forcing, as water wears down stone by yielding. The sage, it counsels, should embrace simplicity, humility, and stillness; the soft outlasts the hard, and the ruler governs best who governs least and leaves the people to their natural course.

The famous legend of his end tells that, grieved by the decay of the age, the old sage mounted an ox and rode west toward the frontier. At the mountain pass the gatekeeper Yinxi, recognizing his wisdom, begged him not to depart without leaving a record; Laozi thereupon set down his book and passed beyond the pass, never to be seen again. Tradition also holds that Confucius once met him and came away comparing him to a dragon, beyond his understanding.

In later centuries he was worshipped as a god of the Daoist religion. The philosopher who carried his vision furthest, in dazzling parables of freedom and paradox, was Zhuangzi.
