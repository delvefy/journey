Paul Cézanne

French post-impressionist painter, father of modern art (1839–1906)

Paul Cézanne is so pivotal to the story of modern art that both Picasso and Matisse called him “the father of us all.” Born in Aix-en-Provence, he worked for much of his life in obscurity and isolation, doggedly pursuing a single revolutionary aim: to make of Impressionism “something solid and durable,” to find the underlying structure—the cylinder, the sphere, the cone—beneath the shimmer of appearances.

Again and again he painted the same motifs—the looming Mont Sainte-Victoire near his home, still lifes of apples and drapery, groups of bathers, peasants at cards—building each canvas patch by patch into a tense, monumental architecture of colour. Few understood him in his lifetime, and recognition came only at its end.

But the young painters who saw his memorial exhibition understood at once. From his broken, faceted planes the Cubists would take their start, and his influence runs through nearly all the art that followed.

His deepest human bond, though, lay outside painting. His closest friend from boyhood in Aix, with whom he had roamed the Provençal countryside, was a writer who became one of France's greatest novelists—until a novel about a doomed, failed painter, which Cézanne read as a portrait of himself, broke their friendship forever. That friend was Émile Zola.
