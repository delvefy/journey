Arthur Schopenhauer

German philosopher of the Will and of pessimism (1788–1860)

Arthur Schopenhauer was the great philosopher of the Will and the most eloquent pessimist in the Western tradition. Born to a wealthy merchant family, he had the means to think and write as he pleased, and in 1818 he set out his whole system in The World as Will and Representation. Building on Kant, he held that the world we perceive is mere “representation,” appearance; but its hidden inner essence is Will—a blind, restless, insatiable striving that drives all of nature and ourselves.

From this he drew a bleak conclusion: because the Will is endless craving, existence is shot through with suffering, and satisfaction is only the brief lull before fresh desire. Yet he saw two paths of release. In the contemplation of art—above all music, which he thought the direct voice of the Will—we are lifted for a moment out of wanting; and through compassion and the ascetic renunciation of desire we may quiet the Will altogether, as the saints and sages of India and of Christendom had taught.

He was among the first European thinkers to take the philosophy of India seriously, treasuring a Latin version of the Upanishads and finding deep kinship with the teaching of the Buddha. He revered Plato alongside Kant, and poured scorn on his contemporary Hegel, whose fame he bitterly envied; his friend from Weimar days was the poet Goethe, with whom he studied the theory of colours.

Ignored for most of his life, he won fame only at its end—and then enormous influence over the arts and thought that followed. The young thinker whose life his work transformed was Friedrich Nietzsche.
