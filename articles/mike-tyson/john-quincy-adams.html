John Quincy Adams

Sixth President of the United States (1767–1848)

John Quincy Adams, son of the second president, was perhaps the most accomplished diplomat in early American history and, in a remarkable second act, a fearless congressional foe of slavery. Carried to Europe as a boy on his father's missions, he served as secretary to an American envoy in Russia at fourteen, and as a young man was appointed by George Washington to his first diplomatic posts.

He helped negotiate the Treaty of Ghent that ended the War of 1812, then served as a brilliant Secretary of State, acquiring Florida from Spain, fixing the nation's western boundary, and drafting the principles of the Monroe Doctrine that warned Europe away from the Americas. He had broken earlier with his own Federalist party to support the foreign policy of Thomas Jefferson.

Elected president in the disputed contest of 1824—decided in the House amid cries of a “corrupt bargain”—he found his ambitious plans for roads, canals, and learning frustrated by his opponents, and was turned out after a single term.

Rather than retire, he won election to the House of Representatives, the only former president ever to do so, and served there for seventeen years as “Old Man Eloquent.” He battled the gag rule that silenced antislavery petitions and won freedom for the captives of the Amistad before the Supreme Court. He collapsed at his desk in the Capitol and died in 1848. The rival who had defeated him for reelection was Andrew Jackson.
