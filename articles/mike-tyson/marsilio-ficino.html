Marsilio Ficino

Florentine philosopher and translator of Plato (1433–1499)

Marsilio Ficino was the scholar who gave the Latin West its Plato. The son of a physician near Florence, he came to the attention of Cosimo de' Medici, who in his old age longed to read the Greek philosophers and saw in the young Ficino the man to translate them. Cosimo gave him a villa at Careggi and a mission that would occupy his life: to render the whole of Plato into Latin.

Before Plato, Cosimo set him a more urgent task—the freshly recovered Corpus Hermeticum, the writings ascribed to the legendary sage Hermes Trismegistus, which Ficino translated first. He then completed the first full Latin version of Plato's dialogues, and afterward the Enneads of Plotinus and other Neoplatonists, opening a tradition that had been all but lost for a thousand years.

Around Ficino gathered the revived Platonic Academy of Florence, an informal circle of scholars and Medici intimates that included the young Pico della Mirandola and the poet Poliziano. Ordained a priest in 1473, Ficino sought to reconcile Platonism with Christianity; his great work, the Platonic Theology, argued for the immortality of the soul, and his letters made fashionable the idea of a pure, spiritual “Platonic love.”

His treatise On Life mingled medicine, astrology, and a cautious natural magic, and influenced physicians and occultists for generations. Ficino died in 1499, having shaped the intellectual color of the entire Florentine Renaissance. The patron who had set him to work and built the Medici greatness on which that Renaissance rested was Cosimo de' Medici.
