Humphry Davy

Cornish chemist and inventor (1778–1829)

Humphry Davy was born in Penzance in 1778 and apprenticed to an apothecary before joining the Pneumatic Institution in Bristol, where new gases were tested as medicines. There he investigated nitrous oxide, discovered its intoxicating effects, and shared the experience of “laughing gas” with friends including the poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge.

Moving to the Royal Institution in London, Davy became the most dazzling scientific lecturer of his day, drawing fashionable crowds. Wielding the powerful battery recently invented by Alessandro Volta, he passed currents through molten compounds and isolated a remarkable run of new elements—potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, barium, and more—and proved that chlorine was itself an element, overturning a claim of Antoine Lavoisier that all acids contained oxygen.

In 1815 he devised the miner's safety lamp, whose fine wire gauze kept the flame from igniting explosive gases underground. It saved countless lives, and he refused to patent it, giving it freely to the world.

Knighted, made a baronet, and elected president of the Royal Society, Davy was also a poet who moved among the Romantics. He took on a young bookbinder, Michael Faraday, as his assistant, and later admitted that Faraday was the greatest of all his discoveries. He died at Geneva in 1829.
