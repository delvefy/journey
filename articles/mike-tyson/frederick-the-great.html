Frederick the Great

King of Prussia (1712–1786)

Frederick the Great raised Prussia from a middling kingdom into one of the great powers of Europe and became the model of the enlightened warrior-king. As a sensitive, music-loving crown prince he chafed under his harsh soldier-father, even attempting to flee—a bid punished by the execution of his friend before his eyes. When he came to the throne in 1740, the cultivated young man astonished Europe by his ruthlessness in war.

Almost at once he seized the rich province of Silesia from the Habsburg ruler Maria Theresa, plunging the continent into years of conflict. In the grinding Seven Years' War he held out, often near ruin, against the combined might of Austria, France, and Russia, saving his kingdom through brilliant generalship and a stroke of late fortune. He emerged with Prussia doubled in strength and his military genius legendary.

Yet Frederick was also a child of the Enlightenment. He reformed Prussia's law and administration, championed religious toleration, called himself "the first servant of the state," and filled his rococo retreat of Sanssouci with art, music, and conversation. A skilled flute player and composer, he drew the philosopher Voltaire to his court, in a celebrated friendship that ended in famous quarrels.

By his death in 1786 he had made Prussia the nucleus of a future Germany. The Habsburg empress who was his lifelong adversary, and whose Silesia he had torn away, was Maria Theresa.
