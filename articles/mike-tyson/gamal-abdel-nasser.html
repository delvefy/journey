Gamal Abdel Nasser

President of Egypt and pan-Arab leader (1918–1970)

Gamal Abdel Nasser was the charismatic president of Egypt who became the hero of Arab nationalism and a leading voice of the post-colonial world.

An army officer of humble origins, he led the Free Officers who overthrew the monarchy of King Farouk in 1952 and soon emerged as Egypt's undisputed ruler. His defining triumph came in 1956, when he nationalized the Suez Canal and faced down an invasion by Britain, France and Israel; pressure from both Dwight D. Eisenhower and the Soviet Union forced the invaders to withdraw, and Nasser stood vindicated as the champion of the Arab and colonized world.

At home he pursued land reform and a state-led “Arab socialism,” and abroad he became, with Jawaharlal Nehru and Tito, a founder of the non-aligned movement that refused to take sides in the Cold War. He leaned on Nikita Khrushchev's Soviet Union to build the great Aswan High Dam after the West withdrew its support. Yet he ruled with an iron hand, crushing the Muslim Brotherhood and sending the thinker Sayyid Qutb to the gallows.

His dream of Arab unity faltered, and in 1967 his armies suffered a catastrophic defeat by Israel in the Six-Day War, a blow from which his prestige never fully recovered. He died of a heart attack in 1970, mourned by enormous crowds across the Arab world. His comrade from the Free Officers who succeeded him—and who would startle the world by making peace with Israel—was Anwar Sadat.
