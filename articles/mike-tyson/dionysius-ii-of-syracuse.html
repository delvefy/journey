Dionysius II of Syracuse

Tyrant of Syracuse (c. 397–343 BC)

Dionysius II inherited from his formidable father the throne of Syracuse, but little of his ability. Young and given to wine and pleasure when power fell to him, he became the unlikely subject of one of antiquity's most famous experiments: the attempt to make of a tyrant a philosopher-king.

His relative Dion, devoted to Plato, persuaded the great philosopher to come to Syracuse and take the young ruler in hand. Plato made the long voyage—twice—and for a time Dionysius affected an enthusiasm for geometry and wisdom. But his vanity and suspicion soon won out; he turned on Dion, exiled him, seized his property, and let the philosophical dream collapse. Plato departed, the project a failure that he would lament for the rest of his life.

Dion returned in arms and drove him from Syracuse, and thereafter Dionysius's fortunes wandered: he clung to lesser cities, oppressed them, briefly recovered his capital amid the chaos, but never regained his father's greatness.

His final fall came at the hands of a liberator from across the sea, who stripped him of his power and packed him off to live out his life as a private man at Corinth—where, the moralists loved to say, the former tyrant ended as a humble schoolmaster, a byword for the turning of fortune's wheel. The Corinthian general who deposed him and set Syracuse free was Timoleon.
