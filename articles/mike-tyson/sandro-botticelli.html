Sandro Botticelli

Italian Renaissance painter (1445–1510)

Sandro Botticelli was the painter who gave the early Florentine Renaissance its most graceful and poetic expression. Born in Florence and trained under Filippo Lippi, he developed a style of flowing line, delicate beauty, and wistful sentiment that set him apart. Taken up by the Medici, he became the favored artist of the brilliant circle around Lorenzo the Magnificent, steeped in its revival of classical myth and Platonic philosophy.

From that world came his immortal mythologies—the Primavera, with its dancing goddesses in a flowering grove, and The Birth of Venus, in which the goddess drifts to shore upon a shell. These remain among the best-loved images of the entire Renaissance. He also painted tender Madonnas, contributed to the wall frescoes of the Sistine Chapel in Rome, and made an extraordinary series of drawings illustrating Dante's Divine Comedy.

In his later years Botticelli fell under the spell of the apocalyptic preacher who came to dominate Florence, and his art turned darker, more intense, and severely religious; tradition holds that he cast some of his own "pagan" pictures into the flames of the Bonfire of the Vanities.

His sweet style fell from fashion after his death in 1510, and he was nearly forgotten until rediscovered centuries later. The fiery friar whose preaching seized his conscience and transformed his final years was Girolamo Savonarola.
