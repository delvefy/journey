Pericles

Athenian statesman (c. 495–429 BC)

Pericles, born around 495 BC into the aristocratic Alcmaeonid family, was the statesman who led Athens through its golden age, so dominating the city's politics and culture that the era bears his name. A gifted orator and general, he became the leading figure in Athenian public life for some three decades, repeatedly elected to the board of generals.

He deepened Athenian democracy, introducing pay for jurors and public officeholders so that even poor citizens could take part in governing. At the same time he presided over the height of Athenian imperial power, leading the Delian League and using its treasury to fund an extraordinary building program on the Acropolis. Under his patronage the sculptor Phidias oversaw the construction of the Parthenon, and his circle included the philosopher Anaxagoras, the playwright Sophocles, and his brilliant companion Aspasia.

His most famous speech, the Funeral Oration delivered for the war dead and preserved by the historian Thucydides, remains a stirring statement of democratic and civic ideals. Yet Pericles also led Athens into the long and ruinous Peloponnesian War against Sparta, adopting a defensive strategy of sheltering the population behind the city's walls.

That strategy turned tragic when a devastating plague swept the crowded city in 430 BC, killing a large part of its people. Pericles himself fell victim to the epidemic in 429 BC. His death deprived Athens of its steadying leadership, and the democracy he had guided soon faltered amid the strains of war.
