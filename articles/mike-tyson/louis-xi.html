Louis XI

King of France, "the Universal Spider" (1423–1483)

Louis XI, born in 1423, was the son of Charles VII, the king whom Joan of Arc had crowned. Impatient and conspiratorial by nature, the young Dauphin twice rebelled against his father and spent years in exile at the Burgundian court before succeeding to the throne in 1461. The experience taught him to trust no one, and as king he became the supreme practitioner of cunning statecraft—earning the nickname "the Universal Spider" for the webs of intrigue he spun across Europe.

Where earlier kings had fought their over-mighty nobles in the field, Louis preferred to undermine them by bribery, diplomacy, spies and patience. His great antagonist was Charles the Bold, the proud and warlike Duke of Burgundy, who led the feudal princes against him in the League of the Public Weal. Louis endured humiliations and reverses, but he played a long game. When Charles the Bold was killed at the Battle of Nancy in 1477, Louis pounced, seizing the duchy of Burgundy and much of his rival's inheritance for the French crown.

He was as active beyond his borders. He meddled in England's Wars of the Roses, brokering the alliance that briefly restored Henry VI, and later bought off the invading Edward IV with a pension at the Treaty of Picquigny in 1475. Against the Habsburg Maximilian he contested the remaining Burgundian lands.

Secretive, superstitious and increasingly fearful in his last years, Louis withdrew to his castle at Plessis-lès-Tours, ringed by guards. He died in 1483, leaving a France far larger, richer and more centralised than he had found it—the cold founder of the modern French monarchy, immortalised centuries later in Walter Scott's novel Quentin Durward.
