Richard Neville, 16th Earl of Warwick

English magnate, "the Kingmaker" (1428–1471)

Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, born in 1428, was the wealthiest and most powerful magnate in fifteenth-century England, and the man whose ability to raise armies and tip the balance of the Wars of the Roses earned him the enduring nickname "the Kingmaker." Through inheritance and a great marriage he commanded vast estates across the north and the Midlands, and he used that power to dominate the politics of a divided kingdom.

In the 1450s and early 1460s Warwick threw his might behind the Yorkist cause. His generalship and resources were decisive in driving the feeble Henry VI from the throne and installing the young Edward IV as king in 1461. For a time Warwick was the true power behind the crown, governing as Edward's chief minister and conducting his diplomacy.

But the two fell out. Edward's secret marriage to Elizabeth Woodville cut across Warwick's plans for a French alliance, and the rise of the queen's relatives pushed the earl aside. Resentful and ambitious, Warwick rebelled. In an extraordinary reversal, he allied with his old enemy, the Lancastrian queen Margaret of Anjou, and in 1470 drove Edward into exile and restored the captive Henry VI to the throne.

His triumph lasted only months. Edward IV returned in 1471, and the two armies met in fog at the Battle of Barnet. There Warwick was defeated and cut down as he tried to flee. With his death the house of Neville was broken, and no subject ever again wielded such over-mighty power. Through his daughters Isabel and Anne, married to the royal brothers George of Clarence and the future Richard III, his blood nonetheless flowed close to the throne he had spent his life making and unmaking.
