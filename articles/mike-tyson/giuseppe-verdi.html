Giuseppe Verdi

Italian opera composer (1813–1901)

Giuseppe Verdi was the dominant figure of Italian opera and, for his countrymen, a living emblem of the nation itself. Born in humble circumstances near Busseto, he suffered the loss of his young wife and both children before his career had even taken hold, and nearly abandoned music. The triumph of Nabucco in 1842 saved him; its chorus of exiled Hebrews, “Va, pensiero,” became an unofficial anthem of a people longing for unity, and the cry “Viva Verdi” doubled as a coded slogan of the Risorgimento.

Through the 1850s he produced the trio of works that conquered the world's stages—Rigoletto, drawn from Victor Hugo, Il trovatore, and La traviata—followed by the grand canvases of Aida, Don Carlos, and Un ballo in maschera. In 1874 he crowned the memory of the writer Alessandro Manzoni with his thunderous Requiem.

His deepest literary devotion was to William Shakespeare. An early Macbeth gave way, after a long silence and in partnership with the poet-composer Arrigo Boito, to the two supreme operas of his old age: the tragic Otello and the radiant comedy Falstaff, completed when he was nearly eighty.

His towering contemporary across the Alps was Richard Wagner, born the same year; the two never met but divided the operatic world between them. Verdi died in Milan in 1901 amid national mourning, and the succession in Italian opera passed to the younger Giacomo Puccini.
