Aryabhata

Indian mathematician and astronomer (476–550)

Aryabhata was the first of the great named mathematician-astronomers of classical India, and one of the most original minds in the history of science. At about the age of twenty-three, around the year 499, he composed the Aryabhatiya, a compact treatise in verse that founded a whole school of Indian astronomy.

In mathematics the work is astonishingly rich. Aryabhata used a place-value decimal system, gave a remarkably good value for pi of 3.1416, which he seems to have understood as only approximate, and set out the first tables of the sine function, the foundation of trigonometry. He described methods for square and cube roots, arithmetic progressions, and the solution of certain equations.

His astronomy was bolder still. Aryabhata held that the Earth rotates daily on its own axis, explaining the apparent westward sweep of the stars as an effect of the observer's own motion—an idea most of his successors, including Brahmagupta, rejected. He taught that the Moon and planets shine by reflected sunlight, and explained eclipses correctly as the shadows of Earth and Moon, dismissing the demon Rahu of myth.

He calculated the length of the sidereal year to within minutes of the modern figure and offered workable models of planetary motion. He lived and taught at Kusumapura, near the old Mauryan capital of Pataliputra, and may have led an institution of learning there.

The Aryabhatiya was studied and debated for a thousand years and carried his system into the work of later masters such as Bhaskara II. Honoured as the father of Indian mathematics and astronomy, Aryabhata gave his name to India's first artificial satellite.
