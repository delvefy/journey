Giotto

Florentine painter, father of Western painting (c. 1267–1337)

Giotto di Bondone, born around 1267 near Florence, was the painter who turned European art toward life. According to a tradition recorded by Vasari, the great Florentine master Cimabue discovered him as a shepherd boy drawing sheep on a rock and took him as a pupil. Whatever the truth of the tale, Giotto soon surpassed his teacher and broke decisively with the flat, golden, formulaic manner of Byzantine painting.

In his hands figures acquired weight and volume, occupied real space, and—above all—felt. His painted men and women grieve, embrace, and turn to one another with a tenderness and drama never before seen. His supreme achievement is the cycle of frescoes in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua, painted around 1305, where the lives of the Virgin and of Christ unfold with profound human emotion against luminous blue skies. He is also closely associated with the frescoes of the life of St Francis at Assisi.

So great was his reputation that Dante, his friend and contemporary, named him in the Divine Comedy as the painter who had eclipsed Cimabue's fame, and Boccaccio celebrated his genius and wit in the Decameron. Honoured by his city, Giotto was appointed master of works of Florence Cathedral, and the soaring bell tower beside it—the Campanile—was raised to his design.

When Giotto died in 1337, he was already regarded as the man who had revived the art of painting. The Renaissance masters two centuries later looked back to him as their forefather, and Vasari began his Lives of the artists with Giotto as the dawn of the rebirth. Few artists have so completely changed the course of their art.
