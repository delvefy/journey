Jan van Eyck

Early Netherlandish painter (c. 1390–1441)

Jan van Eyck, born around 1390 in the Low Countries, was the supreme master of early Netherlandish painting and one of the most influential artists in European history. By 1425 he had entered the service of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, who valued him so highly that he employed him not only as court painter but as a trusted servant, and even sent him abroad on confidential diplomatic missions.

Working in the wealthy Flemish cities of Bruges and Ghent, van Eyck brought the medium of oil paint to a perfection no one had achieved before. Layering translucent glazes, he rendered light, texture and surface—velvet, metal, glass, human skin—with a microscopic, almost miraculous realism. Tradition long credited him with inventing oil painting outright; in truth he transformed it into the dominant technique of Western art for centuries.

His masterpieces still astonish. The vast Ghent Altarpiece, completed in 1432 and begun with his brother Hubert, is among the most celebrated paintings in the world. His Arnolfini Portrait of 1434, with its convex mirror reflecting the whole room and its inscription "Jan van Eyck was here," is a landmark of Western portraiture and a puzzle still debated by scholars.

Van Eyck signed and dated his works—rare for the age—often with the motto "Als ich can" ("As best I can"). He died in Bruges in 1441, honoured and prosperous. His revolutionary naturalism set the course of Northern painting, taken up by Rogier van der Weyden and the generations of Flemish masters who followed.
