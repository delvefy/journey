Vespasian

Roman emperor, founder of the Flavian dynasty (9–79 AD)

Vespasian rose from modest Sabine origins to found a dynasty and restore the Roman state after the ruin of Nero's reign. A "new man" without grand ancestry, he made his name as a soldier—winning distinction in the conquest of Britain—and was entrusted by Nero with crushing the great Jewish revolt in Judaea, a war he prosecuted with steady success.

When Nero fell and the empire dissolved into the chaos of the Year of the Four Emperors, with Galba, Otho, and Vitellius rising and falling in swift succession, the eastern legions hailed Vespasian as emperor. His forces won the throne by the end of 69 AD, and he set about repairing what civil war and extravagance had broken.

As emperor he was frugal, shrewd, and famously down-to-earth, mending the bankrupt treasury with new taxes—when his son objected to a levy on public latrines, he held a coin to the boy's nose and observed that money carries no smell. He restored discipline and confidence, and began the vast amphitheatre, the Colosseum, that still stands as the symbol of imperial Rome. Even dying he kept his wit, joking that he supposed he was now becoming a god.

He left his son Titus to complete the conquest of Jerusalem, and a stable throne to pass to his heirs. Among the men whose fortunes he made was the captured Jewish commander who became the great historian of that war—Josephus.
