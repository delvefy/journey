Karl Marx

German philosopher and revolutionary theorist (1818–1883)

Karl Marx, born in 1818 in Trier in the Prussian Rhineland, was a philosopher, economist, and revolutionary whose theories became the foundation of communism and reshaped politics across the globe. He studied law and philosophy at Bonn and Berlin, where he absorbed and then radically transformed the dialectical philosophy of Hegel, turning it toward a materialist account of history.

Barred from academic life by his radicalism, Marx worked as a journalist until censorship and political pressure drove him into exile—first to Paris and Brussels, then permanently to London. In Paris he began his lifelong partnership with Friedrich Engels, who would support him financially and intellectually for decades. In 1848, amid a wave of European revolutions, the two issued The Communist Manifesto, with its ringing call, "Workers of the world, unite!"

Marx argued that history is driven by class struggle and that capitalism, by exploiting wage labor to extract surplus value, sows the seeds of its own overthrow. He devoted years in the reading room of the British Museum to his masterwork, Das Kapital, the first volume of which appeared in 1867. He also helped lead the International Workingmen's Association, the First International.

Living much of his life in poverty and grief—several of his children died young—Marx died in London in 1883 and was buried at Highgate Cemetery. Though he saw no revolution in his lifetime, his ideas inspired mass movements and states across the world, most consequentially the regimes built by Lenin and Stalin.
