Belshazzar

Co-regent of Babylon (6th century BC)

Belshazzar was the eldest son of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, and he ruled the great city as co-regent while his father lingered for years at a distant Arabian oasis. Babylonian inscriptions name him as crown prince and entrust the kingship to his hand, so that he governed in fact if not in title during the empire's final years.

It is the Book of Daniel that made him immortal. There he gives a sumptuous feast and, in his arrogance, calls for the sacred gold and silver vessels looted from the Temple of Jerusalem to serve as drinking cups. As the revelers profane them, a bodiless hand appears and writes upon the wall four mysterious words. None of the king's wise men can read them, until Daniel is summoned and declares their meaning: the days of the kingdom are numbered, the king has been weighed and found wanting, and his realm will be given to the Medes and Persians. That same night, the story says, Belshazzar was slain.

History records that Babylon did indeed fall, to the armies of Cyrus the Great in 539 BC, and the proud city passed without a long struggle into Persian hands. The scene of the doomed banquet so seized the imagination of later ages that it was painted by Rembrandt and set to music by Handel, and “the writing on the wall” became a proverb for impending ruin.

Behind the regent stood the true king, the strange and pious monarch who had left him to hold Babylon while he pursued his own devotions in the desert—his father, Nabonidus.
