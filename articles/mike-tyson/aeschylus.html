Aeschylus

Athenian tragic poet, "father of tragedy" (c. 525–456 BC)

Aeschylus, born around 525 BC near Athens, was the earliest of the three great masters of Greek tragedy and is justly called its father. Before he was a famous poet he was a soldier: he fought against the Persians at Marathon, where his brother fell, and probably again at Salamis, and his own epitaph boasted only of his courage in battle, saying nothing of the plays that made him immortal.

Of the seventy or more dramas he is said to have written, only seven survive. The earliest, The Persians, is unique among surviving tragedies in treating recent history rather than myth: it stages the defeat of Xerxes I at Salamis and summons up the ghost of the dead king Darius the Great to lament his son's folly. His masterpiece is the Oresteia, the only complete tragic trilogy to come down to us, which follows the bloody curse upon the house of Agamemnon to its resolution in the founding of a court of law at Athens.

Aeschylus transformed the theatre by adding a second actor, so that true dramatic conflict could unfold in dialogue rather than between a single speaker and the chorus. Grand and solemn in style, he called his works mere "slices from the great banquets of Homer," and Aristotle would later credit him with shaping the very form of tragedy.

He died around 456 BC in Sicily. A generation later the comic poet Aristophanes brought him back to the stage in The Frogs, crowning the old master victor in a contest of poets against the younger Euripides.
