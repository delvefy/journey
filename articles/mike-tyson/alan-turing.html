Alan Turing

English mathematician and computer scientist (1912–1954)

Alan Turing was born in London in 1912 and studied at Cambridge, where in 1936, still in his twenties, he wrote the paper that founded computer science. To settle a deep question posed by David Hilbert about the limits of formal reasoning, he imagined an abstract machine reading and writing symbols on a tape—the Turing machine—and proved that a single universal machine could carry out any computation whatever. He then took his doctorate at Princeton.

When war came, Turing went to Bletchley Park and became the central figure in breaking the German Enigma cipher, designing the electromechanical Bombe that read the enemy's secret traffic. His work is reckoned to have shortened the Second World War and saved countless lives; at a moment of crisis he and his colleagues wrote directly to Winston Churchill, who ordered that they be given everything they needed.

After the war he helped design early stored-program computers, proposed the test of machine intelligence that bears his name, and turned his mind to the mathematics of biological pattern and growth. His vision of the universal machine shaped the work of John von Neumann and the whole computing age.

In 1952 Turing was prosecuted for homosexuality, then a crime, and subjected to chemical treatment that left him broken. He died in 1954 of cyanide poisoning, an apparent suicide at forty-one. Decades later he received a royal pardon and was recognized as one of the founders of the modern world.
