Aristotle

Greek philosopher (384–322 BC)

Aristotle, born in 384 BC in the northern Greek town of Stagira, was a philosopher whose systematic inquiries shaped Western thought for two thousand years. At seventeen he traveled to Athens and entered Plato's Academy, where he studied and taught for some twenty years. After Plato's death he left Athens and, in time, was summoned to the Macedonian court to tutor the boy who would become Alexander the Great.

Returning to Athens, Aristotle founded his own school, the Lyceum, whose members were known as Peripatetics from his habit of teaching while walking. There he produced an astonishing body of work spanning nearly every field of knowledge. In logic he devised the syllogism and the first formal system of reasoning. In natural science he observed and classified animals with a care unmatched until modern times. In metaphysics he analyzed substance, cause, and potentiality, and argued for an unmoved mover.

His ethical writings, above all the Nicomachean Ethics, located human flourishing in the exercise of virtue as a mean between extremes, while his Politics examined the forms of the state and called man "a political animal." He also laid foundations for rhetoric and literary theory.

When anti-Macedonian feeling flared after Alexander's death, Aristotle withdrew from Athens—reportedly so the city would not "sin twice against philosophy," recalling the execution of Socrates—and died the following year, in 322 BC. Revered in the Middle Ages simply as "the Philosopher," his thought became central to Islamic scholars and to Christian theologians such as Thomas Aquinas.
