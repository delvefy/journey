James Monroe

Fifth President of the United States (1758–1831)

James Monroe was the last of the Revolutionary-generation presidents and the author of a doctrine that would guide American foreign policy for more than a century. A Virginian who left college to fight in the Revolution, he served under George Washington and was severely wounded at the Battle of Trenton after the crossing of the Delaware. He then studied law with Thomas Jefferson, whose protégé he became.

As a diplomat he helped negotiate the Louisiana Purchase, traveling to Paris to treat with the government of Napoleon and doubling the size of the nation at a stroke. Under James Madison he served as Secretary of State and of War through the War of 1812, and in 1816 he succeeded him as president.

His two terms were dubbed the Era of Good Feelings. He acquired Florida from Spain, presided over the fraught Missouri Compromise, and in 1823 proclaimed the Monroe Doctrine—largely shaped by his Secretary of State John Quincy Adams—warning Europe against new colonisation in the Americas.

Like Jefferson and Adams before him, he died on the Fourth of July, in 1831. Throughout his life he kept a warm friendship with the French hero of the American Revolution, whose family he had aided during the Terror and whose triumphal tour of the United States he welcomed as president—the Marquis de Lafayette.
