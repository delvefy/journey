Charles VI of France

King of France, "the Mad" (1368–1422)

Charles VI, born in 1368, succeeded his father Charles the Wise in 1380 as a boy of eleven. His early reign, dominated by grasping royal uncles, gave way in 1388 to a promising period of personal rule that won him the name "the Beloved." But in 1392, riding through a forest, the young king was seized by a sudden fit of madness in which he killed several of his own attendants. It was the first of dozens of such episodes that would recur for the rest of his life.

During his lucid intervals Charles ruled; during his long bouts of insanity—when he failed to recognise his wife, or believed himself made of glass and liable to shatter—France was governed by feuding relatives. The murderous rivalry between the king's brother Louis of Orléans and his cousins, the dukes of Burgundy, split the kingdom into the warring Armagnac and Burgundian factions and slid into open civil war.

This fatal weakness laid France open to invasion. In 1415 Henry V of England landed and destroyed the French nobility at Agincourt, then methodically conquered Normandy. With the Burgundians siding with the English, the broken Charles was forced into the humiliating Treaty of Troyes in 1420. By its terms he gave his daughter Catherine of Valois in marriage to Henry V, recognised the English king as his heir, and disinherited his own son, the Dauphin.

When Charles VI died in 1422, the infant Henry VI of England was proclaimed king of France in Paris, while the disinherited Dauphin clung to the lands south of the Loire. The mad king's reign had undone the patient work of his father and brought the French monarchy to the edge of extinction—an abyss from which only Joan of Arc and his exiled son would rescue it.
