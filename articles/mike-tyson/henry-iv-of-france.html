Henry IV of France

King of France, first of the Bourbons (1553–1610)

Henry was born in 1553 at Pau, son of the Queen of Navarre, and was raised a Protestant in the midst of France's ferocious Wars of Religion. As King of Navarre he became the political leader of the Huguenot cause. In 1572 his marriage to Margaret of Valois, daughter of the powerful queen mother Catherine de' Medici, drew Protestant nobles to Paris—and within days the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre slaughtered them in their thousands. Henry survived only by a feigned conversion to Catholicism.

The extinction of the Valois line left him heir to the throne, and when Henry III was assassinated in 1589 he became King Henry IV. Catholic France, however, refused a heretic king. After years of warfare and the long siege of Paris, he embraced Catholicism in 1593, reputedly judging that "Paris is worth a Mass," and was at last crowned.

He set about healing a ruined kingdom. The Edict of Nantes in 1598 granted the Protestants toleration and brought the religious wars to an end. With his able minister the Duke of Sully he restored the royal finances, encouraged agriculture and commerce, and wished, it was said, for every peasant to have a chicken in his pot.

Genial and pragmatic, "good King Henry" became the most beloved of French monarchs. His second marriage, to Marie de' Medici, produced the future Louis XIII and a dynasty that would culminate in Louis XIV. In 1610 he was stabbed to death in his carriage by the fanatic Ravaillac, mourned across France.
