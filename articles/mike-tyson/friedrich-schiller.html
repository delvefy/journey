Friedrich Schiller

German poet, playwright, and philosopher (1759–1805)

Friedrich Schiller was the great dramatist of German idealism and, with his friend Goethe, a founder of Weimar Classicism. Born at Marbach in Württemberg, he was pressed by his duke into a harsh military academy and trained as an army doctor. He escaped into literature with the incendiary Sturm und Drang drama The Robbers, whose success forced him to flee the duchy to avoid punishment.

There followed a succession of towering plays—Intrigue and Love, Don Carlos, the Wallenstein trilogy set in the Thirty Years' War, Mary Stuart, The Maid of Orleans, and the freedom-drama William Tell. He wrote history and a profound body of aesthetic philosophy, deeply shaped by Immanuel Kant, above all the Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man. His dramatic art took its measure from William Shakespeare, whom he and Goethe pressed upon the German stage.

From 1794 his alliance with Johann Wolfgang von Goethe became one of the most fertile friendships in literary history; together they sharpened each other's work and flooded the journals with ballads and epigrams. Schiller was ennobled in 1802 and died at Weimar three years later, only forty-five, of the tuberculosis that had long wasted him.

His verse and ideals echoed onward: Ludwig van Beethoven set his Ode to Joy as the choral climax of the Ninth Symphony. And his dramas of liberty and doomed nobility gave the opera house some of its grandest subjects, taken up above all by Giuseppe Verdi.
