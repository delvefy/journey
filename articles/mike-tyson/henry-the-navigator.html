Henry the Navigator

Portuguese prince and patron of discovery (1394–1460)

Prince Henry, called the Navigator, was the royal patron whose vision and persistence launched the European Age of Discovery. A younger son of King John I of Portugal and of Philippa of Lancaster, he first won renown at the storming of the Moorish stronghold of Ceuta in 1415. From that conquest grew a lifelong ambition to push Portuguese ships ever farther into the unknown waters along the western coast of Africa.

Though he himself rarely went to sea, Henry gathered around him at the southern tip of Portugal a community of mariners, mapmakers, and shipwrights, financing voyage after voyage from the revenues of the religious order he headed. His captains perfected the nimble caravel, colonized the Atlantic islands of Madeira and the Azores, and at last rounded the dreaded Cape Bojador, which sailors had believed marked the edge of the navigable world.

Beyond it they opened the coast of Guinea to Portuguese trade—in gold, and, more darkly, in enslaved Africans, beginning a traffic that would stain the centuries to come. Step by step, his expeditions extended European knowledge of the African shore and built the confidence and the craft that would later carry Portuguese sails all the way to India and beyond.

Henry died in 1460, the father of an enterprise that would make a small kingdom into a global empire. Through his mother he was the grandson of one of the mightiest noblemen of medieval England, the great Duke of Lancaster—John of Gaunt.
