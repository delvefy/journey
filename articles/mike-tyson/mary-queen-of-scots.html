Mary, Queen of Scots

Queen of Scotland (1542–1587)

Mary, Queen of Scots, was one of the most tragic and romantic figures in British history, a queen whose beauty, misjudgments, and long captivity ended on the scaffold. She became queen of Scotland when only six days old, on the death of her father James V. Sent for safety to France and raised at its glittering court, she married the Dauphin and was for a brief time also queen of France, until his early death left her a widow at eighteen.

Returning in 1561 to a Scotland newly won for Protestantism, the Catholic Mary struggled to rule a divided land and endured the public scoldings of the reformer John Knox. Her marriage to the vain Lord Darnley gave her a son but soon turned poisonous; Darnley was murdered in 1567, and Mary's hasty marriage to the Earl of Bothwell, widely blamed for the crime, ruined her. Her nobles rose in revolt, and she was forced to abdicate in favor of her infant son.

Escaping captivity, defeated in battle, Mary fled across the border to throw herself on the mercy of her cousin Elizabeth I. It was a fatal miscalculation. As a Catholic with a claim to the English throne, she was too dangerous to free and too royal to kill easily; Elizabeth held her prisoner for nearly nineteen years. At last, implicated in a plot against Elizabeth's life, Mary was tried and beheaded at Fotheringhay in 1587, meeting death with great dignity.

Her cause triumphed in the end through her blood: the son she had last seen as a baby grew up to rule Scotland and then to inherit England itself, uniting the two crowns—James VI and I.
