Richard Burton

Welsh actor of stage and screen (1925–1984)

Richard Burton rose from the coalfields of Wales to become one of the most magnetic actors of his generation, famed above all for his extraordinary voice—deep, musical, and capable of filling a theatre with a whisper. Born Richard Jenkins, the son of a miner and one of thirteen children, he was lifted out of poverty by a devoted schoolmaster whose surname he gratefully took.

He made his name on the stage as a thrilling interpreter of Shakespeare, with admired performances of Hamlet, Henry V, and Coriolanus that marked him as a successor to the great classical tradition. In the cinema he starred in Becket, The Spy Who Came in from the Cold, Where Eagles Dare, and the colossal Cleopatra, earning seven Academy Award nominations—though, famously, never the prize itself.

It was on Cleopatra that he met Elizabeth Taylor, and their love affair and twice-made, twice-broken marriage became the most sensational celebrity drama of the age. A man of large appetites and a famous drinker, he squandered some of his gifts, yet at his best he was incomparable, and he remained a beloved reader of poetry, especially the work of his countryman Dylan Thomas.

Throughout his career his talent was set beside that of the man universally hailed as the supreme actor of the twentieth century, the standard by which all the others were judged—Laurence Olivier.
