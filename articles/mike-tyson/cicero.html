Cicero

Roman orator and statesman (106–43 BC)

Marcus Tullius Cicero, born in 106 BC at Arpinum, was the greatest orator of ancient Rome and one of its most influential writers and thinkers. A "new man" without noble ancestry, he rose through sheer talent in the law courts to reach the consulship in 63 BC, in which year he exposed and crushed the conspiracy of Catiline, denouncing the plotters in speeches still studied as models of rhetoric.

A devoted defender of the Roman Republic and the authority of the Senate, Cicero spent his career resisting the concentration of power in single men. He navigated warily around the rivalry of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus, and after Caesar's assassination he threw himself into a last campaign for the old order, attacking Mark Antony in a series of furious orations he called the Philippics. He pinned his hopes on the young Octavian, the future Augustus—who soon abandoned him. In 43 BC, proscribed by the new rulers, Cicero was hunted down and killed, his head and hands displayed in the Forum.

His true monument is his writing. A vast body of speeches, letters, and treatises survives, and through works such as On Duties and On the Republic he carried Greek philosophy—Plato's above all, alongside Stoic and Academic thought—into the Latin world and refined Latin prose into a supple instrument.

His influence echoed for ages: his rediscovered letters helped spark the Renaissance, and his lost dialogue Hortensius turned the young Augustine toward philosophy and faith.
