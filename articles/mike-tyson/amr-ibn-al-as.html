Amr ibn al-As

Arab general, conqueror of Egypt (c. 573–664)

Amr ibn al-As, born around 573, was one of the ablest generals and shrewdest politicians of early Islam, remembered above all as the conqueror of Egypt. A leading man of Mecca who had at first fought against the Muslims, he converted shortly before the conquest of Mecca and quickly became a trusted commander under the Prophet Muhammad.

His great achievement came under the caliph Umar. With a small army, Amr invaded Byzantine Egypt around 640 and, in a swift campaign, wrested the rich province from the empire of Heraclius, taking the fortress of Babylon and the great city of Alexandria. He founded a new garrison town, Fustat, on the Nile, which would grow into Cairo, and he governed Egypt as its first Muslim ruler.

Amr was as cunning in politics as in war. Dismissed from his governorship by the caliph Uthman, he nursed his grievances, and after Uthman's murder he threw in his lot with Mu'awiya against Ali in the first Muslim civil war. At the Battle of Siffin in 657 it was Amr who reputedly devised the famous stratagem of fixing pages of the Quran to the soldiers' lances to halt the fighting and force arbitration—a ploy that worked to Mu'awiya's advantage. As Mu'awiya's chief negotiator, he helped secure the Umayyad triumph.

His reward was the return of Egypt: Mu'awiya made him governor once more, and Amr ruled the province until his death around 664. A worldly, witty and pragmatic man, Amr ibn al-As stands as one of the architects of the Arab empire—the soldier-statesman who brought Egypt into the world of Islam.
