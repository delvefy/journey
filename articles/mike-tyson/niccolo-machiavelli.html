Niccolò Machiavelli

Florentine statesman and political philosopher (1469–1527)

Niccolò Machiavelli, born in Florence in 1469, was a diplomat and writer whose hard-eyed analysis of power founded modern political thought. After the fall of the firebrand friar Savonarola, he rose to become a senior official of the Florentine Republic, serving for fourteen years as its secretary and envoy. His missions carried him across Italy and Europe, where he studied rulers at close range—none more closely than the ruthless and brilliant Cesare Borgia, whose methods both repelled and fascinated him, and at whose court he crossed paths with Leonardo da Vinci.

When the Medici overthrew the republic in 1512, Machiavelli's world collapsed. Dismissed from office, imprisoned, and tortured on suspicion of conspiracy, he was released into bitter rural exile. There, in the evenings, he put on his finest clothes to "converse" with the ancients in his books, and poured his disillusioned wisdom onto the page.

The result was The Prince, a short, shocking manual of statecraft that set aside pious ideals to ask how power is really won and kept. A ruler, he argued, must know when not to be good, for it is safer to be feared than loved, and the security of the state may justify cruelty and deceit—ideas that made "Machiavellian" a byword for cunning. In his longer Discourses on Livy he revealed himself, by contrast, a passionate admirer of republics.

Never fully restored to favor, Machiavelli died in 1527, leaving works that still define the study of politics.
