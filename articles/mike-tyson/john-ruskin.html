John Ruskin

English art critic and social thinker (1819–1900)

John Ruskin was the most influential art critic of the Victorian age, a writer who moved from the defence of painting to a sweeping moral critique of industrial society.

The only child of a prosperous London wine merchant, he was raised in earnest evangelical comfort and travelled widely through the Alps and Italy, schooling his extraordinary eye. He burst into fame with Modern Painters, a multi-volume work begun as a passionate defence of a single landscape painter and expanded into a meditation on art, nature and truth. In The Stones of Venice he exalted the Gothic and the freedom of the medieval craftsman, ideas that would inspire William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement, and he became the great early champion of the Pre-Raphaelite painters.

As Slade Professor at Oxford he lectured a generation that included the young Oscar Wilde, and he provoked one of the most famous quarrels in art when he accused the painter Whistler of “flinging a pot of paint in the public's face,” losing the resulting libel suit for a farthing. But his deepest turn was toward social criticism: in Unto This Last he denounced the cruelties of laissez-faire economics and pleaded for justice to the worker. That little book changed lives across the world—Leo Tolstoy revered it, and Mahatma Gandhi, reading it on a train in South Africa, remade his whole philosophy around it.

His private life was unhappy, his marriage annulled, his old age clouded by mental breakdown at his home in the Lake District. He died in 1900, his prose later lovingly translated into French by Marcel Proust. The painter whose genius had launched his career, and whom he defended above all others, was J. M. W. Turner.
