Richard the Lionheart

King of England and crusader (1157–1199)

Richard I, called the Lionheart for his courage, was a king of England who scarcely lived in England, regarding his island realm chiefly as a treasury to fund his wars. A son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, he learned soldiering young in the turbulent south of France, rebelled against his own father alongside his brothers, and came to the throne in 1189 already famous as the finest warrior of his line.

His great enterprise was the Third Crusade, launched to recover Jerusalem after its fall to the Muslims. Sailing east, he stormed Acre, smashed the enemy in open battle at Arsuf, and recovered the coast of the Holy Land. Twice his army came within sight of Jerusalem, and twice he judged it could not be held; in 1192 he made a truce that left the city in Muslim hands but secured safe passage for Christian pilgrims.

The journey home brought disaster. Shipwrecked and traveling in disguise, he was seized by an enemy he had insulted on crusade and handed to the German emperor, who held him until England paid a crippling ransom. Freed at last, Richard spent his remaining years fighting the King of France to defend his French inheritance, raising the great fortress of Château Gaillard above the Seine.

In 1199, besieging a trifling castle in Limousin, he was struck by a crossbow bolt and died of the wound, leaving his throne to his brother John. The Muslim sultan against whom he had matched arms and courtesy before the walls of Jerusalem, and whose magnanimity matched his own, was Saladin.
