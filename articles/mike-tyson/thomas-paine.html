Thomas Paine

Anglo-American revolutionary writer (1737–1809)

Thomas Paine was the great pamphleteer of the age of revolutions, a man whose plain, fierce prose helped set two continents ablaze. Born in England, the son of a corset-maker, he drifted through failed trades until he met Benjamin Franklin in London, who encouraged him to seek his fortune in America. He arrived in 1774, just as the colonies neared their breach with Britain.

In January 1776 he published Common Sense, a short, blazing argument for independence that sold in staggering numbers and did more than any other writing to turn American opinion toward revolution. Through the war his American Crisis papers—"These are the times that try men's souls"—were read aloud to George Washington's weary soldiers, and his cause was embraced by Thomas Jefferson.

Returning to Europe, Paine answered Edmund Burke's attack on the French Revolution with Rights of Man, a ringing defense of republican liberty that made him a hunted man in Britain. He fled to France, won a seat in the National Convention, but—having opposed the execution of the king—was imprisoned during Robespierre's Terror and narrowly escaped the guillotine. His later Age of Reason, a deist assault on organized religion, cost him many friends.

He died poor and largely scorned in America in 1809, his vast influence only later acknowledged. Among the fellow radicals of his London circle who also took up the pen against Burke—and went further, to claim the rights of women—was Mary Wollstonecraft.
