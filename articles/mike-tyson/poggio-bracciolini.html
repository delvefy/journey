Poggio Bracciolini

Italian humanist and book-hunter (1380–1459)

Poggio Bracciolini did as much as any man to recover the lost literature of the ancient world. A Tuscan notary's son, he rose through skill with the pen to become a secretary at the papal court, where he served pope after pope for decades. He perfected a clear, graceful style of handwriting that scribes and, soon after, printers adopted as their model—the ancestor of the roman type still read today.

His great passion was hunting for manuscripts. When the Council of Constance left him idle, he ranged through the neglected libraries of remote monasteries and brought back to the light works that had been all but lost: the complete Institutes of Quintilian, forgotten orations of Cicero, the architecture of Vitruvius, and—most fateful of all—in 1417 the only surviving copy of Lucretius's great poem on the nature of things, whose bold vision of a universe of atoms would unsettle and inspire the centuries to come.

Poggio was also a writer of spirit and spite: moral dialogues, a notorious book of witty and bawdy anecdotes, and venomous invectives, above all against his rival Lorenzo Valla. In his last years he returned to Florence as its chancellor and wrote a history of the city.

Through his discoveries the voices of antiquity spoke again to the Renaissance. The Roman poet whose vanished masterpiece he rescued from a German monastery, and who had sung of atoms and the fearless life, was Lucretius.
