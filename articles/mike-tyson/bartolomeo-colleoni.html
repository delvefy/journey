Bartolomeo Colleoni

Italian condottiero (c. 1400–1475)

Bartolomeo Colleoni, born to the minor nobility of Bergamo, became one of the great mercenary generals of fifteenth-century Italy, a master of the new warfare in which hired companies and field cannon decided the fates of cities. Like all condottieri he sold his sword where the pay and prospects were best, serving the dukes of Milan—among them Filippo Maria Visconti—and the rising Francesco Sforza, before he settled at last into the long service of the Republic of Venice.

As Venice's captain-general he commanded its armies for decades, growing enormously rich on the proceeds, and in semi-retirement he kept a brilliant court at his castle of Malpaga. He was a careful, professional soldier in an age of shifting loyalties, valued as much for his reliability as for his skill.

His wealth he turned to memory. At Bergamo he built a sumptuous chapel to hold his tomb, and on his death in 1475 he left the bulk of his fortune to Venice on a single proud condition: that the republic raise an equestrian statue in his honor. Venice took the money and, with characteristic guile, set the monument not in the forbidden Piazza San Marco but in a square nearby.

That statue, cast in bronze by a Florentine master, made his arrogant figure immortal. He thus joined a select company of condottieri honored with such a monument—the first and most famous of whom, raised a generation earlier at Padua, was the captain known as Gattamelata.
