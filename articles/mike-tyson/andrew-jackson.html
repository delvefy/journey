Andrew Jackson

Seventh President of the United States (1767–1845)

Andrew Jackson was the dominant American politician of his era and the embodiment of a new, rougher democracy. Born on the Carolina frontier and orphaned during the Revolution, in which he served as a boy, he rose as a Tennessee lawyer, planter, and militia general. His shattering defeat of the British at New Orleans in January 1815 made him a national hero and earned him the nickname Old Hickory.

After narrowly losing the disputed 1824 election in the House to John Quincy Adams, he built a mass political movement—the Democratic Party—and swept into the presidency in 1828, casting himself as champion of the common man against entrenched elites and heir to the democratic spirit of Thomas Jefferson.

His two terms were combative. He faced down South Carolina in the Nullification Crisis with a ringing defence of the Union, and he waged and won a war against the Second Bank of the United States. But he also drove the Indian Removal Act and the brutal expulsions remembered as the Trail of Tears, a lasting stain on his record.

His forceful style left a deep mark: Abraham Lincoln would later cite his stand for the Union against secession, and much later Donald Trump would claim him as a populist forerunner. He died at his Hermitage estate in 1845. His most persistent political adversary across these years was Henry Clay.
