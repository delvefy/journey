Éamon de Valera

Irish statesman, Taoiseach and President (1882–1975)

Éamon de Valera was the towering and divisive figure who dominated independent Ireland for half a century, shaping its constitution, its neutrality, and its very idea of itself.

Born in New York to an Irish mother and a Spanish father and raised in County Limerick, he became a mathematics teacher before the cause of Irish freedom claimed him. He commanded a garrison in the 1916 Easter Rising and was sentenced to death, but the sentence was commuted—helped by his American birth—and he emerged as the political leader of the independence movement. As president of the revolutionary Dáil he dispatched the delegation that negotiated the 1921 treaty, then repudiated its compromise, breaking with Michael Collins and leading the losing republican side in the bitter civil war that followed.

Returning to constitutional politics, he founded the Fianna Fáil party and took power in 1932. As head of government for most of the next two decades he dismantled the treaty's links to the Crown, wrote the 1937 constitution that remains the basis of the Irish state, and waged an economic war with Britain. During the Second World War he held Ireland firmly neutral, clashing famously with Winston Churchill—who had years earlier helped negotiate with him through David Lloyd George—and answering Churchill's wartime taunts with a dignified broadcast that won him wide admiration at home.

He served finally as President of Ireland into his nineties, the last surviving commandant of the Rising. The leader under whom he had first taken up arms in 1916, the poet who proclaimed the Republic outside the General Post Office, was Patrick Pearse.
