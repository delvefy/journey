Niels Bohr

Danish physicist (1885–1962)

Niels Bohr was born in Copenhagen in 1885 and, after working in England under Ernest Rutherford, made in 1913 one of the decisive discoveries of modern physics. He proposed that the electrons of an atom move only in certain fixed orbits and emit or absorb energy in discrete jumps between them, a model that explained the spectral lines of hydrogen and married Rutherford's nuclear atom to the new quantum theory. It won him the Nobel Prize in 1922.

In Copenhagen he founded an institute that became the world center of theoretical physics, drawing the brilliant young men—Heisenberg, Pauli, Dirac and others—who built quantum mechanics. From their work Bohr drew the principle of complementarity and the interpretation, named for his city, that the act of measurement itself shapes the quantum world.

This view set him against Albert Einstein, and their long, courteous, and profound debates over whether physics could be reduced to probabilities—Einstein protesting that God does not play dice—became legendary.

Of partly Jewish descent, Bohr fled occupied Denmark in 1943 and briefly lent his counsel to the Allied bomb project, having two years earlier held a tense, mysterious wartime meeting in Copenhagen with his former pupil Werner Heisenberg. After the war he pressed for openness among nations and the peaceful use of atomic energy. He died in 1962.
