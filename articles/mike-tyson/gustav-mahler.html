Gustav Mahler

Austrian composer and conductor (1860–1911)

Gustav Mahler stood at the threshold between the Romantic age and the modern, a composer who poured the whole of life—nature, love, terror, and transcendence—into vast symphonies. Born to a German-speaking Jewish family in Bohemia and trained at the Vienna Conservatory, he made his living as one of the most demanding and brilliant conductors of his time.

He rose to direct the Vienna Court Opera, the summit of the European musical world, a post for which, barred by antisemitism, he converted to Catholicism; there he revolutionised staging before crossing to conduct in New York. Composing was the work of his summers. In nine completed symphonies and song cycles such as Kindertotenlieder and Das Lied von der Erde he aimed, as he said, to build a world that embraced everything.

His symphonic ambition measured itself against Ludwig van Beethoven, whose shadow—and the superstition that no great composer survived a Ninth Symphony—haunted him. Largely dismissed as a composer in his lifetime even as he was revered as a conductor, his music would triumph only decades after his death.

His last years were shadowed by heart disease and by a marital crisis with his young wife Alma. In the summer of 1910, in deep distress, he sought out the founder of psychoanalysis for counsel, walking for hours in conversation with Sigmund Freud. Mahler died in Vienna the following year, not yet fifty-one.
