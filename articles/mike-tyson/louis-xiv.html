Louis XIV

King of France, "the Sun King" (1638–1715)

Louis XIV, born in 1638, became king of France at the age of four and reigned for seventy-two years, the longest of any major European monarch and the very model of absolute kingship. During his childhood the realm was governed by his mother and by Cardinal Mazarin, heir to Richelieu, and the noble revolts of the Fronde left the boy with a lifelong horror of disorder and a determination to master his kingdom utterly. When Mazarin died in 1661 he astonished his court by announcing that henceforth he would rule alone.

Taking the sun as his emblem, Louis made himself the dazzling centre of France. He built the vast palace of Versailles and drew the great nobles there, taming them with the elaborate etiquette of his court while able ministers such as Colbert rebuilt the finances and industry of the state. Under his patronage France became the cultural arbiter of Europe and its language the tongue of diplomacy; he protected the playwright Molière and gathered around him a brilliant company of writers, composers, and artists in the age that bears his name.

Abroad he waged almost continual war to expand France's frontiers and glory, culminating in the long and exhausting War of the Spanish Succession, fought to place his grandson on the throne of Spain against a grand coalition. At home his revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 stripped France's Protestants of toleration and drove many of his most industrious subjects into exile.

Magnificent and ruinous in equal measure, his reign left France pre-eminent in arts and arms but drained by debt. He outlived his son and grandson and was succeeded by a great-grandson. The grandeur of his century was later chronicled with admiration by Voltaire.
