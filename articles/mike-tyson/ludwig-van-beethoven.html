Ludwig van Beethoven

German composer (1770–1827)

Ludwig van Beethoven was the colossus who stood at the turning point between the Classical and Romantic ages of music, transforming what music could express. Born in Bonn and driven hard as a child by an ambitious father, he moved as a young man to Vienna—the musical capital of Europe—to study with Joseph Haydn, and soon made his name as a thunderous virtuoso of the piano and a composer of startling originality.

His output reshaped every form he touched. The nine symphonies—among them the heroic Eroica, the fate-hammering Fifth, and the choral Ninth, which sets Schiller's "Ode to Joy"—expanded the scale and ambition of the orchestra forever. To these he added thirty-two piano sonatas, five piano concertos, the opera Fidelio, the towering Missa Solemnis, and the visionary late string quartets.

All this he achieved while losing the very sense most precious to a musician. From his late twenties his hearing failed, and he sank at times into despair; yet, almost wholly deaf, he composed his most profound works in an inner silence. He had meant to dedicate the Eroica to Napoleon as a champion of liberty—then furiously struck out the name when Napoleon made himself emperor.

When he died in Vienna in 1827, a vast crowd followed his coffin. The supreme master of the generation before him, whose perfection Beethoven revered and sought to rival, was Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart.
