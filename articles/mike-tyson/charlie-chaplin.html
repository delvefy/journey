Charlie Chaplin

English actor and filmmaker (1889–1977)

Charlie Chaplin was the defining genius of silent cinema, whose figure of the Little Tramp made him perhaps the most famous and beloved film star the world has ever known.

Rising from a Dickensian London childhood of poverty and the music hall, he came to America and created in the bowler-hatted, cane-twirling Tramp a character of universal appeal, blending slapstick with extraordinary pathos. As writer, director, composer and star he made masterpiece after masterpiece—The Kid, The Gold Rush, City Lights, Modern Times—and in 1919 he co-founded the studio United Artists to control his own work. His art would echo down the decades to physical comedians as distant as Jackie Chan.

He used his fame with daring: in The Great Dictator he savagely lampooned Adolf Hitler, the tyrant who shared his birth week and his toothbrush moustache, in a plea against fascism. He moved among the towering minds of his age, counting Albert Einstein a friend and admirer. But his leftist sympathies made him a target in the McCarthy era, and he left the United States to spend his last decades in Switzerland, honored at the end with a knighthood and a special Academy Award.

The other supreme genius of silent comedy—his great contemporary, the stone-faced acrobat of film with whom he at last shared the screen in Limelight—was Buster Keaton.
