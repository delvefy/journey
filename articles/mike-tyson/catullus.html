Catullus

Roman poet (c. 84–54 BC)

Gaius Valerius Catullus, born at Verona around 84 BC, was the most original lyric voice of the late Roman Republic. Coming to Rome from the provinces, he became the leading light of the "new poets" who, taking the Alexandrian Callimachus as their master, scorned the old long-winded epic in favour of short, polished, intensely personal poems—learned, witty, and charged with feeling.

His fame rests above all on the cycle addressed to "Lesbia," a name chosen in homage to Sappho of Lesbos. In these poems he traced a love affair through ecstasy, jealousy, and bitter disillusion, coining lines that have never lost their force—"let us live and love," "I hate and I love," and his cry for a thousand and then a hundred kisses. One of them is a direct rendering into Latin of Sappho's most famous ode.

Catullus could also be savage. He flung obscene and witty invective at the great men of his day, sparing not even Julius Caesar and his favourite Mamurra; Caesar admitted the verses had marked him, yet, when the poet apologised, dined with him the same day. A gentler poem hailed the orator Cicero as the most eloquent of Romans.

He died young, perhaps around thirty, leaving a slim book of poems that shaped all later Roman love elegy and, rediscovered in the Renaissance, the lyric of Europe. The model for his most intimate verse was the archaic Greek poetess Sappho.
