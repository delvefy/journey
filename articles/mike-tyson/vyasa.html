Vyasa

Sage and traditional compiler of the Hindu scriptures

Vyasa, called Veda Vyasa, "the arranger of the Vedas," is the sage to whom Hindu tradition ascribes the compilation of its greatest scriptures. His historical existence is uncertain—the name may stand for a whole tradition of editors rather than a single man—but he is honoured as one of the towering figures of Indian civilization, invoked wherever the Sanskrit canon is studied.

Tradition holds that the sacred knowledge was once a single undivided body, and that Vyasa parted it into the four collections—Rig, Yajur, Sama, and Atharva—so that it might be preserved and taught. For this work of ordering he is said to have received the title by which he is known.

He is revered above all as the author of the Mahabharata, the immense epic of the war of the Bharatas, within which is set the Bhagavad Gita, the most celebrated of Hindu sacred poems. By the story he was himself bound to the royal line whose fate the epic recounts. To him are also attributed the eighteen Puranas, the vast storehouses of myth and devotion, among them the Bhagavata Purana.

Hindu philosophy likewise traces to him the Brahma Sutras—the aphorisms, ascribed to Badarayana and traditionally identified with Vyasa, that systematize the teaching of the Upanishads and became the foundation of all Vedanta. The great schools of later thought are in large part rival readings of this one root text.

Whether one man or many, "Vyasa" stands at the source of the Sanskrit tradition, and the full-moon festival of Guru Purnima is kept in his honour. His Brahma Sutras would be expounded by the supreme philosophers of India, foremost among them Adi Shankara.
