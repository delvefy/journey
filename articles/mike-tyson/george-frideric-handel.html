George Frideric Handel

German-British Baroque composer (1685–1759)

George Frideric Handel was, with Bach, one of the twin giants of the late Baroque—born in the same year, 1685, in the same corner of Germany, though the two never met. Where Bach spent his life in German church lofts, Handel became a man of the wider world: he mastered Italian opera in his youth in Italy, then settled in London, where he reigned for decades as the most celebrated composer in Britain and a favorite of its Hanoverian kings.

For the court he wrote the buoyant Water Music and the Music for the Royal Fireworks; for the London stage a stream of Italian operas such as Rinaldo and Giulio Cesare. When public taste turned against Italian opera, Handel reinvented himself, creating the English oratorio—and with it his immortal Messiah of 1742, whose Hallelujah Chorus remains among the most beloved music ever written.

Grand, dramatic, and inexhaustibly melodic, his music made him a national institution. He went blind in his last years yet continued to perform, and at his death in 1759 he was buried with honor in Westminster Abbey. Later masters revered him without reserve: Beethoven called him the greatest of all composers, and Mozart re-orchestrated his Messiah.

Handel's grand style had been shaped in his Italian years by the brilliant concerto writing of the age, above all by the red-haired Venetian priest whose music swept Europe—Antonio Vivaldi.
