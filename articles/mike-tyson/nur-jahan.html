Nur Jahan

Mughal empress, power behind the throne of Jahangir (1577–1645)

Nur Jahan—“Light of the World”—was the chief wife of the emperor Jahangir and the most powerful woman the Mughal Empire ever knew. Born Mehr-un-Nissa to a distinguished Persian family that had sought its fortune in India, where her father rose to be the emperor's chief minister, she was a widow when Jahangir married her in 1611 and made her his favourite above all others.

As the emperor sank into ill health and the pleasures of wine and opium, the reins of government passed into her capable hands. Coins were struck in her name and imperial decrees issued under her seal—honours given to no other Mughal woman—and she led a dominant family faction that effectively ruled the empire in his stead.

She was as accomplished as she was powerful: a celebrated markswoman said to have shot tigers, a patron of poets, a designer of cloth and gardens, and a discerning builder. The delicate marble tomb she raised for her father at Agra, inlaid with precious stones, is regarded as a forerunner of the Taj Mahal itself.

When Jahangir died in 1627, she was outmanoeuvred in the struggle for the succession—betrayed in part by her own brother—and withdrew from power, spending her last years quietly at Lahore, where she designed her own resting place. Her niece, who became the cherished wife of the next emperor and the inspiration for the world's most famous tomb, was Mumtaz Mahal.
