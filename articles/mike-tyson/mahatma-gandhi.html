Mahatma Gandhi

Leader of Indian independence (1869–1948)

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, born in 1869 in the coastal town of Porbandar, led India to independence from British rule through a philosophy of nonviolent resistance that reshaped the twentieth century. Trained as a lawyer in London, he sailed to South Africa in 1893 and spent twenty-one years there fighting racial discrimination, developing his method of satyagraha—truth-force, or mass civil disobedience grounded in moral courage.

Returning to India in 1915, Gandhi rose to lead the Indian National Congress and transformed the independence struggle into a movement of millions. He launched the Non-Cooperation Movement against colonial institutions and, in 1930, the famous Salt March, walking 240 miles to the sea to defy the British salt monopoly. His repeated imprisonments only deepened his moral authority.

Living with deliberate simplicity—spinning his own cloth, fasting, and wearing a homespun loincloth—he became known as Mahatma, "the great soul." He campaigned tirelessly against the caste system's treatment of "untouchables," whom he called Harijans, and insisted on unity between Hindus and Muslims.

The Quit India Movement of 1942 demanded an immediate end to British rule. Independence came in 1947, but at the terrible cost of partition into India and Pakistan, accompanied by communal massacres that broke Gandhi's heart. He fasted to halt the violence. In January 1948 he was assassinated in Delhi by a Hindu nationalist who resented his conciliation toward Muslims. His example of disciplined nonviolence inspired later movements led by figures such as Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela.
