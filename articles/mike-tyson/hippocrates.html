Hippocrates

Greek physician (c. 460–370 BC)

Hippocrates of Kos, who lived from about 460 to 370 BC, is honored as the father of Western medicine. Born on the Greek island of Kos, he was already famous in his own lifetime—the philosopher Plato names him as the model physician—yet so much legend gathered around him that the details of his life are now hard to recover.

His enduring importance lies in a revolution of attitude. The school of medicine associated with his name insisted that disease arises from natural causes rather than the anger of the gods, and that the physician's task is patient observation: noting symptoms, recording the course of an illness, and offering a reasoned prognosis. Treatment favored rest, diet, cleanliness, and trust in the healing power of nature. The tradition's theory of the four humors—blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile—would dominate medical thinking for two thousand years.

A large body of texts known as the Hippocratic Corpus survives under his name, though it was clearly composed by many hands across generations. From this tradition comes the Hippocratic Oath, the ancient pledge of medical ethics whose spirit—above all the duty to do no harm—physicians still invoke today.

Whatever the precise share of the historical man, the Hippocratic legacy set medicine on the path of careful, ethical, evidence-based practice. It was gathered and systematized by Galen in the Roman age and carried into later centuries by physicians such as Avicenna.
