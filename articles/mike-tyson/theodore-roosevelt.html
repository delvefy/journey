Theodore Roosevelt

26th President of the United States (1858–1919)

Theodore Roosevelt was the most exuberant and energetic of American presidents, a man who turned a sickly, asthmatic boyhood into a lifelong cult of "the strenuous life." Before he was forty he had been a Dakota rancher, a prolific author, a New York police commissioner, and Assistant Secretary of the Navy; then, in the war with Spain in 1898, he raised and led the volunteer "Rough Riders" and won national fame with a celebrated charge up the heights of San Juan.

Vice-president under McKinley, he became, at forty-two, the youngest president in history when an assassin's bullet struck down his chief in 1901. In office he pursued a "Square Deal" for ordinary Americans, breaking up the great trusts, regulating the railroads and the purity of food and drugs, and mediating between capital and labour. Above all he was the great conservationist, setting aside vast tracts of forest and wilderness and founding national parks for the generations to come.

Abroad he wielded a big stick: he built up the navy, engineered the Panama Canal, and won the Nobel Peace Prize for ending the war between Russia and Japan—the first American so honoured. Leaving office in 1909, he later broke with his successor and ran again as the "Bull Moose" candidate, surviving a gunshot to finish a speech.

His face was later carved upon Mount Rushmore, and his cousin Franklin would follow him to the White House. As a small boy he had watched from a window the funeral procession of the president he revered above all, Abraham Lincoln.
