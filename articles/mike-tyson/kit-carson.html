Kit Carson

American frontiersman and scout (1809–1868)

Christopher “Kit” Carson was the most famous frontiersman of the American West, a mountain man, scout, and soldier whose exploits, real and embroidered, made him a legend in his own lifetime. Raised in Missouri, he ran away as a teenager to join a caravan bound for Santa Fe and spent his early years trapping beaver through the Rocky Mountains, learning the languages and trails of the West and the peoples who lived there.

His fame was made when he served as guide to John C. Frémont's western expeditions in the 1840s. Frémont's widely read reports cast the quiet, illiterate Carson as a hero of the wilderness, and dime novels soon spun his name into tall tales. In the Mexican-American War he guided American forces to California and made daring rides through enemy country to carry word of their plight.

He later served as a federal agent to the Ute and, in the Civil War, as a Union officer in New Mexico, fighting Confederate invaders at Valverde. His most controversial service came in 1864, when he led a punishing scorched-earth campaign against the Navajo that ended in their forced removal—the tragic Long Walk—a dark chapter that shadows his reputation.

He died in Colorado in 1868, worn out at fifty-eight. Long before his expeditions with Frémont, he had learned his craft in the fur-trade brotherhood of the mountain men, whose most renowned figure alongside him was Jim Bridger.
