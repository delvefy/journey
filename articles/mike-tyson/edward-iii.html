Edward III

King of England (1312–1377)

Edward III came to the throne at fourteen, in the wake of a revolution. His father, Edward II, had been deposed and soon murdered, and for three years the boy king was a figurehead while his mother, Isabella of France, and her lover Roger Mortimer ruled in his name. In 1330, not yet eighteen, Edward struck back, seizing Mortimer by night in Nottingham Castle and sending him to the gallows. From that moment he reigned in his own right for nearly half a century.

His great enterprise was the war with France. Claiming the French crown through his mother, daughter of Philip IV, he launched in 1337 the long struggle later called the Hundred Years' War. The English won stunning victories: the sea fight at Sluys in 1340, the slaughter of the French chivalry by the longbow at Crécy in 1346, and the year-long siege that delivered Calais. His son, the Black Prince, crowned the triumph at Poitiers, where the King of France himself was taken prisoner.

Edward clothed his monarchy in the glamour of chivalry, founding the Order of the Garter and styling his court a new Round Table. Yet his reign also saw the horror of the Black Death, which carried off perhaps a third of his people.

His last years were a long decline into senility, dominated by a grasping mistress while a critical Parliament chafed and the Black Prince died before him. Among the servants of his glittering court was the poet Geoffrey Chaucer. The grandfather whose conquests in Wales and Scotland had first made the Plantagenet name terrible was Edward I.
