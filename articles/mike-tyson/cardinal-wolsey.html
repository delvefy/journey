Cardinal Wolsey

English cardinal and Lord Chancellor (c. 1473–1530)

Thomas Wolsey rose from the most humble beginnings—the son of an Ipswich butcher—to become the most powerful man in England after the king himself. Ordained a priest and educated at Oxford, he attached himself to the young Henry VIII and made himself indispensable. By 1515 he was Archbishop of York, a cardinal of the Roman Church, and Lord Chancellor of England, and as papal legate he wielded authority over the English Church as well.

For some fifteen years Wolsey governed England in all but name, directing its diplomacy and dazzling Europe with spectacles such as the Field of the Cloth of Gold. His wealth was legendary: he built the magnificent palace of Hampton Court and founded a great college at Oxford. Proud and grasping, he made many enemies who resented a low-born cleric set above the ancient nobility.

His fall came with "the King's Great Matter." Charged with securing the annulment of Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, Wolsey found the road to Rome blocked, for the pope dared not defy the queen's nephew, the Emperor Charles V. As the cardinal failed, Anne Boleyn and the court turned against him. In 1529 he was stripped of his offices, and a year later, summoned south on a charge of treason, he died at Leicester—lamenting that had he served God as diligently as he served his king, God would not have forsaken him.

His place as the master of Henry's government, and the architect of the religious revolution Wolsey had not lived to see, was taken by his own former servant and protégé—Thomas Cromwell.
