Jean-Paul Sartre

French philosopher and writer (1905–1980)

Jean-Paul Sartre was the most famous philosopher of the twentieth century, the man who made existentialism a worldwide movement and the towering intellectual of the Parisian Left.

Educated at the École Normale Supérieure, where he met his lifelong companion Simone de Beauvoir, he steeped himself in German phenomenology and forged from it his own philosophy of radical freedom. In the novel Nausea, the dense treatise Being and Nothingness, and the play No Exit—with its verdict that “hell is other people”—he argued that human beings are “condemned to be free,” that existence precedes essence, and that we alone are responsible for what we make of ourselves. The lonely, anxious individual at the heart of his thought descended directly from Søren Kierkegaard.

After the war he became a public conscience, editing an influential review and weighing in on every great question of the day. Increasingly he tried to wed his existentialism to the revolutionary politics of Karl Marx, championed anti-colonial struggles, and embraced a militant engagement that made him a hero to the young and a scandal to the establishment. In 1964 he refused the Nobel Prize, unwilling to be turned into an institution.

He died in 1980, and tens of thousands followed his coffin through Paris. For years his closest comrade in the postwar intellectual world—before a bitter, public quarrel over revolution and violence split them forever—had been the novelist of the absurd, Albert Camus.
