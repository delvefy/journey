Seleucus I Nicator

Macedonian general of Alexander and founder of the Seleucid Empire (c. 358–281 BC)

Seleucus was a Macedonian officer who rose under Alexander the Great to command the elite infantry of the Companions. When Alexander died in 323 BC and his marshals divided the empire, Seleucus received the governorship of Babylon. Driven out by the most powerful of his rivals, Antigonus the One-Eyed, he took refuge with Ptolemy in Egypt, fought at his side in the great victory at Gaza, and in 312 BC fought his way back to Babylon—the year from which his dynasty would date its calendar.

From that base he built the largest of all the successor kingdoms, stretching from Asia Minor to the frontiers of India. Marching east, he came into conflict with the Indian emperor Chandragupta Maurya, and concluded a famous treaty: Seleucus ceded the eastern provinces in return for five hundred war elephants, sealed the peace with a marriage alliance, and sent the envoy Megasthenes to the Mauryan court.

Those elephants proved decisive in 301 BC at Ipsus, where Seleucus and Lysimachus crushed the army of Antigonus, who fell on the field. Seleucus founded great cities to anchor his realm—Seleucia on the Tigris and Antioch, named for his father—and earned the surname Nicator, “the Victor.”

In 281 BC he defeated his last rival Lysimachus at Corupedium and stood as the sole surviving marshal of Alexander, poised to claim Macedonia itself. But as he crossed into Europe he was murdered by Ptolemy Keraunos, a guest he had sheltered. The empire he founded endured for two centuries. The Indian emperor with whom he made his celebrated peace was Chandragupta Maurya.
