Voltaire

French Enlightenment writer and philosopher (1694–1778)

Voltaire, born François-Marie Arouet in Paris in 1694, was the most celebrated writer of the French Enlightenment and one of its presiding spirits. Prolific in every form—plays, poems, histories, essays, and the biting philosophical tale Candide—he wielded wit as a weapon in a lifelong campaign for reason, tolerance, and freedom of thought against superstition and the abuses of church and state. His rallying cry against fanaticism was "Écrasez l'infâme," crush the infamous thing.

His sharp pen made him as many enemies as admirers. He was twice imprisoned in the Bastille and driven into exile in England, where he absorbed the science of Newton and the liberty of the English constitution. He returned to popularize Newton's physics for French readers, studying it with his brilliant companion Émilie du Châtelet, and his Letters on the English helped import a new spirit of empiricism and freedom.

For a time he graced the court of Frederick the Great of Prussia, until that friendship soured, and he corresponded with monarchs and thinkers across Europe. He used his fame to defend victims of religious persecution and judicial cruelty, making himself the conscience of the age.

Voltaire died in 1778, lionized in Paris, and was later enshrined in the Panthéon. His relentless advocacy of reason and liberty helped prepare the ground for the French Revolution and shaped the modern ideal of free expression.
