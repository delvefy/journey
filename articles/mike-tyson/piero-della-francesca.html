Piero della Francesca

Italian painter and mathematician (c. 1415–1492)

Piero della Francesca painted a world of motionless clarity, bathed in even light and ordered by geometry, that has come to seem one of the purest expressions of the early Renaissance. Born in the small Tuscan town of Borgo San Sepolcro, he learned the new Florentine science of perspective from the tradition of Brunelleschi and Alberti and made it the very bones of his art.

His figures stand grave and still, modeled by light into almost architectural solidity. In the church of San Francesco at Arezzo he covered the walls with the Legend of the True Cross, a fresco cycle of serene grandeur; elsewhere he left the haunting Flagellation, the dreamlike Baptism of Christ, and the great altarpiece in which an ostrich egg hangs above the enthroned Madonna.

For much of his career he served the courts of central Italy, above all the cultivated Duke of Urbino, whose unmistakable profile—hooked nose, red cap—he fixed forever in a famous double portrait. In these pictures painting and measurement became one.

In his later years, perhaps as his eyesight failed, Piero turned from the brush to the pen, composing rigorous treatises on perspective, on the abacus, and on the regular solids—works of real mathematics that his pupil Luca Pacioli would later absorb into his own. The princely patron whose Urbino court Piero adorned, and whose face he made immortal, was Federico da Montefeltro.
