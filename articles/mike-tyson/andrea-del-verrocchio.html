Andrea del Verrocchio

Florentine sculptor and painter (c. 1435–1488)

Andrea del Verrocchio trained first as a goldsmith and grew into the foremost sculptor of Florence in the years after Donatello, running the most important and crowded workshop in the city under the patronage of the Medici. Through that workshop passed a remarkable generation of artists—Pietro Perugino, Lorenzo di Credi, and, most famously of all, the young Leonardo da Vinci.

In bronze and marble he produced works of poised elegance: a lithe bronze David for the Medici, a laughing Putto with a Dolphin, and the powerful group of Christ and the doubting Thomas for the guild niche at Orsanmichele. He also carved the splendid tomb of two Medici princes in San Lorenzo.

As a painter he was capable but cautious, and the most repeated story about him concerns his pupil: that when Leonardo painted an angel into Verrocchio's Baptism of Christ with such grace that it outshone the master's own figures, Verrocchio laid down his brush for good. True or not, the tale captures how the teacher's studio nurtured a genius beyond himself.

His greatest commission took him to Venice and occupied his final years: a colossal bronze equestrian statue of a famous mercenary captain, taut with arrogant energy, which he left unfinished at his death in 1488 and which still rides above a Venetian square as one of the supreme monuments of the Renaissance. The condottiero whose proud likeness he set upon that bronze horse was Bartolomeo Colleoni.
