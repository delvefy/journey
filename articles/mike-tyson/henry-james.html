Henry James

American-British novelist (1843–1916)

Henry James was born in New York in 1843, the younger brother of the philosopher William James, and raised between America and Europe in a family that prized ideas above all. That divided upbringing gave him his great subject, the encounter of innocent Americans with the older, subtler world of Europe, and he eventually settled in England to write about it.

Over a long career he refined the novel into a conscious art of consciousness itself. The Portrait of a Lady made his name; the chilling tale The Turn of the Screw showed his command of suggestion; and the dense late masterpieces The Wings of the Dove, The Ambassadors, and The Golden Bowl traced the finest movements of perception and motive through their characters' minds.

James lived at the center of literary life. He took Honoré de Balzac for his master, befriended Ivan Turgenev and frequented the Paris salon of Gustave Flaubert, and counted Edith Wharton and Joseph Conrad among his intimates. His prefaces and essays made him one of the great theorists of fiction.

Intensely private and unmarried, devoted wholly to his craft, he became a British subject in 1915 out of loyalty to his adopted country during the war and was given the Order of Merit. He died in 1916, revered as “the Master” and a bridge between Victorian realism and the modern novel.
