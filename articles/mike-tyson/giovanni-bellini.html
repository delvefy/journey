Giovanni Bellini

Venetian Renaissance painter (c. 1430–1516)

Giovanni Bellini was the founding father of the Venetian school, the painter who taught his city to glory in colour, light and atmosphere.

Born into a family of painters and trained in his father's workshop, he began under the hard, linear spell of his brother-in-law Andrea Mantegna, whose sculptural precision marks his early work. But Bellini gradually softened and warmed his art, and when the technique of oil painting reached Venice he embraced it fully, learning to bathe his figures in a glowing, enveloping light and to set sacred scenes in tender, luminous landscapes.

Over a career of more than half a century he transformed Venetian painting. His serene Madonnas and majestic altarpieces—the San Zaccaria and San Giobbe altars, the rapt St Francis in Ecstasy, the steely portrait of Doge Leonardo Loredan—set a standard of devotional grandeur and human feeling. When Albrecht Dürer came to Venice he sought out the aged Bellini and pronounced him, for all his years, “still the best in painting.”

From his vast workshop came the masters who would carry Venice to its golden age, above all the young Titian, who absorbed his teacher's colour and outshone the whole world. Bellini died in 1516, honoured as the patriarch of his art. The other brilliant pupil of his shop—the short-lived, enigmatic poet of mood and landscape—was Giorgione.
