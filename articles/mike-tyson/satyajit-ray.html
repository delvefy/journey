Satyajit Ray

Indian filmmaker (1921–1992)

Satyajit Ray, born in Calcutta in 1921 into a celebrated Bengali family of writers and artists, became one of the greatest filmmakers in the history of cinema. A commercial artist by training, he was steeped in Bengali culture—he had studied at Rabindranath Tagore's university at Santiniketan—and his outlook was transformed by two encounters: assisting the French director Jean Renoir during the shooting of The River in Bengal, and seeing the Italian neorealist classic Bicycle Thieves in London.

Inspired, and against great odds, Ray made his first film, Pather Panchali (1955), the story of a poor rural Bengali family seen through the eyes of a small boy, Apu. Shot on a shoestring with mostly amateur actors, its tender realism and luminous images astonished the world and won a prize at Cannes. With its two sequels it became the Apu Trilogy, among the most beloved works in world cinema.

Over the next three decades Ray directed a remarkable body of films—Charulata, The Music Room, the Tagore adaptations of Teen Kanya, and many more—marked by humanism, subtlety and exquisite craft. He composed their music, wrote their scripts, and designed their look himself, and he became a popular author of Bengali stories and novels besides.

Admired by directors from Akira Kurosawa to Martin Scorsese, Ray brought Indian cinema to the attention of the world and remained its most revered figure. Shortly before his death in 1992 he received an honorary Academy Award for his lifetime's achievement. He died in Calcutta, mourned as a national treasure and a master whose quiet, compassionate films had enlarged the art of film itself.
