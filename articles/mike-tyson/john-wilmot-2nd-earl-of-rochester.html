John Wilmot, 2nd Earl of Rochester

Restoration courtier and poet (1647–1680)

John Wilmot, second Earl of Rochester, was the most brilliant and most scandalous of the wits who surrounded King Charles II, a poet of dazzling gifts and a libertine who burned out his life by thirty-three.

He won early honour for courage in the naval wars against the Dutch, then made himself a fixture of the merry, dissolute court, where he was by turns the king's favourite and his exile, banished again and again for some fresh outrage and as often forgiven. His verse matched his life: love lyrics of great delicacy, lampoons of savage obscenity, and the famous philosophical poem A Satyr Against Reason and Mankind, whose bleak materialism drew on the philosophy of Thomas Hobbes.

In the literary wars of the day he was first the patron and then the enemy of the laureate John Dryden, and was widely blamed for the bullies who set upon Dryden in Rose Alley. Few doubted his genius, and fewer trusted his friendship.

His riotous living wrecked his health, and he died young, by report repenting at the last under a clergyman's care. He left behind the image of the Restoration itself—glittering, cynical, and doomed.

Among the writers and players of that vivid world he knew the pioneering dramatist and novelist Aphra Behn.
