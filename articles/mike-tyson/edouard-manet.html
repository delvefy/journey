Édouard Manet

French painter (1832–1883)

Édouard Manet stood at the hinge between the old painting and the new, a Parisian of comfortable birth who scandalised the official Salon and became the reluctant father figure of modern art. Trained in the studio of Thomas Couture, he looked past his teacher to the Spanish masters, above all Diego Velázquez, whom he called the painter of painters, and Francisco Goya.

In 1863 his Luncheon on the Grass, with a nude woman picnicking among clothed men, was rejected by the Salon and shown amid uproar at the Salon des Refusés. Two years later his Olympia—a frank, modern reworking of Titian's reclining Venus—provoked even greater outrage. Manet had not set out to rebel; he sought official approval all his life, yet his cool, flat handling and contemporary subjects broke decisively with academic convention.

He painted modern life directly: cafés, racetracks, the Parisian crowd, and, near the end, the dreamlike A Bar at the Folies-Bergère. His Execution of Emperor Maximilian openly echoed Goya's firing-squad pictures. The novelist Émile Zola championed him in print, and Manet repaid him with a celebrated portrait.

Though he declined to exhibit with the younger Impressionists, he was their admired elder and absorbed their bright outdoor palette in turn. Crippled by illness, he died in Paris in 1883 at fifty-one. Foremost among the friends who gathered around him was the leader of Impressionism, Claude Monet.
