Lord Mountbatten

British admiral and last Viceroy of India (1900–1979)

Louis Mountbatten, Earl Mountbatten of Burma, was a British naval commander, statesman, and royal who stood at the center of twentieth-century imperial history. Born in 1900 as a prince of the German house of Battenberg—a name his family anglicized during the First World War—he was a great-grandson of Queen Victoria, uncle of Prince Philip, and a trusted mentor to the young Prince Charles.

A career naval officer, he rose during the Second World War to head Combined Operations and then, on Winston Churchill's appointment, to serve as Supreme Allied Commander in South East Asia, directing the long campaign to drive the Japanese from Burma.

In 1947 he was sent to India as its last Viceroy, charged with winding up nearly two centuries of British rule. Working at speed with Nehru, Gandhi, and Jinnah, he presided over the transfer of power and the Partition of the subcontinent into India and Pakistan—a division accomplished with bewildering haste and accompanied by horrific bloodshed, for which his rushed timetable has been much criticized. He stayed on briefly as independent India's first Governor-General.

He later capped his naval career as First Sea Lord. In 1979 the Provisional IRA murdered him with a bomb hidden aboard his fishing boat off the Irish coast, a shocking act that stunned Britain. Glamorous, ambitious, and ever close to the throne, Mountbatten linked the worlds of empire, war, and the modern monarchy of Queen Elizabeth II.
