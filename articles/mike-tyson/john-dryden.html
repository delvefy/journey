John Dryden

English poet, playwright, and critic (1631–1700)

John Dryden so dominated the literature of Restoration England that the whole era is called the Age of Dryden. Poet, dramatist, and the first great English critic, he set the standard of the polished couplet that would rule English verse for a century.

Made Poet Laureate under Charles II, he served the crown with his pen, defending the king's cause in the masterly political satire Absalom and Achitophel. He ruled the stage with heroic dramas and comedies, among them All for Love, his own retelling of the story of Antony and Cleopatra, and he laid the foundations of English criticism in his Essay of Dramatic Poesy.

He was also the supreme translator of his age. He gave English readers the complete Aeneid of Virgil, and in his last book, the Fables Ancient and Modern, he refashioned the tales of Geoffrey Chaucer for a new age, while in prose he praised the older poet's boundless humanity. He revered Milton as well, calling him the heir of Homer and Virgil together.

His later years were hard: having turned Catholic, he refused to recant after the Revolution of 1688 and lost his offices. Among the wits of the court he had known the brilliant and reckless John Wilmot, Earl of Rochester, first his patron and then his enemy.

His art passed directly to his great successor, Alexander Pope. Dryden died in 1700 and lies in Westminster Abbey.
