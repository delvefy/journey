Francis of Assisi

Founder of the Franciscan order (c. 1181–1226)

Francis of Assisi, born around 1181 to a wealthy cloth-merchant family in the Umbrian town of Assisi, became one of the most beloved saints in the history of Christianity and the founder of the Franciscan order. As a young man he led a carefree, worldly life and dreamed of knightly glory, but after a period of imprisonment and illness he underwent a dramatic conversion. Renouncing his inheritance—stripping off his fine clothes before the bishop and his astonished father—he embraced a life of absolute poverty.

Francis devoted himself to the poor, the sick and the outcast, famously embracing a leper, and preached the Gospel with joyful simplicity. He gathered followers drawn by his radiant humility, and in 1209 the powerful Pope Innocent III gave his blessing to their new way of life, the seed of the great order of Friars Minor. With his friend Clare of Assisi he later inspired a parallel order for women.

His love extended to all creation. Legend tells of his preaching to the birds and taming a wolf, and he celebrated the natural world in his "Canticle of the Sun," praising Brother Sun and Sister Moon. During the Fifth Crusade he crossed the lines in Egypt to preach peacefully before the Muslim Sultan al-Kamil. In 1224, on Mount La Verna, he is said to have received the stigmata, the wounds of Christ, in his own body.

Francis died in 1226, worn out by austerity, and was canonised just two years later. His life inspired Dante's praise and Giotto's frescoes, and his ideals of poverty, humility and reverence for nature have echoed through the centuries—down to the modern pope who took his name.
