Themistocles

Athenian statesman and admiral (c. 524–459 BC)

Themistocles was the Athenian statesman whose foresight and cunning saved Greece from Persian conquest. Born around 524 BC to a modest family, he rose through the new Athenian democracy on the strength of energy and ambition rather than noble birth. Convinced that Persia would return after its defeat at Marathon, he persuaded his fellow citizens to spend a windfall of silver from the mines at Laurion not on a handout but on a great fleet of warships—the decision that would make Athens a naval power.

When Xerxes invaded in 480 BC, Themistocles became the guiding mind of the allied Greek navy. After the fall of Thermopylae, he engineered the decisive confrontation at Salamis, reportedly sending Xerxes a false message that lured the Persian fleet into the narrow straits, where the lighter Greek ships destroyed it. The victory broke the back of the invasion and earned him fame as the savior of his city.

In its aftermath he rebuilt Athens' walls over Spartan objections and fortified the harbor of Piraeus, laying the foundations of the Athenian empire. Yet his arrogance and rivalries told against him. Ostracized and later accused of treason, he was driven into exile.

In a remarkable turn, the man who had defeated Persia found refuge at the Persian court, where Xerxes's son Artaxerxes welcomed him and granted him the revenues of several cities in Asia Minor. He died there around 459 BC. The biographer Plutarch later recorded his eventful life in the Parallel Lives.
