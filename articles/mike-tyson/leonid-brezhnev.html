Leonid Brezhnev

Leader of the Soviet Union, 1964–1982 (1906–1982)

Leonid Brezhnev led the Soviet Union for eighteen years, presiding over its Cold War zenith abroad and its long slide into stagnation at home.

A party functionary who had risen through the apparatus of Joseph Stalin, he was promoted by Nikita Khrushchev and then helped engineer his patron's overthrow in 1964, taking the top post for himself. He restored a measure of order and even partly rehabilitated Stalin's tarnished image, ruling through a cautious, conservative collective leadership. In 1968 he crushed the reform movement of the Prague Spring and proclaimed the “Brezhnev Doctrine,” asserting Moscow's right to intervene wherever socialism was threatened.

Yet his were also the years of détente. He signed landmark arms-control treaties with Richard Nixon at the 1972 Moscow summit, worked through Nixon's adviser Henry Kissinger, and later concluded a further agreement with Jimmy Carter—before the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 froze relations once more. At home the economy ossified and the leadership aged into a gerontocracy; the reformer Mikhail Gorbachev, who rose under him, would inherit the stagnation he left behind.

Brezhnev died in office in 1982, weighed down with medals and infirmity. The American president with whom he sealed the Helsinki Accords and the Vladivostok arms framework in the mid-1970s was Gerald Ford.
