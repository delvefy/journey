Giovanni Boccaccio

Italian writer and humanist (1313–1375)

Giovanni Boccaccio was one of the three great fathers of Italian literature and a pioneer of Renaissance humanism. The illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant, he passed his youth in the lively court and counting-houses of Naples before returning to Florence. There he composed, in the years around the catastrophe of the Black Death, his enduring masterpiece, the Decameron.

In that work a company of ten young Florentines, fleeing the plague to a country villa, pass the days telling a hundred tales by turns comic, tragic, bawdy, and shrewd. Together they form a dazzling panorama of medieval life and a foundation stone of European prose fiction, mined for centuries by later storytellers, Chaucer and Shakespeare among them.

His friendship with Petrarch, begun in 1350, turned Boccaccio decisively toward classical scholarship. He became a devoted hunter of forgotten Latin manuscripts, an early promoter of Greek studies in the West, and the author of learned encyclopedic works on the pagan gods and on famous men and women. Above all he was the great champion of Dante: he wrote the poet's first biography, gave the Comedy its lasting epithet "Divine," and delivered the first public lectures on it in Florence.

Through his manuscript-hunting in the libraries of Italy, Boccaccio is credited with helping rescue from oblivion the works of one of Rome's greatest authors—the incomparable historian of the early empire, Tacitus.
