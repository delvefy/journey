Procopius

Byzantine historian (c. 500–565)

Procopius of Caesarea was the principal historian of the age of Justinian and our chief window onto the sixth-century Roman world. Trained in law and rhetoric, he became the legal secretary and confidant of the great general Belisarius, accompanying him on campaign against the Persians in the East, the Vandals in Africa, and the Goths in Italy. From this vantage he wrote with the authority of an eyewitness.

His three works could hardly be more different. The History of the Wars is a sober, detailed, and reliable account of Justinian's campaigns. On Buildings is a glowing panegyric cataloguing the emperor's vast program of construction across the empire. But the work that made his name notorious is the Secret History, a venomous tract circulated only after his death, which portrayed Justinian as a fiend, the empress Theodora as a scheming debauchee, and even his patron Belisarius as a weak and deluded husband.

The contrast between his public praise and private venom has fascinated readers ever since, while his careful narrative of the wars remains an indispensable historical source. Procopius died around 565, leaving the fullest portrait we have of a pivotal century.

His vivid record of Rome's twilight glory and the wars to reclaim it would, more than a thousand years later, feed the greatest of all narratives of the empire's long collapse—the work of the English historian Edward Gibbon.
