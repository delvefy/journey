Francis I of France

King of France, reigned 1515–1547 (1494–1547)

Francis I was the archetypal Renaissance king of France, a lavish patron of art and learning whose long reign was dominated by his rivalry with the Holy Roman Emperor.

He came to the throne in 1515 and at once won glory at the battle of Marignano, recovering Milan. A man of boundless appetite and ambition, he made his court the brilliant centre of the French Renaissance: he lured Leonardo da Vinci to France for the master's final years, cherishing him so that legend has Leonardo dying in the king's own arms; he built the soaring château of Chambord, founded what became the Collège de France, and gathered humanists and artists around him.

His reign was defined by struggle with Charles V, who had beaten him to the imperial crown. The Italian Wars between them dragged on for decades and reached their nadir at Pavia in 1525, where Francis was captured and carried prisoner to Madrid, writing that nothing was left to him “save honour and life.” To counter the encircling Habsburg power he met Henry VIII amid fabulous splendour at the Field of the Cloth of Gold, and—scandalously for a Christian king—allied himself with the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent.

He died in 1547, his kingdom strengthened and his court the envy of Europe, his son's bride being the Medici princess Clement VII had sent north. The pope with whom, early in his reign, he had struck the great Concordat of Bologna that gave the French crown command of its own Church, was Leo X.
