Annibale Carracci

Italian Baroque painter (1560–1609)

Annibale Carracci was the Bolognese master who, more than anyone, rescued Italian painting from the artificiality of late Mannerism and laid the foundations of the Baroque.

Born in Bologna, he founded with his cousin and brother an influential academy that taught a fresh study of nature wedded to the grandeur of the great Renaissance masters. He travelled to absorb the lessons of the past—the radiant color of Titian and the Venetians, the harmony of Raphael—and forged from them a robust, naturalistic yet idealizing style that set painting on a new course. In these same years his austere classicism stood as one pole of the new art, with the violent naturalism of his rival Caravaggio as the other.

His great achievement came in Rome, where Cardinal Farnese commissioned him to fresco the ceiling of the gallery in the Palazzo Farnese. The resulting Loves of the Gods, a dazzling illusionistic vault, was at once measured against the Sistine Chapel of Michelangelo and hailed as its worthy successor, and it launched a century of grand decorative painting.

From his Bolognese academy came the next generation of Italian painters—Guido Reni, Domenichino and others—and his disciplined classicism passed down through the academies to shape Nicolas Poussin and the whole European tradition. Worn down by melancholy and the cardinal's miserly treatment, he died in Rome in 1609 and was buried, as he wished, near Raphael in the Pantheon. The sensuous master of Parma whose soft illusionism he had studied and made his own was Correggio.
