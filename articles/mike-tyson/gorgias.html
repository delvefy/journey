Gorgias

Greek sophist and rhetorician (c. 485–380 BC)

Gorgias of Leontini, born in Sicily around 485 BC and said to have lived more than a century, was among the most celebrated of the sophists and a founding master of the art of rhetoric. A pupil, by tradition, of the philosopher Empedocles, he came to Athens in 427 BC on an embassy from his native city and astonished his hearers with a dazzling, ornate style of speech—balanced clauses, antitheses, and chiming sounds—that bewitched the Greek world and made his fortune as a travelling teacher.

For Gorgias the supreme power was that of the spoken word. In his Encomium of Helen he set out to excuse Helen of Troy by showing how speech can master the human soul as a drug masters the body—"speech is a mighty lord," he wrote, that can banish fear, soothe grief, and persuade against all reason. In a more provocative work he argued, with virtuoso logic, that nothing truly exists; that if anything did, it could not be known; and that if it could be known, it could not be communicated to another.

This delight in persuasion over truth made him the great foil of philosophy. Plato devoted to him an entire dialogue, the Gorgias, in which Socrates challenges the rhetorician and his followers, condemning their art as mere flattery and defending the pursuit of justice and knowledge instead.

Through his many pupils, among them the educator Isocrates, Gorgias shaped the teaching of eloquence for generations. Behind him stood his reputed teacher, the strange and brilliant Empedocles.
