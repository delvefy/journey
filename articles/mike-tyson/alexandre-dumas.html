Alexandre Dumas

French novelist, master of historical adventure (1802–1870)

Alexandre Dumas was among the most widely read authors who ever lived, the supreme storyteller of historical adventure. He was the grandson of a French nobleman and an enslaved woman of the Caribbean, and the son of a dashing general of the Revolution; the colour of his ancestry drew sneers in his lifetime that he answered with wit and pride.

He made his name first as a Romantic playwright in Paris, in the same heady years that Hugo was conquering the stage, then turned to the serialized novel and conquered the reading public of the world. The Three Musketeers, with its valiant d'Artagnan and the cry “all for one and one for all,” and The Count of Monte Cristo, with its patient, magnificent revenge, have never been out of print or off the screen since.

He wrote at a furious pace, running what critics called a fiction factory, drafting plots and chapters with collaborators—above all Auguste Maquet—to feed the newspapers' insatiable demand. He lived as lavishly as his heroes, made and squandered fortunes, and built himself a fairy-tale château.

For all his French settings, the form he perfected—the panoramic novel that clothes real history in romance and intrigue—he learned from a master across the Channel. The Scottish writer who had invented the historical novel and whom Dumas took as his model was Walter Scott.
