John Maynard Keynes

English economist (1883–1946)

John Maynard Keynes was born in Cambridge in 1883, the son of an economist, and was educated at Eton and King's College, Cambridge, where he read mathematics and joined the secret society of the Apostles. Through it he entered the Bloomsbury Group, mixing with writers and artists for the rest of his life, even as he became the most influential economist of his age.

After service in the Treasury during the First World War, he attended the Paris Peace Conference and resigned in disgust at the punitive reparations imposed on Germany. His polemic The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919) made him famous and proved grimly prophetic about the instability to come.

The Great Depression gave urgency to his central insight. In The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936) he argued that economies could become stuck far below full employment, and that governments should act—through spending and monetary policy—to revive demand. This "Keynesian revolution" overturned the classical orthodoxy descending from Adam Smith and reshaped economic policy across the democratic world.

A shrewd investor, art patron and bibliophile—he assembled the alchemical papers of Isaac Newton—Keynes married the ballerina Lydia Lopokova and helped found Britain's Arts Council. In 1944 he led the British delegation at Bretton Woods, helping design the postwar monetary order and the institutions that became the IMF and World Bank. Worn out by the effort, he died of heart disease in 1946.
