Savonarola

Florentine friar and reformer (1452–1498)

Girolamo Savonarola was the fiery Dominican preacher who briefly turned Renaissance Florence into a puritan republic of God before being burned in its great square.

Born in Ferrara, he came to the convent of San Marco in Florence and made his name with apocalyptic sermons denouncing the corruption of the Church and the luxury and paganism of the age. He thundered against the splendours of the Medici, and tradition holds that the dying Lorenzo de' Medici summoned him for a last, uneasy interview. When a French invasion drove the Medici out in 1494, the friar became the moral dictator of the city, proclaiming Christ its king and Florence a new Jerusalem.

His followers, the “weepers,” heaped books, paintings, mirrors and finery onto the famous Bonfire of the Vanities; the painter Sandro Botticelli, caught in his spell, is said to have cast some of his own works into the flames. But his attacks on the corruption of the papacy enraged the Borgia pope Alexander VI, who excommunicated him and pressed for his silence. As his prophecies failed and the city tired of austerity, he was seized, tortured, and in 1498 hanged and burned in the Piazza della Signoria.

His memory long outlived him. Niccolò Machiavelli weighed him coldly as the type of the “unarmed prophet” doomed to fall; Martin Luther and the reformers claimed him as a forerunner; and Michelangelo, who had heard him preach as a youth, never forgot his voice. Centuries later he became the brooding center of a great Victorian historical novel, Romola, by George Eliot.
