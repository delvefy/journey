Seneca

Roman Stoic philosopher and statesman (c. 4 BC–65 AD)

Lucius Annaeus Seneca, born around 4 BC at Córdoba in Roman Spain, was the leading Stoic philosopher of imperial Rome and one of its most accomplished writers. The son of a famous rhetorician, he made his way in Roman public life until the emperor Claudius exiled him to Corsica on a charge of adultery. He was recalled when Nero's mother, Agrippina, chose him to tutor her young son.

When Nero became emperor, Seneca served as his chief advisor, and for several years he and the guard commander Burrus effectively guided the government, restraining the young ruler and giving Rome a period of good administration. Seneca grew enormously rich in the process, a fact his critics never let him forget.

His writings carried the practical wisdom of the school Zeno had founded. In essays such as On the Shortness of Life and On Anger, and in his Letters to Lucilius, he counseled self-mastery, the right use of time, and serenity before fortune; he also wrote blood-soaked tragedies that would later shape European drama.

As Nero descended into tyranny and matricide, Seneca withdrew from court. Accused—perhaps unjustly—of joining a conspiracy against the emperor in 65 AD, he was ordered to take his own life. He died with Stoic calm, opening his veins and discoursing to his friends, consciously echoing the serene death of Socrates centuries before.
