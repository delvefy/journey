Charles Babbage

English mathematician and inventor (1791–1871)

Charles Babbage was born in London in 1791 and educated at Cambridge, where with John Herschel he founded the Analytical Society to reform British mathematics, pressing the elegant calculus notation of Leibniz upon a nation still loyal to Newton. A founder of the Royal Astronomical Society, he was a restless and quarrelsome polymath who wrote on everything from manufactures to ciphers.

His life's obsession was the mechanization of calculation. Frustrated by the errors in mathematical tables, he designed the Difference Engine, a great clockwork calculator that would compute and print such tables automatically. Though he won government money and built portions of it, the machine's vast complexity defeated him, and it was never finished in his lifetime.

He then conceived something far bolder, the Analytical Engine, a general-purpose programmable computer of cogwheels, with a separate “mill” to calculate and “store” to hold numbers, fed by punched cards borrowed from the weaving loom, and able to branch on its own results. It anticipated the electronic computer by a full century. His collaborator Ada Lovelace, drawing on earlier mechanical calculators such as those of Pascal, described the engine and saw that it might one day manipulate not just numbers but symbols of any kind.

Babbage died in 1871 with his engines still unbuilt, dismissed by many as an eccentric. Only in the twentieth century was he honored as a prophet of the computing age.
