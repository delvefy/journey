Tokugawa Ieyasu

Founder of the Tokugawa shogunate (1543–1616)

Tokugawa Ieyasu, born in 1543 into a minor warlord family during Japan's age of civil wars, became the founder of the Tokugawa shogunate and the architect of more than two and a half centuries of peace. Patient, cautious and supremely calculating, he was the last and most successful of the three great unifiers of Japan.

He spent his youth as a hostage of rival clans, learning the value of survival and timing. In his twenties he allied himself with the brilliant, ruthless warlord Oda Nobunaga, and under that partnership steadily expanded his own domain. After Nobunaga's assassination, power passed to Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the peasant-born general who completed the work of unification; Ieyasu prudently submitted, biding his time and consolidating his eastern stronghold around Edo, the future Tokyo.

His patience was rewarded when Hideyoshi died in 1598, leaving an infant heir. Ieyasu out-manoeuvred his rivals and, at the great Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, smashed the opposing coalition in the decisive clash that made him master of Japan. In 1603 the emperor named him shogun, and to secure his dynasty he crushed the last of the Toyotomi at the Siege of Osaka in 1614–15.

Ieyasu built a system of government that held the daimyo in check, closed Japan largely to the outside world, and kept the realm at peace for the rest of the Edo period. Among his advisers was even the shipwrecked English navigator William Adams. He passed the shogun's title to his son but ruled from behind the scenes until his death in 1616. Revered and enshrined as a god, he had founded a dynasty that would rule Japan until 1868.
