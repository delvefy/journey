Franz Schubert

Austrian composer (1797–1828)

Franz Schubert poured an almost unbelievable wealth of music into a life of just thirty-one years, and in doing so made the German art song into one of the great forms of Western music. Born on the outskirts of Vienna, he sang as a boy in the imperial chapel and studied composition under Antonio Salieri before scraping a living as a schoolteacher and devoting every spare hour to writing.

He composed with extraordinary fluency—some six hundred songs, nine symphonies, operas, masses, sonatas, and chamber works—much of it performed at the convivial musical evenings his friends called Schubertiads. His genius for melody found its purest outlet in the Lied: the galloping terror of Erlkönig, the spinning-wheel lament of Gretchen, and the great song cycles Die schöne Müllerin and the desolate Winterreise.

His instrumental music matched it—the haunting Unfinished Symphony, the spacious Great C major, the Trout Quintet, and the String Quintet in C with its aching slow movement. Many of these works went unheard in his lifetime and surfaced only decades later through admirers like Robert Schumann and Franz Liszt.

He worshipped Ludwig van Beethoven, walked as a torchbearer at his funeral, and asked to be buried near him; he prized too the classical poise of Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. For his songs he turned again and again to the great German poets—above all to Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, and to that poet's illustrious companion, Friedrich Schiller.
