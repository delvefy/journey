Nikolai Gogol

Russian writer and satirist (1809–1852)

Nikolai Vasilyevich Gogol was a founder of Russian realism whose work is shot through with the grotesque, the fantastic, and a strange laughter through tears. Born in the Ukrainian village of Sorochintsy into the minor Cossack gentry, he came to St. Petersburg seeking a career and found fame instead with Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka, vivid tales of Ukrainian folklore and the supernatural.

He followed them with the Mirgorod collection, including the Cossack epic Taras Bulba and the horror tale Viy, and with the Petersburg stories that secured his stature: The Nose, Diary of a Madman, Nevsky Prospekt, and above all The Overcoat, whose pitiful clerk became a touchstone of Russian fiction. His comedy The Government Inspector skewered provincial corruption so sharply that it scandalised officialdom.

His masterpiece was Dead Souls, a satirical panorama of serf-owning Russia built around a swindler buying up the names of dead serfs. Both that novel and the play grew from subjects reportedly given to him by Alexander Pushkin, whose encouragement he treasured above all.

In later years Gogol sank into religious anguish and self-doubt, fell under the sway of a severe confessor, burned much of the unfinished second part of Dead Souls, and died in 1852 after refusing food, aged forty-two. His influence ran deep through Russian letters—Dostoevsky's generation, it was later said, all came out of Gogol's Overcoat—and was mourned by his younger contemporary Ivan Turgenev.
