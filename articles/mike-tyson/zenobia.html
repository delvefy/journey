Zenobia

Queen of Palmyra (c. 240–after 274)

Zenobia was the warrior queen of Palmyra who carved a brief but dazzling empire out of the Roman East and dared to challenge Rome itself. The wealthy caravan city of Palmyra, set amid the Syrian desert, had risen to power as a bulwark against Persia under her husband Odaenathus. When he was assassinated around 267, Zenobia took the reins as regent for her young son—and proved far more ambitious than anyone expected.

Cultivated and commanding, fluent in several languages and a patron of Greek learning, she gathered scholars to her court and led her armies in person. Within a few years she had seized Egypt and much of Asia Minor, severing Rome's richest eastern provinces, and at last she threw off Roman authority altogether, assuming imperial titles for herself and her son. To burnish her glory she claimed descent from the last and greatest queen of Egypt, Cleopatra.

Her defiance brought the full weight of Rome upon her. The emperor Aurelian marched east, defeated her forces in two great battles, and besieged her desert capital until it fell in 272. Zenobia was captured as she fled toward Persia and carried to Rome, where, by the famous account, she was paraded in golden chains in Aurelian's triumph.

Her later fate fades into legend. Among the learned men she had drawn to her brilliant court, the most celebrated was the Greek philosopher and critic who served as her counselor—Cassius Longinus.
