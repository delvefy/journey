Toyotomi Hideyoshi

Second great unifier of Japan (1537–1598)

Toyotomi Hideyoshi, born in 1537 to a humble peasant family, achieved one of the most extraordinary ascents in history, rising from a foot soldier to become the supreme ruler of Japan. He entered the service of the warlord Oda Nobunaga as a lowly attendant and, by sheer ability, made himself Nobunaga's most brilliant and trusted general.

When Nobunaga was betrayed and killed at Honnō-ji in 1582, Hideyoshi moved with astonishing speed, defeating the assassin Akechi Mitsuhide within days and seizing the leadership of Nobunaga's cause. Over the following decade he completed what his master had begun, subduing the last of the rival warlords and unifying all Japan by 1590. Barred by his low birth from the title of shogun, he governed as imperial regent.

A ruler of immense energy, Hideyoshi reshaped the country. He built the mighty Osaka Castle, carried out a nationwide land survey, and ordered the great "sword hunt" that disarmed the peasantry and froze the social classes, separating warrior from farmer. He was also a famous patron of the arts and of the tea ceremony.

His ambition finally outran him. Dreaming of conquering China, he launched two massive invasions of Korea in the 1590s. There his armies became bogged down, and his fleets were shattered by the brilliant Korean admiral Yi Sun-sin. The wars ended in costly failure with Hideyoshi's death in 1598. He left only a young son under the guardianship of rivals; within two years Tokugawa Ieyasu had seized power. Yet as the unifier of Japan, the peasant who ruled an empire remains one of his nation's most celebrated figures.
