Claude Lorrain

French Baroque landscape painter (c. 1600–1682)

Claude Lorrain was the supreme master of the ideal landscape, whose serene, golden-lit visions of an imagined classical world set the standard for landscape painting for two centuries.

Born Claude Gellée in the duchy of Lorraine—from which his nickname comes—he was of humble birth and orphaned young, making his way to Rome as a boy, where he is said to have begun as a pastry cook before training as a painter. Rome became his home for life, and there he rose to be the most sought-after landscapist of the age, his harbours and pastoral idylls bathed in a luminous light he was among the first to paint by looking straight toward the sun.

So widely was he imitated that he kept a book of drawings, the Liber Veritatis, recording each composition to expose the forgeries that flooded the market. His patrons ranged from popes to the princes of Europe, and his influence outlasted them all. The poet Johann Wolfgang von Goethe held him up as the painter who showed nature not as it is but as it ought to be, and in England his vision was worshipped: John Constable copied his canvases as a young man, and J. M. W. Turner so revered him that he willed his own paintings to hang forever beside Claude's.

He died in Rome in 1682, the acknowledged king of his art. The other great French painter who had made Rome his home in the same years—his austere classical counterpart, with whom he is forever paired—was Nicolas Poussin.
