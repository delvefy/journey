William the Conqueror

Norman conqueror and king of England (c. 1028–1087)

William the Conqueror, born around 1028, was the illegitimate son of Robert, Duke of Normandy, and succeeded to the duchy as a boy amid violence and treachery that he survived by sheer toughness and cunning. Known in his youth as "William the Bastard," he grew into a formidable warrior and ruler who mastered the unruly Norman barons and made his duchy one of the strongest powers in France.

His claim to greatness came across the Channel. William asserted that his kinsman Edward the Confessor, the childless king of England, had promised him the English throne, and that Harold Godwinson had sworn to support that claim. When Edward died and Harold took the crown in 1066, William gathered an army and a fleet and invaded. On 14 October 1066, at the Battle of Hastings, after a long and desperate fight, Harold was killed and his army broken. On Christmas Day William was crowned king of England.

The Conquest transformed England utterly. William dispossessed the Anglo-Saxon aristocracy and replaced it with a Norman ruling class, built castles—including the Tower of London—to hold the country down, and crushed rebellions with ferocity, devastating the north in the "Harrying of the North." He bound the land in a new feudal order and, in 1086, commissioned the Domesday Book, an extraordinary survey of the kingdom's wealth.

William ruled England and Normandy until his death in 1087, from a riding injury, and his line founded the long succession of English monarchs. The Norman Conquest reshaped the English language, law, and aristocracy forever. Few single events, and few single men, have so permanently altered the course of a nation's history.
