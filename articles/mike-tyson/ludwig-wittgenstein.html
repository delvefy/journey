Ludwig Wittgenstein

Austrian-British philosopher (1889–1951)

Ludwig Wittgenstein was born in Vienna in 1889 into one of the richest families of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, the son of a steel magnate and patron of the arts. Trained first as an engineer in Berlin and Manchester, he grew fascinated by the foundations of mathematics and travelled to Cambridge to study under Bertrand Russell, who quickly judged him a genius.

He served in the Austro-Hungarian army through the First World War, much of it at the front, and composed his first masterpiece amid the fighting and a prisoner-of-war camp. Published in 1921, the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus argued that the limits of language are the limits of the world, ending with the famous injunction that "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." Believing he had solved the problems of philosophy, he gave away his inherited fortune and withdrew to teach in remote Austrian village schools.

By 1929 he had returned to Cambridge and to philosophy, repudiating much of his early work. His later thought, gathered after his death in the Philosophical Investigations (1953), abandoned the search for hidden logical structure and treated meaning as a matter of use within shared "language games."

Austere, intense and self-tormenting, he inspired devotion and unease in equal measure. He died of cancer in Cambridge in 1951, reportedly asking that the friends gathered there be told he had had a wonderful life. His two very different philosophies each reshaped the course of twentieth-century thought.
