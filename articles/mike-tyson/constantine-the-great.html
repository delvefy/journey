Constantine the Great

Roman emperor, first Christian emperor (c. 272–337)

Constantine the Great was the emperor who turned the Roman Empire toward Christianity and reshaped the course of Western history. Son of the western Caesar Constantius and of Helena, he rose to prominence within the complex power-sharing system of Diocletian's Tetrarchy and was hailed emperor by his troops at York in 306, on his father's death. A long series of civil wars followed as he fought to make himself master of the whole Roman world.

The turning point came in 312 at the Battle of the Milvian Bridge outside Rome. According to tradition Constantine saw a sign of the Cross in the sky with the words "in this sign conquer," fought under the Christian symbol, and crushed his rival Maxentius. The next year, with his co-emperor Licinius, he issued the Edict of Milan, granting toleration to the long-persecuted Christians. By 324 he had defeated Licinius too and ruled alone.

Constantine threw the weight of the throne behind the Church. He summoned the First Council of Nicaea in 325, which formulated the Nicene Creed against the Arian heresy, and he lavished patronage on great basilicas in Rome and the Holy Land. On the Bosphorus he founded a magnificent new capital, Constantinople, the "New Rome" that would outlast the old by a thousand years.

Baptized only as he lay dying in 337, he had nonetheless set empire and continent on a Christian path. He had risen to power within the system created by the formidable soldier-emperor who reorganized the Roman world a generation before him—Diocletian.
