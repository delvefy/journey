Plotinus

Founder of Neoplatonism (c. 204–270)

Plotinus was the last great philosopher of the ancient world and the founder of Neoplatonism, the system that crowned a thousand years of Greek thought. Born in Roman Egypt, he studied for eleven years in Alexandria under the enigmatic Ammonius Saccas, then made his way to Rome, where he opened a school and taught for the rest of his life. So revered was he that senators and even the emperor flocked to hear him, and he ascetic master once dreamed of founding a city of philosophers.

Plotinus saw himself not as an innovator but as a faithful interpreter of Plato, whose ideas he fused with elements of Aristotle into a sublime vision of reality. From a single ineffable source, "the One," all things flow in descending order—the divine Intellect, the Soul, and at last the material world. The goal of human life, he taught, is for the soul to turn inward and upward, retracing the path of emanation until it achieves mystical union with the One.

He wrote his treatises in difficult, compressed Greek, and it fell to his devoted pupil to gather and arrange them into the six sets of nine that are known as the Enneads.

Plotinus's influence was vast and lasting: his thought shaped Christian theology—above all that of Augustine—as well as Islamic and Jewish philosophy and the Renaissance. The disciple who preserved his teaching and wrote his life was Porphyry.
