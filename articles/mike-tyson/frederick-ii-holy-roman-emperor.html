Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor

Holy Roman Emperor and King of Sicily, “Stupor Mundi” (1194–1250)

Frederick II was so dazzling and so strange a ruler that his own age called him Stupor Mundi, the Wonder of the World. Grandson of Frederick Barbarossa and heir through his mother to the Norman kingdom of Sicily, he was orphaned young and grew up in the polyglot brilliance of Palermo, fluent in many tongues and at home among Latin, Greek, and Arabic learning alike.

From that island base he built one of the most advanced states in Europe, codifying its laws in the Constitutions of Melfi and founding the University of Naples to train its servants. His court was a marvel: he gathered poets who wrote the first verse in an Italian tongue, kept astrologers and mathematicians about him, maintained a menagerie of exotic beasts, and himself composed a precise and famous treatise on the hunting of birds.

His crusade was as unconventional as everything else he did. Excommunicated and forbidden to go, he sailed east anyway and, by patient negotiation rather than battle, persuaded the Sultan of Egypt to cede Jerusalem, then crowned himself king of the holy city. To Rome this was scandal upon scandal.

For most of his reign he was locked in a death-struggle with the papacy, excommunicated again and again, branded Antichrist, and at last declared deposed, while he answered with armies and bitter propaganda. He died suddenly in 1250, and within a generation his Hohenstaufen house was hunted to extinction. So vivid was his reputation that Dante set him among the heretics of the Inferno. The red-bearded grandfather whose imperial greatness he inherited and magnified was Frederick Barbarossa.
