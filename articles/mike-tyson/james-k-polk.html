James K. Polk

Eleventh President of the United States (1795–1849)

James K. Polk was the great expansionist president, a disciplined and single-minded executive who in one term enlarged the United States to span the continent. Born in North Carolina and raised in Tennessee, he rose as a protégé of Andrew Jackson—earning the nickname Young Hickory—and served as Speaker of the House and governor of his state before bursting onto the national stage.

In 1844 he became the first true dark-horse nominee, plucked from relative obscurity with Jackson's backing to defeat the veteran Henry Clay. He entered office with a precise list of goals and achieved nearly all of them in four punishing years.

He completed the annexation of Texas, settled the long dispute with Britain over the Oregon Country at the forty-ninth parallel, and lowered the tariff. Above all he led the nation into the Mexican-American War, a conflict that ended in 1848 with the vast Mexican Cession—California and the future Southwest—fulfilling the dream of Manifest Destiny but sharpening the deadly quarrel over the spread of slavery.

True to his pledge, he served only one term, and the labour of the office broke his health; he died of cholera barely three months after leaving the White House. The war that won him a continent had been waged against Mexico's dominant soldier-statesman, Antonio López de Santa Anna.
