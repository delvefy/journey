Ashoka the Great

Mauryan emperor and patron of Buddhism (c. 304–232 BC)

Ashoka was the third emperor of the Maurya dynasty, grandson of its founder Chandragupta and son of Bindusara. By the time he came to the throne around 268 BC he ruled an empire that covered almost the entire Indian subcontinent, governed from the great capital of Pataliputra. In his early reign he was a conventional warrior-king, extending his dominions by the sword.

The turning point of his life was the conquest of Kalinga, on the eastern coast, in about 261 BC. The slaughter and suffering of that campaign filled Ashoka with such remorse that, by his own testimony carved in stone, he renounced war and embraced the teaching of the Buddha. He devoted the rest of his reign to dhamma—righteousness, compassion, and non-violence—toward all living things.

Ashoka proclaimed his new principles in edicts inscribed on rocks and on tall polished sandstone pillars erected across the empire, among the oldest deciphered writings of Indian history. They urged tolerance among sects, care for the poor and for animals, honesty in officials, and respect between subjects. The lion capital that crowned one of these pillars at Sarnath became, more than two thousand years later, the national emblem of independent India, and its wheel the centre of the Indian flag.

He became Buddhism's great royal patron, building monuments to enshrine relics, convening a council of monks, and dispatching missionaries far beyond his borders—his own son Mahinda is remembered as the apostle of Sri Lanka. Through Ashoka the faith spread across Asia. The sage whose teaching transformed his reign was Gautama Buddha.
