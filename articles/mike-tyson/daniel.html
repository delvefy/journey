Daniel

Exile and prophet of the Babylonian captivity (traditionally 6th century BC)

Daniel is the hero of the biblical book that bears his name, a young Judean of noble birth carried to Babylon in the exile and raised, by the account, to high office in the courts of its kings. From the start he is shown as faithful amid a foreign world, refusing the royal food to keep the law of his fathers and trusting his God to vindicate him.

His fame rests on a series of unforgettable scenes. He interprets Nebuchadnezzar's troubling dreams—the great statue of four metals that figures the rise and fall of empires, the felled tree that foretells the king's madness. His three companions walk unharmed through a fiery furnace rather than bow to an idol. And he himself, condemned for praying to his God, passes a night unscathed among the lions.

The book's later chapters turn to soaring apocalyptic vision: beasts rising from the sea, kingdoms succeeding kingdoms, and one “like a son of man” coming with the clouds of heaven to receive an everlasting dominion—imagery that would shape Jewish hope and, through the Gospels, the words of Jesus. Scholars debate when the book took shape and how much history underlies its hero, but as a figure of steadfast faith Daniel has never lost his hold on the imagination.

The most dramatic of his court scenes unfolds at a doomed royal banquet, where a hand writes upon the wall the doom of a kingdom and Daniel alone can read it. The king whose feast that was, the last to rule in Babylon, was Belshazzar.
