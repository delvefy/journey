Joseph Montgolfier

French inventor of the balloon (1740–1810)

Joseph-Michel Montgolfier, son of a paper-making family of Annonay, was the dreamer who, with his younger brother Étienne, first lifted human beings off the earth. Watching smoke and embers rise, Joseph conceived that a "cloud in a bag" might be made to float, and the brothers built great envelopes of paper and cloth that, filled with heated air over a fire, tugged upward and climbed into the sky.

After a first public ascent of an unmanned balloon at Annonay in June 1783, they were summoned to perform before the court. In September of that year, in the gardens of Versailles, a Montgolfier balloon rose before King Louis XVI and Queen Marie Antoinette bearing the first aerial passengers in history—a sheep, a duck, and a rooster—sent aloft to prove that living creatures could survive in the upper air.

Two months later came the supreme moment: over Paris, before vast crowds, two men ascended in a Montgolfier balloon and made the first free human flight. Among the witnesses was Benjamin Franklin, who, when a skeptic asked what use the new invention could be, famously replied, "What use is a newborn baby?"

Celebrated and ennobled, the brothers gave their name to the hot-air balloon, the "montgolfière," and opened the age of human flight. The queen who watched their balloon rise at Versailles, and who would meet a darker fate, was Marie Antoinette.
