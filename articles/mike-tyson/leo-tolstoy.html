Leo Tolstoy

Russian novelist and moral philosopher (1828–1910)

Leo Tolstoy is regarded by many as the greatest novelist who ever lived. Born to the Russian aristocracy and seasoned as a young officer in the Crimean War, he poured the whole of life into two colossal masterpieces: War and Peace, the sweeping epic of Russia in the age of Napoleon, and Anna Karenina, the tragedy of love and society often called the most perfect of all novels. His art combined a panoramic grasp of history with an almost miraculous intimacy with the inner lives of his characters.

At the height of his fame he was seized by a spiritual crisis that remade him. Renouncing the church, the state, and his own wealth, he preached a stark Christian ethic drawn from the Sermon on the Mount—nonresistance to evil, simplicity, universal love—in works like The Kingdom of God Is Within You. The Orthodox Church excommunicated him; pilgrims from around the world came to his estate to hear the sage.

His youthful devotion to Rousseau and his later debt to Schopenhauer fed this moral vision, and his doctrine of nonviolence reached across the world to inspire a young lawyer in South Africa, Mahatma Gandhi, with whom he corresponded in his last year. Tolstoy died in 1910 after fleeing his home in the night, expiring at a remote railway station as the whole world watched.

The younger writer whom he befriended and admired, the master of the modern short story and the stage, was Anton Chekhov.
