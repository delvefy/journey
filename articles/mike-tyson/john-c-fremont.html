John C. Frémont

American explorer, general, and politician (1813–1890)

John Charles Frémont won fame as “the Pathfinder” for the expeditions that mapped and publicised the American West. An army topographical engineer, he led a series of journeys in the 1840s across the Oregon Trail, the Great Basin he named, and the snowbound passes of the Sierra Nevada into California. His vivid expedition reports, polished with his wife Jessie Benton Frémont, became bestsellers that lured thousands of settlers westward.

He was swept up in the conquest of California during the Mexican-American War, only to be court-martialed for insubordination amid a quarrel between rival commanders, though the president remitted his sentence. He served briefly as one of California's first senators.

In 1856 the new Republican Party chose Frémont as its first presidential candidate, running on opposition to the spread of slavery; he lost to James Buchanan. In the Civil War he was made a general and given command in Missouri, where in 1861 he issued his own order freeing the slaves of rebels—a step Abraham Lincoln countermanded before relieving him. He flirted with a rival candidacy against Lincoln in 1864 before standing aside.

His later railroad ventures collapsed, and he ended as a territorial governor of Arizona, dying in relative obscurity in 1890. His expeditions had been made possible by his celebrated guide, the frontiersman Kit Carson.
