Pope Julius II

Pope and Renaissance patron (1443–1513)

Giuliano della Rovere, who reigned as Pope Julius II from 1503 to 1513, was the most formidable pontiff of the Renaissance, so warlike that he earned the nickname "il Papa terribile." After years of rivalry with the Borgia pope Alexander VI, he seized the papacy and ruled it like a prince, personally donning armor and leading armies to recover and expand the Papal States and to drive foreign powers from Italy through a shifting succession of leagues and alliances.

Yet his ferocity was matched by a magnificent vision for the arts that made Rome the capital of the High Renaissance. He compelled a reluctant Michelangelo to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel and to begin a grand tomb crowned by the statue of Moses. He set the young Raphael to adorning the papal apartments, where the School of Athens took shape. And he charged Bramante with tearing down the ancient basilica of St. Peter's to raise a new one, the greatest church in Christendom.

His reach extended across Europe: it was Julius who granted the dispensation permitting the young Henry VIII of England to marry his brother's widow, Catherine of Aragon—a decision whose unraveling would later convulse a kingdom.

Mocked by the humanist Erasmus as too worldly for heaven, Julius died in 1513, leaving the Church militarily strengthened and, above all, immortalized in the art his ambition had summoned into being.
