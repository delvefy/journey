Xenophon

Athenian soldier, historian and writer (c. 430–354 BC)

Xenophon, born about 430 BC, was soldier, historian, and man of letters all at once, and a devoted follower of Socrates. His most famous adventure became his most famous book. In 401 BC he marched into the Persian empire with the Ten Thousand, a Greek mercenary force hired by the prince Cyrus the Younger to wrest the throne from his brother. At Cunaxa, near Babylon, Cyrus was killed and the Greek commanders were lured to their deaths, leaving the army stranded a thousand miles from home.

In the Anabasis, Xenophon tells how the leaderless soldiers chose new generals—himself among them—and fought their way north through mountains and hostile tribes until, from a high ridge, they at last saw the water and raised the cry "The sea! The sea!" The tale of that desperate march has thrilled readers ever since.

Exiled from Athens for his service with Persia and Sparta, Xenophon settled on a country estate and wrote prolifically in a clear, graceful Attic prose. He continued the unfinished history of Thucydides in his Hellenica, and in the Cyropaedia he composed an idealised life of Cyrus the Great as the model of a wise ruler, a book later prized by princes and statesmen.

Above all he preserved the memory of his teacher. In the Memorabilia and his own Apology and Symposium he defended and portrayed Socrates, offering, alongside the dialogues of Plato, our fullest picture of the philosopher who shaped them both.
