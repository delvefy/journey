Michel Ney

Marshal of France (1769–1815)

Michel Ney was the most celebrated of Napoleon's marshals for sheer courage, a soldier the emperor called the bravest of the brave. The son of a barrel-maker from the Franco-German borderland, he rose through the cavalry of the Revolution by talent and daring, and was among the first marshals Napoleon created in 1804. He fought with distinction across the great campaigns, from Elchingen and Jena to Friedland.

His legend was sealed in the retreat from Russia in 1812, when he commanded the rearguard through the snows and was said to be the last French soldier to quit Russian soil, fighting on foot with a musket among his men.

His loyalties were tested at the fall of the empire. Having sworn allegiance to the restored king, he was sent in 1815 to arrest the returning Napoleon and vowed to bring him back in an iron cage—only to embrace his old master and rejoin him. At Waterloo he hurled the French cavalry again and again against the British squares of the Duke of Wellington, having several horses killed beneath him, but could not break them.

After the final defeat the Bourbon government tried him for treason and shot him in Paris in December 1815; by tradition he gave the firing squad its order himself. He had risen as a marshal alongside Joachim Murat and served the cause of Napoleon to the end. The king who signed his death was Louis XVIII.
