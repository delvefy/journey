Gattamelata

Italian condottiero (c. 1370–1443)

Erasmo da Narni, remembered by his odd nickname Gattamelata—“the honeyed cat”—rose from humble beginnings in an Umbrian town to become one of the most respected soldiers of his day. Tradition made him the son of a baker; whatever his origins, he learned the trade of arms in the hard school of the early condottieri and climbed by steady competence rather than flamboyance.

He served the Pope and Florence before entering the long employ of Venice, which raised him to captain-general of its armies. In that office he led the republic's forces through the grinding wars against Milan and its duke, Filippo Maria Visconti, holding the line in Lombardy with a soldier's patience. A stroke disabled him near the end, and he died at Padua in 1443.

It was in death that he won lasting fame. To honor him, the city set the sculptor Donatello to cast a bronze equestrian statue—a calm, commanding rider on a great horse—the first such monument made since the days of ancient Rome, and one of the founding works of Renaissance sculpture. Through it the modest captain became an emblem of the age's revival of antiquity.

His career belonged to the world of the hired captains, men like Francesco Sforza and, in the next generation, Bartolomeo Colleoni, who turned warfare into a profession and sometimes into a throne. The famous early condottiero in whose following the young Gattamelata first learned his craft, lord of Perugia and founder of a whole school of war, was Braccio da Montone.
