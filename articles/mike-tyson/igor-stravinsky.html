Igor Stravinsky

Russian composer (1882–1971)

Igor Stravinsky was among the most important and influential composers of the twentieth century, a restless innovator who reinvented his art more than once across a long career.

A pupil of Rimsky-Korsakov, he was lifted to sudden fame by three ballets written for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes in Paris—The Firebird, Petrushka, and above all The Rite of Spring, whose savage rhythms and harsh harmonies set off a near-riot at its 1913 premiere and changed the direction of music forever.

After the war he turned to a leaner, cooler manner, the “neoclassical” style, reaching back to older forms; his ballet Pulcinella was staged with designs by his friend Pablo Picasso, and he paid loving homage to Tchaikovsky, the master he revered above all Russian composers. In old age, settled in America, he astonished the world again by taking up the serial technique of a younger generation.

He moved among the great artists of his time and was sheltered for a season by Coco Chanel. Among his fellow composers, none did he engage more closely as friend and rival than Claude Debussy.
