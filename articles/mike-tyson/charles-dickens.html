Charles Dickens

English novelist (1812–1870)

Charles Dickens was the most popular novelist of the Victorian age and one of the great storytellers of all literature, who peopled the world with unforgettable characters and turned fiction into a weapon against social cruelty.

His own childhood gave him his great subject: when his father was thrown into debtors' prison, the boy was sent to labour in a blacking factory, a humiliation he never forgot. He rose through journalism, and the runaway success of The Pickwick Papers made him famous at twenty-four. There followed a torrent of serialized masterpieces—Oliver Twist, A Christmas Carol, David Copperfield, Bleak House, A Tale of Two Cities, Great Expectations—crowded with comedy, sentiment, and fierce indignation at poverty, the law, and the treatment of the poor.

He drew on the English tradition that ran back to William Shakespeare, whom he revered, and his influence flowed outward across the world: in Russia, Fyodor Dostoevsky absorbed his portraits of the humiliated and the poor, while Leo Tolstoy kept his image on the wall and called him the greatest of novelists. At the height of his fame his public readings drew adoring crowds on both sides of the Atlantic, and Queen Victoria herself, a devoted reader, received him near the end of his life.

Exhausted by overwork and tangled in a painful private life, he died in 1870 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. Among the writers who sought out his friendship was a celebrated visitor from Denmark, who came to stay at Dickens's country house in 1857 and lingered for five weeks until even the genial host longed for him to leave—the fairy-tale teller Hans Christian Andersen.
