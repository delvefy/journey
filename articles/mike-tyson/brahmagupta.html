Brahmagupta

Indian mathematician and astronomer (c. 598–668)

Brahmagupta was the foremost Indian mathematician and astronomer of his age, working at or near the great observatory city of Ujjain. In 628 he composed the Brahmasphutasiddhanta, the "Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma," a work that would carry Indian mathematics across the world.

His most lasting contribution was to the very idea of number. Brahmagupta was the first to set down systematic rules for calculating with zero, treating it as a number in its own right rather than a mere placeholder, and the first to lay out the arithmetic of negative quantities, which he framed as "debts" set against "fortunes." These rules underlie all later algebra.

He went much further. He gave a celebrated formula for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral, methods for solving quadratic and certain indeterminate equations, and techniques of interpolation for his astronomical tables. His later work, the Khandakhadyaka, refined the practical computation of the positions of the planets.

In astronomy he wrote within the tradition of his predecessor Aryabhata, whose system he both used and sharply criticized—rejecting, for instance, Aryabhata's bold suggestion that the Earth turns on its axis. Brahmagupta held that the Earth stood fixed, yet his reckonings of eclipses and planetary motion were of remarkable accuracy.

When the Brahmasphutasiddhanta reached the court of the Abbasid caliphs in the eighth century, it became the "Sindhind" that shaped early Islamic astronomy and the work of al-Khwarizmi. Through that channel Brahmagupta's zero and decimal arithmetic passed to the Arab world and at last to Europe, making him one of the pivotal figures in the history of mathematics.
