Orville Wright

American aviation pioneer (1871–1948)

Orville Wright, with his elder brother Wilbur, achieved one of humanity's oldest dreams: powered flight. The two were self-taught engineers who ran a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio, and brought to the problem of flying a patient, methodical genius. Building their own wind tunnel to gather reliable data, they cracked the central difficulty that had defeated all before them—how to control a craft in the air—by devising a system to balance and steer it about all three axes.

On the windy dunes at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, on the 17th of December 1903, Orville lay at the controls and made the first powered, sustained, and controlled flight of a heavier-than-air machine—twelve seconds and a hundred and twenty feet that changed the world. The brothers refined their invention into the first truly practical aeroplane, and their demonstrations in America and Europe a few years later made them famous across the globe.

After Wilbur's early death from typhoid in 1912, Orville carried on alone, defended their patents, and then sold the business to become a revered elder of aviation. He lived until 1948, long enough to see his fragile invention grow into airliners and bombers—and to know the great fliers who followed, among them Charles Lindbergh.

The achievement was never his alone. At every step it was shared with his brother and partner in the adventure, Wilbur Wright.
