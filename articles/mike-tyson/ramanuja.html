Ramanuja

Tamil philosopher of Vishishtadvaita Vedanta (c. 1017–1137)

Ramanuja was the greatest teacher of Sri Vaishnavism and the founder of the school of Vishishtadvaita, "qualified non-dualism." A Tamil Brahmin born at Sriperumbudur, he became the most powerful counterweight to the austere monism of Adi Shankara and gave philosophical form to the worship of a personal God.

Against Shankara's teaching that the world is mere appearance and the self ultimately identical with an impersonal absolute, Ramanuja held that Brahman is the supreme personal Lord—Vishnu, or Narayana—and that finite souls and matter are wholly real, forming as it were the body of which God is the soul. Unity and difference are both true: the many are real modes of the one.

He set out this vision in his Sri Bhashya, a great commentary on the Brahma Sutras of Vyasa composed in deliberate answer to Shankara, and in works such as the Vedartha Sangraha and his commentary on the Bhagavad Gita. He wedded this metaphysics to the path of bhakti, loving devotion, and prapatti, total surrender to the grace of God.

As head of the great temple at Srirangam he reorganized its worship and, drawing on the Tamil hymns of the Alvar saints, carried devotion to a wider community across the barriers of caste. Tradition tells of his flight from a hostile Chola king to the Hoysala country, where he won the local ruler to the Vaishnava faith and founded the shrine of Melkote.

Ramanuja's qualified non-dualism became the great theistic alternative within Vedanta, and his stress on grace and devotion shaped later Hindu piety profoundly. The road of Vedantic debate would pass next to Madhva, who pressed the case for difference further still.
