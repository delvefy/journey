Otto Lilienthal

German aviation pioneer (1848–1896)

Otto Lilienthal was the first man to fly in a way the world could see and believe. A German engineer who had studied the flight of birds since boyhood, he set out to master gliding by experiment, and from the early 1890s he made more than two thousand flights from the hills around Berlin—even heaping up an artificial conical hill so he could launch into the wind from any direction. Photographs of him soaring beneath his great bat-like wings circled the globe and proved, as nothing before had, that heavier-than-air flight was truly possible.

He was a careful scientist as well as a daring birdman. His book on bird flight as the basis of aviation, with its tables of the lift given by curved wings, became a foundational text, and his data and his example were studied intently by every serious experimenter who followed—above all by the Wright brothers, who built upon his work and then corrected it.

In 1896 a sudden stall sent his glider plunging to the earth; fatally injured, he is remembered for the stoic last words, "sacrifices must be made." His death, as much as his flights, spurred others to take up the challenge he had left unfinished.

Lilienthal himself built upon an older foundation, the science of flight laid down by the English baronet who first set out its principles—Sir George Cayley.
