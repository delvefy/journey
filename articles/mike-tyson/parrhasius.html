Parrhasius

Greek painter (late 5th century BC)

Parrhasius, born at Ephesus and active in Athens, was among the supreme painters of classical Greece and the great rival of Zeuxis. Where others were praised for colour or for the modelling of light, Parrhasius was held the master of the contour line, the subtle, living outline that seems to suggest the rounded form behind it; later critics judged that in this he had never been surpassed. He was famous, too, for capturing feeling and character in a face.

His victory over Zeuxis in their celebrated contest—deceiving the painter himself with a curtain that was only paint—made his name proverbial. Success made him insufferably vain: he went about in purple robes and a golden crown, styled himself the prince of painters, and boasted of descent from the gods.

The philosopher Socrates, in a conversation preserved by Xenophon, sought him out to ask whether so imitative an art could represent things that have no shape—the moods and character of the soul—and led the painter to admit that the greatest pictures do indeed render the workings of the mind through the body's expression.

None of his work survives, and we know him, like the other Greek masters, only through the words of those who saw or recorded the paintings. Chief among those who preserved his fame was the Roman encyclopedist Pliny the Elder.
