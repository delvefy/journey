Henri Rousseau

French naive painter (1844–1910)

Henri Rousseau was the self-taught French painter whose dreamlike, “naive” canvases, mocked in his lifetime, came to be cherished as visionary masterpieces.

For most of his working life he was a humble toll collector in Paris—hence his nickname, Le Douanier, the customs officer—and he took up painting seriously only in middle age, without formal training. Critics jeered at his flattened perspective and childlike directness, yet he painted on with serene self-belief, exhibiting at the open Salon des Indépendants. His most famous works are lush, hallucinatory jungles—a tiger lashed by a tropical storm, a sleeping gypsy beneath the moon, a nude reclining in dense foliage—though he never left France, conjuring them from the botanical gardens and picture books of Paris.

Where the academy saw incompetence, the avant-garde saw genius. Pablo Picasso, stumbling on one of his canvases, threw a famous banquet in his honor at the ramshackle studios of Montmartre, and the poet Guillaume Apollinaire sang his praises. He died in 1910, still poor and only half-recognized, just as the tide of opinion was turning decisively in his favor.

His influence on modern art proved immense. Among those who held him up as a model of pure, authentic vision—reproducing his work in a famous almanac of the new art—was the pioneer of abstraction, Wassily Kandinsky.
