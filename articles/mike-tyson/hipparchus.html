Hipparchus

Greek astronomer (c. 190–120 BC)

Hipparchus, born at Nicaea and working chiefly on the island of Rhodes, was the foremost astronomer of antiquity before Ptolemy and the founder of trigonometry. Almost all his writings are lost, and he is known largely because the later Almagest preserved and built upon his achievements—but those achievements were extraordinary.

Comparing his own careful observations with older records handed down from Babylon, Hipparchus detected an almost imperceptible drift in the positions of the stars relative to the equinoxes—the slow wheeling now called the precession of the equinoxes, one of the most subtle discoveries of ancient science. He compiled a great catalogue of the fixed stars, several hundred in number, and ranked them by brightness in a scale of magnitudes that astronomers use to this day.

To turn astronomy into a quantitative science he drew up the first known table of chords, in effect the first trigonometric table, and with it built geometric models of the motions of the Sun and Moon, refining the lengths of the month and the year and estimating the Moon's distance with impressive accuracy.

A meticulous and skeptical observer, he insisted on measurement over speculation and bequeathed to later ages the data and the methods on which Ptolemy would raise his system of the heavens. Among the bold theorists of his own tradition was one whose ideas Hipparchus weighed and largely set aside—Aristarchus of Samos, who had dared to place the Sun at the centre of the world.
