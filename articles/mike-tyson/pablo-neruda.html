Pablo Neruda

Chilean poet and Nobel laureate (1904–1973)

Pablo Neruda was the Chilean poet of love and revolution, a Nobel laureate whose vast, sensuous body of work made him one of the most widely read poets of the twentieth century.

Famous at nineteen for his Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair, he spent years as a Chilean consul in Asia and Europe. Posted to Spain, he befriended Federico García Lorca and the Republican poets, and the horror of the Civil War turned him into a committed communist. Out of that conviction, and out of a Whitmanesque ambition learned from Walt Whitman, came his epic Canto General, a sweeping song of the land, history and peoples of the Americas.

Hounded at times for his politics and forced into exile, he nonetheless became a beloved national figure and a giant of world letters; Gabriel García Márquez called him the greatest poet of the twentieth century in any language. He won the Nobel Prize in 1971.

His last act on the public stage was political. He threw his prestige behind Chile's socialist president, serving as his ambassador to France and campaigning for his cause. When that government was destroyed by a military coup in September 1973, the cancer-stricken Neruda died days later, and his ransacked funeral became the first act of public defiance against the new regime. The president he had served and championed was Salvador Allende.
