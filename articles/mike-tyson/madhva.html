Madhva

Founder of Dvaita Vedanta (1238–1317)

Madhva was the founder of Dvaita, the dualist school of Vedanta, and the third of the great teachers—after Shankara and Ramanuja—who shaped Hindu philosophy through rival readings of a common scripture. Born near Udupi in Karnataka and known in religion as Anandatirtha, he taught a vision of reality as irreducibly plural.

Where Shankara had declared all things ultimately one, Madhva insisted on real and eternal differences—between God and the soul, between one soul and another, between souls and matter. Vishnu alone is the independent reality; everything else depends utterly upon him. Salvation comes by God's grace through devotion, and Madhva held, almost uniquely in Hindu thought, that souls differ eternally in their natures and their destinies.

He expounded this in commentaries on the Brahma Sutras of Vyasa, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Upanishads, and in a body of some thirty-seven works; tradition holds that he journeyed to the Himalayas to study at the feet of Vyasa himself. He framed his system in sharp polemic against the Advaita of Shankara, and apart from the qualified non-dualism of Ramanuja.

At Udupi he installed a celebrated image of Krishna and founded eight monasteries to maintain its worship, an institution that endures to this day. By tradition he believed himself an incarnation of the wind-god Vayu, sent into the world to restore true doctrine.

From Madhva's school sprang the Haridasa movement, a flowering of Kannada devotional song whose greatest figure, Purandara Dasa, is honoured as the father of Carnatic music. Through philosophy and through that music alike, Madhva's dualism became one of the enduring currents of Indian religion.
