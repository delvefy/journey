Madame de Pompadour

Mistress and confidante of Louis XV (1721–1764)

Jeanne Antoinette Poisson was born in Paris in 1721 into a financier's family of doubtful reputation. Clever, beautiful, and carefully educated, she shone in the salons and, married young to a wealthy man, set her sights higher still. In 1745 she caught the attention of Louis XV at a masked ball, became his official mistress, and was raised to the nobility as the Marquise de Pompadour.

Her hold on the king outlasted their physical affair. For nearly twenty years she remained his trusted companion and one of the most influential figures at Versailles, consulted on appointments and policy. She helped advance ministers such as the Duc de Choiseul and lent her support to the Diplomatic Revolution that aligned France with Austria, a turn that led into the disastrous Seven Years' War.

Pompadour's most lasting fame rests on her patronage of art and thought. She championed painters and the Sèvres porcelain works, shaped the Rococo taste of her age, and extended her protection to the philosophers of the Enlightenment. She supported Voltaire's career at court and shielded the great Encyclopédie from suppression when it came under attack.

Her power made her many enemies, who blamed her extravagance and the military defeats on her influence, circulating savage verses against her. Worn down by ill health and the strain of court life, she died at Versailles in 1764, only forty-two years old. Among the writers she defended, none did more to define the century than Denis Diderot.
