Philip II of Spain

King of Spain, champion of the Counter-Reformation (1527–1598)

Philip II ruled the mightiest empire of his age, a dominion so vast it was said the sun never set upon it. Son of the Emperor Charles V, he inherited Spain, the Netherlands, much of Italy, and the silver-rich realms of the Americas when his father abdicated in 1556. A devout and tireless Catholic, he made himself the sword and shield of the Counter-Reformation and governed his sprawling possessions through an endless flow of paperwork from his desk in the austere palace-monastery of the Escorial, which he built outside Madrid.

His reign blazed with both triumph and disaster. In 1571 the fleet of his Holy League, commanded by his half-brother Don John of Austria, smashed Ottoman sea power at the great battle of Lepanto. In 1580 he seized the crown of Portugal, uniting the whole Iberian peninsula under his rule. Yet his attempt to crush Protestantism and rebellion in the Netherlands dragged on for decades and bled his treasury white.

Against Protestant England, ruled by his sister-in-law and rival Elizabeth I, he launched in 1588 the great Armada—a vast fleet meant to carry an invasion. Storms and English guns scattered it in one of history's most famous naval catastrophes. Even the rivers of American silver could not keep pace with his wars, and his crown went bankrupt again and again.

Grave, secretive, and immensely industrious, Philip died at the Escorial in 1598, leaving an empire still unmatched but already strained. His path to the English throne had once seemed open through his marriage to the Catholic queen who was his first royal wife—Mary I of England.
