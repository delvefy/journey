Aristippus

Greek philosopher of Cyrene (c. 435–356 BC)

Aristippus came from Cyrene in North Africa to Athens, drawn by the fame of Socrates, and became his pupil—yet he drew from that teaching a lesson the very opposite of the austere Cynics. Where Antisthenes preached the renunciation of pleasure, Aristippus made pleasure the goal of life and founded the Cyrenaic school upon it.

The good, he taught, is the gentle pleasure of the present sensation; the past is gone and the future uncertain, so a wise man seizes the enjoyment of the moment. He held, too, that we can know only our own feelings, never the things outside us—a bold subjectivism. But his hedonism was no slavery to appetite: his boast was that he could possess fine things without being possessed by them, wearing a rich cloak or a beggar's rag with equal ease and enjoying or surrendering fortune as it came.

He was the first of Socrates's circle to take fees for teaching, and he lived as a polished courtier, gracing the luxurious court of the tyrant of Syracuse. The Cynic Diogenes mocked him for flattering kings; told that if he could live on lentils he need not fawn, Aristippus shot back that if Diogenes knew how to live among men he need not wash lentils.

His easy philosophy of pleasure foreshadowed the later hedonism of Epicurus. The mighty ruler at whose splendid Sicilian court Aristippus practiced his art of graceful living was Dionysius I of Syracuse.
