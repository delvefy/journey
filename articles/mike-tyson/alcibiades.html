Alcibiades

Athenian statesman and general (c. 450–404 BC)

Alcibiades was the most brilliant and most dangerous Athenian of his age: beautiful, eloquent, ambitious, and utterly without scruple. Born around 450 BC into a great family and orphaned of his father young, he was raised in the household of his kinsman Pericles, and as a youth he became the favourite pupil and companion of Socrates, who saved his life in battle at Potidaea and whom he repaid with the famous drunken praise that crowns Plato's Symposium.

In the long Peloponnesian War he rose to lead Athens and pressed his city into its boldest gamble, the great expedition against Sicily in 415 BC. On the very eve of sailing he was implicated in a scandal of religious sacrilege; summoned home to face trial, he chose instead to defect to Sparta, to whom he gave the advice that did Athens grievous harm.

Having seduced the wife of a Spartan king, he fled again, this time to the Persians, and through endless intrigue engineered his recall to Athens, where he won a string of naval victories and returned in triumph in 407 BC. But a single defeat in his absence turned the fickle city against him once more, and he withdrew into exile.

In 404 BC assassins burned his house in Phrygia and cut him down as he fled the flames. Brilliant traitor and patriot by turns, he became the very symbol of the gifted opportunist, and his turbulent career runs like a thread through the great history of the war written by Thucydides.
