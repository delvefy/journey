Boethius

Roman philosopher and statesman (c. 477–524)

Boethius was the last great philosopher of the classical world and a bridge across which much of its learning passed into the Middle Ages—"the last of the Romans and the first of the scholastics." Born into a noble Roman family as the Western Empire gave way to barbarian rule, he was raised in the old learning and rose to the highest honors under the Ostrogothic king who now governed Italy, becoming consul and chief minister of the realm.

His great scholarly ambition was to save Greek philosophy for a Latin world that was forgetting Greek. He set out to translate all of Plato and Aristotle and to show their harmony; though he completed only part, his Latin versions of Aristotle's logic and of Porphyry's introduction to it became the bedrock of medieval learning and shaped the thought of Thomas Aquinas and the schoolmen who followed.

Then fortune turned. Accused of treasonous dealings with the Byzantine East, Boethius was stripped of office, imprisoned, and condemned to death. In his cell, awaiting execution, he wrote his masterpiece, The Consolation of Philosophy, in which Lady Philosophy appears to comfort him and to reason about fortune, happiness, evil, and providence. Drawing deeply on Plato, it became one of the most beloved and widely translated books of the Middle Ages.

He was executed around 524, a casualty of the suspicions of the king he had served. That ruler, the most powerful of the Germanic kings who inherited the Roman West, was Theodoric the Great.
