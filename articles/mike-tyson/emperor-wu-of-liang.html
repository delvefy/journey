Emperor Wu of Liang

Founder and emperor of the Liang dynasty, scholar and patron (464–549)

Xiao Yan, known to history as Emperor Wu of Liang, rose as a general and official under the Southern Qi and in 502 seized the throne to found his own dynasty, the Liang. He ruled the south of China for nearly half a century, one of the longest and most cultivated reigns of the divided age that followed the collapse of the Han.

He was himself a man of letters—poet, musician, and calligrapher—and in his youth one of a celebrated literary circle. As emperor he made his court a centre of learning. He restored the academies of the Confucian classics and revived the rites honouring the Sage, while gathering scholars and poets around him; his son the crown prince compiled there the great anthology that shaped Chinese literary taste for centuries.

Above all he is remembered as the most devout Buddhist ever to sit on a Chinese throne. He built monasteries and pagodas, composed religious commentaries, forbade animal sacrifice in the state cult and turned to a vegetarian diet, and on several occasions theatrically renounced the throne to serve as a temple menial until his ministers ransomed him back. It was he who, by tradition, received and dismissed the monk Bodhidharma.

His long reign ended in catastrophe. He sheltered a treacherous defector, the general Hou Jing, who rose in revolt, stormed the capital, and held the aged emperor captive until he died of want in 549. Among the teachers he revered, the sage whose classics he restored to honour was Confucius.
