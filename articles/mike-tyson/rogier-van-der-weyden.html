Rogier van der Weyden

Early Netherlandish painter (c. 1399–1464)

Rogier van der Weyden, born around 1399 in Tournai, was, with Jan van Eyck, the most important painter of the early Netherlandish school and the most widely imitated artist of his age. He likely trained in the workshop of Robert Campin before settling in Brussels, where by 1435 he had been appointed the official painter to the city.

Where van Eyck dazzled with optical realism, van der Weyden excelled in emotion. His religious paintings convey grief, tenderness and devotion with a restrained, sculptural intensity that moved viewers deeply. His masterpiece, the Descent from the Cross of around 1435, arranges its mourning figures like a carved altarpiece brought to life, their sorrow rendered with unforgettable pathos. The great Beaune Altarpiece of the Last Judgment, commissioned for a hospital in Burgundy, is another monument of his art.

A sought-after portraitist as well, van der Weyden painted the dukes of Burgundy and their courtiers, lending his sitters a cool dignity. Around 1450 he made a pilgrimage to Rome for the Jubilee and worked for Italian patrons, and his influence flowed back across the Alps and throughout the north, shaping painting in Germany, France and Spain for generations.

Unlike many medieval craftsmen, van der Weyden won wealth, civic honour and international renown in his own lifetime. When he died in Brussels in 1464, his expressive style had become the common language of Northern European art, carried forward above all by his pupil Hans Memling.
