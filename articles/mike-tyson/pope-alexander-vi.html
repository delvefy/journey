Pope Alexander VI

Borgia pope (1431–1503)

Rodrigo Borgia, who reigned as Pope Alexander VI from 1492 to 1503, is remembered as the most worldly and notorious of the Renaissance popes. Born near Valencia in Spain, he rose through the Church under the patronage of his uncle Pope Callixtus III, becoming a fabulously wealthy cardinal and vice-chancellor before buying, his enemies charged, his election to the papacy itself.

As pope he made little secret of his ambitions for his family. He openly acknowledged his children—among them Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia—and bent the resources of the Church to their advancement, a nepotism so brazen it scandalized even a cynical age. In Florence he excommunicated and helped destroy the reforming friar Savonarola.

Yet his pontificate also touched the wider world at a decisive moment. In 1493, in the wake of Christopher Columbus's first voyage across the Atlantic, Alexander issued a series of bulls drawing a line down the globe and dividing the newly found lands between Spain and Portugal—an extraordinary assertion of papal authority that shaped the colonization of the Americas and led to the Treaty of Tordesillas.

A lavish patron who adorned the Vatican with the Borgia Apartments, Alexander embodied the splendor and corruption of the Renaissance Church—the very worldliness that would soon help provoke the Protestant Reformation. He died of fever in 1503, and the Borgia fortunes died swiftly with him.
