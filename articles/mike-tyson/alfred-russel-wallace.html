Alfred Russel Wallace

British naturalist and explorer (1823–1913)

Alfred Russel Wallace was the co-discoverer of evolution by natural selection and a founder of the science of biogeography. Of modest means and largely self-taught, he made his living by collecting specimens in the field, financing his own daring expeditions to some of the least-known corners of the tropics.

His first great journey took him up the Amazon, though most of his collections were lost when his ship caught fire on the voyage home. Undeterred, he spent eight years roaming the Malay Archipelago, gathering well over a hundred thousand specimens and noticing the sharp faunal divide—now called the Wallace Line—that separates the animals of Asia from those of Australia.

It was there, shaken by fever on the island of Ternate in 1858, that he hit independently upon the idea of natural selection and posted an essay outlining it to a naturalist he admired. The arrival of that essay prompted the joint announcement of the theory by Wallace and Charles Darwin before the Linnean Society, and spurred Darwin at last to publish.

Generous in giving Darwin priority—he even titled a later book Darwinism—Wallace remained his own man, embracing spiritualism and social reform in ways that set him apart from the scientific establishment. His wanderings had been kindled, like those of so many naturalists of his century, by the example of the great explorer-scientist Alexander von Humboldt.
