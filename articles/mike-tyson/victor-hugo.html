Victor Hugo

Towering poet and novelist of French Romanticism (1802–1885)

Victor Hugo bestrode the literature of nineteenth-century France as poet, novelist, and dramatist, the very embodiment of the Romantic movement. As a young man he led the revolt against the old classical rules of the stage; the riotous opening night of his play Hernani in 1830 became a pitched battle between the generations, and Romanticism won. He revered Shakespeare as his model of untamed genius, and devoted a whole book to him.

His novels reached the whole world. Notre-Dame de Paris conjured medieval Paris around its cathedral and its tragic bell-ringer, while the vast Les Misérables—the tale of the convict Jean Valjean, the policeman Javert, and the barricades—became one of the best-loved books ever written, a sweeping plea for the poor and the outcast. As a poet he was unrivalled in his command of the French language.

He was also a political force. Once a royalist, he became a passionate republican, and when Louis-Napoleon seized power Hugo denounced him and went into nearly twenty years of defiant exile on the Channel Islands, where he wrote some of his greatest work. He returned in 1870 to be hailed as the conscience of the nation.

His death in 1885 brought an immense outpouring of national grief, and he was laid to rest in the Panthéon. His comrade and rival from the days when they stormed the stage together for Romanticism, the inexhaustible spinner of adventures, was Alexandre Dumas.
