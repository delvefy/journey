Charles Lyell

Scottish geologist (1797–1875)

Charles Lyell was born in Scotland in 1797 and trained for the law, but weak eyes and a growing fascination with rocks drew him to geology. Travelling across Europe to examine strata, volcanoes, and river valleys, he gathered the evidence for the book that would reshape the science.

In his Principles of Geology, published in the early 1830s, Lyell argued for uniformitarianism: the doctrine that the Earth had been shaped over immense spans of time by the same slow, ordinary processes—erosion, deposition, volcanism—that can be observed acting today. This directly opposed the catastrophism of Cuvier, with its sudden global revolutions, and revived and systematized the earlier insights of James Hutton.

The book's influence was profound. Charles Darwin carried its first volume aboard HMS Beagle and saw the living world through Lyellian eyes; the vast geological time it described made the gradual evolution of species thinkable. Lyell became Darwin's closest scientific friend and, though long hesitant about evolution, helped arrange the joint announcement of natural selection by Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in 1858.

Knighted and widely honored, Lyell continued to revise his great work through many editions and later took up the question of human antiquity. He died in 1875 and was buried in Westminster Abbey, having done more than any other to give the Earth its true and ancient history.
