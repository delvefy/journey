Anton Chekhov

Russian playwright and master of the short story (1860–1904)

Anton Chekhov was one of the greatest short-story writers in any language and a father of modern drama. The grandson of a serf who had bought his own freedom, he trained as a physician and never quite left medicine—“medicine is my lawful wife,” he joked, “and literature my mistress”—treating the poor for little or nothing even as his fame grew.

In hundreds of stories he perfected a new kind of fiction: spare, unsentimental, and resistant to tidy morals, attending to ordinary lives, fleeting moods, and the things left unsaid. He brought the same revolution to the stage. His great plays—The Seagull, Uncle Vanya, Three Sisters, and The Cherry Orchard—abandoned grand plot for the quiet undercurrents of longing, boredom, and missed connection, and found their ideal interpreter in Konstantin Stanislavski's Moscow Art Theatre, changing the theatre forever.

Admired by the towering Tolstoy and a friend to the rising Gorky, Chekhov stood at the meeting of the old and new in Russian letters. Tuberculosis, which he had diagnosed in himself and long concealed, killed him at only forty-four.

Among the artists he most revered was a great composer, his friend, to whom he proudly dedicated a collection of his stories and with whom he once dreamed of making an opera—Pyotr Tchaikovsky.
