Franz Kafka

German-language writer of Prague (1883–1924)

Franz Kafka was the Prague writer whose nightmarish visions of guilt, alienation and faceless authority gave the modern world a new word for its terrors: Kafkaesque.

Born into a German-speaking Jewish family and oppressed by a domineering father, he trained as a lawyer and spent his days as a conscientious insurance official, writing his strange fictions at night. He published little while he lived: the unforgettable Metamorphosis, in which a man wakes transformed into a monstrous insect, and a handful of stories. His debt to earlier masters was real—he steeped himself in the anguished souls of Fyodor Dostoevsky and modelled his unfinished novel of emigration on the world of Charles Dickens.

His great novels—The Trial, in which a man is arrested and destroyed for a crime never named, and The Castle—he left unfinished, and on his deathbed he asked his friend Max Brod to burn all his manuscripts. Brod refused, and instead published them, an act of beautiful betrayal that gave the world one of its essential writers.

Kafka died of tuberculosis in 1924, only forty and almost unknown. His posthumous influence proved boundless: Albert Camus claimed him for the literature of the absurd, and decades later a young Colombian read the opening line of The Metamorphosis and realized, with a jolt, that one was allowed to write like that. That reader, set free toward magical realism, was Gabriel García Márquez.
