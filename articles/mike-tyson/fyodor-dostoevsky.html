Fyodor Dostoevsky

Russian novelist (1821–1881)

Fyodor Dostoevsky was one of the supreme novelists of world literature and the most searching explorer of the human soul. He won early fame, then in 1849 was arrested for belonging to a circle of liberal intellectuals and condemned to death; he was led out to be shot and reprieved only at the last instant, in a staged mock execution, before being sent to years of hard labour in a Siberian prison. That ordeal seared his faith and his vision forever.

From it came the great novels of his maturity—Crime and Punishment, The Idiot, Demons, and the towering Brothers Karamazov—along with the corrosive Notes from Underground. In them he plumbed guilt and conscience, faith and atheism, free will and the temptations of nihilism, wringing redemption out of suffering. Epileptic, often desperate for money, ruined again and again by a compulsion to gamble, he wrote at white heat under crushing deadlines.

His influence ran deep into the century that followed—into existentialist philosophy and the new science of the mind—and the philosopher Nietzsche hailed him as the only psychologist from whom he had anything to learn. He had begun, fittingly, by translating Balzac into Russian.

His great compatriot and counterpart, the other colossus of the Russian novel against whom he is forever measured, was Leo Tolstoy.
