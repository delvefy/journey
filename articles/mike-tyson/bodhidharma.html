Bodhidharma

Indian monk and founder of Chan (Zen) Buddhism in China (5th–6th century AD)

Bodhidharma is the semi-legendary monk honoured as the founder of Chan—known in Japan as Zen—in China. The traditions describe him as a prince of southern India who renounced his rank, mastered meditation, and travelled by sea to China to plant there the wordless transmission that the Chan school traces back through the patriarchs to the Buddha himself. In that lineage he is counted the twenty-eighth Indian patriarch and the first of China.

The most famous tale of his arrival is his audience with Emperor Wu of Liang, a ruler renowned for building temples, copying scriptures, and supporting the monastic order. When the emperor asked how much merit all his pious works had earned him, Bodhidharma answered bluntly, “No merit at all”—for such acts, he taught, sprang from worldly attachment. The baffled emperor let him go, and Bodhidharma crossed the Yangtze into the north.

There, tradition says, he settled near the Shaolin monastery on Mount Song and sat in meditation facing a wall for nine years. He accepted as his heir the monk Huike, who is said to have stood in the snow and cut off his own arm to prove the sincerity of his longing for the teaching. Later legend also linked Bodhidharma to the physical disciplines of Shaolin.

His teaching was summed up in famous lines: a transmission outside the scriptures, not founded on words, pointing directly to the mind, so that one might see one's own nature and awaken. The devout emperor who first received and dismissed him was Emperor Wu of Liang.
