Louis IX

King of France and saint (1214–1270)

Louis IX came to the throne as a boy of twelve and was guided through a perilous minority by his strong-willed mother, Blanche of Castile, who held the kingdom together against rebellious barons. He grew into the model of the medieval Christian king, so revered for his justice and devotion that, alone among the rulers of France, he was raised to the altar as a saint.

At home he labored to make royal justice fair and accessible, sending out inspectors to root out corrupt officials, curbing the feuds and trials by combat of the nobility, and hearing petitions in person—an image enshrined in the legend of the king dispensing judgment beneath an oak at Vincennes. His piety took visible form in the Sainte-Chapelle, the jewel-box church he raised in Paris to enshrine the Crown of Thorns and other relics he had purchased at vast cost.

Twice he took the cross. On the Seventh Crusade he seized Damietta in Egypt, only to be defeated and captured at Mansurah and ransomed at a great price, after which he lingered for years in the Holy Land. Two decades later, an aging man, he sailed on the Eighth Crusade against Tunis and there died of disease in 1270.

So great was his reputation that his counsel was sought across Christendom, and he tried to mediate the bitter wars between the popes and their imperial enemy. That dazzling, excommunicated ruler of Germany, Sicily, and the empire—the man his age called the Wonder of the World—was Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor.
