Wassily Kandinsky

Russian painter and pioneer of abstraction (1866–1944)

Wassily Kandinsky was the Russian painter widely regarded as a founder of abstract art, who freed painting from the need to depict the visible world.

Born in Moscow, he trained in law and economics and came to art only around the age of thirty. Two experiences pushed him toward his vocation: a performance of Wagner, and the sight of one of Claude Monet's Haystacks, in which he failed at first to recognize the subject and grasped with a shock that colour and form alone could carry a painting's power. Settling in Munich, he became a leader of the avant-garde, co-founding the group Der Blaue Reiter and setting down his ideas in Concerning the Spiritual in Art, which likened painting to music and argued that pure form could express the soul.

Around 1910 he began producing some of the first wholly abstract pictures, swirling compositions of line and colour with no object at all. After the upheavals of revolution and war he joined the Bauhaus, teaching its students a rigorous theory of art, before the Nazis condemned his work as “degenerate” and drove him to spend his final years near Paris, where he died in 1944.

His closest companion through the Blue Rider years and the Bauhaus alike—a fellow painter of luminous, poetic invention—was Paul Klee.
