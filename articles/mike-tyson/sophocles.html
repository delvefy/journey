Sophocles

Athenian tragic playwright (c. 497–406 BC)

Sophocles, born around 497 BC at Colonus near Athens, was the greatest and most honored of the three classical Athenian tragedians. He first triumphed at the dramatic festival of Dionysus around 468 BC, reportedly defeating the older master Aeschylus, and went on to win more victories than any other playwright of his age. Of the some 120 plays he is said to have written, only seven survive complete.

Those survivors include three plays on the Theban legend—Oedipus the King, Oedipus at Colonus, and Antigone—which probe fate, pride, family, and the conflict between human law and divine. Oedipus the King, with its remorseless unfolding of a man's discovery of his own guilt, so impressed Aristotle that he held it up in his Poetics as the model of tragic construction.

Sophocles transformed the art of drama. He added a third actor to the stage, enlarged the chorus, and introduced painted scenery, shifting the emphasis from grand spectacle toward the depth and moral struggle of individual characters. His influence on Western theater has been incalculable.

He was no mere recluse of the study. A respected citizen of Athens during its golden age, he served as a treasurer of the imperial league and as a general alongside Pericles, and held a priesthood. He counted the historian Herodotus among his friends. Living to around ninety, he saw both the height of Athenian glory and the long agony of the Peloponnesian War before his death about 406 BC.
