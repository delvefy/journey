Akira Kurosawa

Japanese film director (1910–1998)

Akira Kurosawa, born in Tokyo in 1910, was the most internationally celebrated of all Japanese filmmakers and one of the supreme directors in the history of cinema. He entered the film industry in the 1930s and, after the Second World War, astonished the world with Rashomon (1950), a tale of a crime recounted in four contradictory versions. Its prize at the Venice Film Festival flung open the door to Japanese cinema for Western audiences.

There followed an extraordinary run of masterpieces. Seven Samurai (1954), an epic of village farmers who hire wandering swordsmen to defend them, became one of the most influential films ever made. Ikiru offered a profound meditation on a dying bureaucrat's search for meaning, and Yojimbo created the archetype of the laconic lone warrior. Kurosawa drew on Western literature as freely as on Japanese tradition, transforming Shakespeare's Macbeth into Throne of Blood and King Lear into the late epic Ran, and Dostoevsky's Idiot into a film. In many of his finest works his leading man was the magnetic Toshiro Mifune.

Kurosawa's influence on world cinema was incalculable. Seven Samurai was remade as the Western The Magnificent Seven, and Yojimbo as A Fistful of Dollars; the structure of The Hidden Fortress helped inspire Star Wars. The leading directors of the New Hollywood—George Lucas, Steven Spielberg, Martin Scorsese—revered him as a master, and when his career faltered they helped finance his comeback films.

A perfectionist with a painter's eye, nicknamed "the Emperor" for his exacting command of the set, Kurosawa directed thirty films across half a century. He received an honorary Academy Award in 1990 and died in 1998, acknowledged everywhere as one of the giants who had shown what cinema could be.
