Charles Darwin

English naturalist (1809–1882)

Charles Robert Darwin gave biology its unifying idea and changed humanity's understanding of its own place in nature. Born at Shrewsbury into a prosperous family—grandson of the physician-poet Erasmus Darwin and of the potter Josiah Wedgwood—he drifted from medicine at Edinburgh to a Cambridge degree intended for the clergy before his real vocation found him.

In 1831 he sailed as naturalist aboard HMS Beagle on a five-year voyage around the world. The fossils of South America, the geology he read through the eyes of Charles Lyell, and above all the peculiar, island-by-island variations of the Galápagos finches and tortoises set him thinking about how species change. Reading Thomas Malthus on population in 1838 gave him the mechanism: a struggle for existence in which favourable variations are preserved—natural selection.

Cautious of the storm his theory would raise, he hoarded his evidence for two decades, until a letter from a younger naturalist proposing the very same idea forced his hand; their findings were announced jointly in 1858. The next year he published On the Origin of Species, and in 1871 The Descent of Man carried the argument to humankind itself.

Plagued by chronic illness, he worked quietly at Down House in Kent, also producing studies of barnacles, orchids, emotions, and earthworms. When he died in 1882 he was buried in Westminster Abbey. His vision of life would echo through later thought, as far afield as the psychology of Sigmund Freud. The man whose letter had spurred him to publish was Alfred Russel Wallace.
