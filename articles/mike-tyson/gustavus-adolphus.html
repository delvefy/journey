Gustavus Adolphus

King of Sweden, "the Lion of the North" (1594–1632)

Gustavus Adolphus, who became king of Sweden in 1611, is often called the father of modern warfare. Inheriting wars with Denmark, Russia, and Poland, he set about transforming his army into the most formidable of the age. He raised a disciplined national force, lightened and made mobile his artillery, and combined musketeers, pikemen, and cavalry in flexible formations whose coordinated firepower and manoeuvre broke the slow, massed tactics of his enemies.

In 1630 he landed in northern Germany to enter the Thirty Years' War on the Protestant side, determined to halt the advance of the Catholic Habsburgs that threatened to overwhelm the German princes. His intervention was underwritten, in one of history's great strokes of realpolitik, by Catholic France under Cardinal Richelieu, who was willing to fund a Protestant king to weaken the house of Habsburg.

At Breitenfeld in 1631 Gustavus won a crushing victory over the imperial-Catholic army, the first great Protestant triumph of the war, and swept across Germany as its champion. But at Lützen in 1632, charging into the fog at the head of his horsemen, he was cut off and killed. His army held the field, yet Sweden had lost its king at the height of his glory.

His innovations were studied by soldiers for generations—Napoleon ranked him among the handful of supreme commanders of history—and he made Sweden a great power whose later defender would be the doomed Charles XII. The realpolitik that had armed him, meanwhile, was the design of France's great minister, the cardinal who ruled in the name of his king.
