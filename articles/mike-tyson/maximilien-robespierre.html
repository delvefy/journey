Maximilien Robespierre

French revolutionary (1758–1794)

Maximilien Robespierre, a provincial lawyer from Arras, became the dominant and most terrible figure of the French Revolution at its height. A devoted disciple of Jean-Jacques Rousseau, he carried into politics the philosopher's dream of a republic founded on virtue, equality, and the general will of the people. Austere, incorruptible in his personal life, and utterly inflexible in his principles, he won the nickname "the Incorruptible" and rose as a leading voice of the Jacobins.

Having pressed hard for the execution of King Louis XVI, he came to preside over the Committee of Public Safety, the emergency government that ruled France during the Reign of Terror. In the name of saving the Republic from its enemies he sent thousands to the guillotine, striking down not only royalists and foreign agents but rival revolutionaries to his left and right.

He sought to remake the very soul of the nation, instituting a deist Cult of the Supreme Being and presiding over its great festival. But as the executions mounted and no one felt safe, fear turned the Convention against him. In the coup of Thermidor, in July 1794, Robespierre was denounced, seized, and—without the trial he had so often denied others—guillotined the next day. His death ended the Terror.

Idealist to some, tyrant to others, he remains the Revolution's most debated figure. The great rival whom he had sent to the scaffold only months before his own fall was the mighty orator Georges Danton.
