John Locke

English philosopher, "father of liberalism" (1632–1704)

John Locke, born in Somerset in 1632, was the most influential philosopher of the English Enlightenment, a founder of empiricism in theory of knowledge and of liberalism in politics. Trained at Oxford in medicine, he moved among the new experimental philosophers, working with Robert Boyle and befriending Isaac Newton, whose Principia he admired and championed. As physician and confidant to the Earl of Shaftesbury he was drawn into the dangerous politics of the day, and during a time of plots against the crown he took refuge in the Dutch Republic, returning only after the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

In his Essay Concerning Human Understanding he argued that the mind at birth is a blank sheet, and that all our ideas arise from experience—sensation and reflection—rather than from innate principles of the kind defended by René Descartes. The work made him the great theorist of how the mind comes to know the world, and it shaped every empiricist who followed.

His Two Treatises of Government grounded political authority not in divine right but in the consent of the governed. Men possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property, he held, and a government that betrays its trust may rightfully be resisted. These ideas became the seedbed of constitutional democracy, were carried into France by admirers such as Voltaire, refined by David Hume's empiricism, and most famously echoed by Thomas Jefferson in the American Declaration of Independence.

Locke also pleaded for religious toleration and wrote on education. His insistence on limited, accountable government found its great continental development in the constitutional theory of Montesquieu.
