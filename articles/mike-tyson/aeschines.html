Aeschines

Athenian orator (389–314 BC)

Aeschines was one of the great Attic orators and the lifelong adversary of Demosthenes. Of humble birth, he made his way as a clerk and as an actor—a training that gave his speaking a fine voice and presence—before rising into the turbulent politics of Athens in the age of Macedon's ascent.

Where Demosthenes urged defiance, Aeschines argued for peace and accommodation with the rising power of Philip II of Macedon. He served on the embassies that negotiated with the king, and Demosthenes bitterly accused him of having been bought with Macedonian gold, dragging him into court on a charge of corruption from which he barely escaped.

Their feud reached its climax when a citizen proposed that Demosthenes be honoured with a golden crown. Aeschines prosecuted, delivering the speech Against Ctesiphon; Demosthenes answered with his immortal oration On the Crown. The verdict went overwhelmingly to Demosthenes, and Aeschines, failing to win even a fifth of the jury, was fined and chose exile.

He withdrew to Rhodes, where tradition says he opened a school of rhetoric and read aloud the very speeches of their great contest to admiring pupils. Only three of his orations survive, all entwined with that rivalry, yet the ancients ranked him second only to Demosthenes himself, and his works were studied by Cicero. In an earlier generation the art he practised had been raised to a science by the teacher Isocrates.
