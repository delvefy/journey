Pythagoras

Greek philosopher and mathematician (c. 570–495 BC)

Pythagoras of Samos is one of the most famous and most shadowy figures of antiquity. He wrote nothing himself, and almost everything told of him comes down wrapped in legend, for his followers ascribed their own discoveries to the master and guarded his teaching in secret. By tradition he travelled in his youth to Egypt and the East before settling around 530 BC at Croton in southern Italy, where he founded a religious and philosophical brotherhood bound by strict rules of life.

To the Pythagoreans, number was the secret of all things. They held that the cosmos is ordered by mathematical ratios, and Pythagoras is credited with discovering that the concords of music correspond to simple whole-number proportions of a vibrating string—the seed of the idea of a "harmony of the spheres." Above all his name is attached, by long tradition, to the theorem relating the sides of a right-angled triangle, knowledge of which in fact reached back to earlier peoples.

Alongside this mathematics ran a powerful religious vision. Pythagoras taught that the soul is immortal and passes after death into other bodies, and he prescribed purification, self-discipline, and abstinence to free it. His brotherhood grew politically dominant in the cities of the Greek west until it was violently broken up.

However uncertain the man, the influence of his school was immense. It shaped the mathematics and the mystical strain of Greek thought, profoundly marking the philosophy of Plato and engaging the criticism of Aristotle. The geometry it pioneered would be given its lasting form by Euclid.
