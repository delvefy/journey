Sam Houston

American soldier and statesman, founder of Texas (1793–1863)

Sam Houston was the central figure in the birth of Texas and one of the most colourful statesmen of nineteenth-century America. Raised in Tennessee, as a youth he ran off to live for years among the Cherokee, who adopted him and called him the Raven—a bond he honoured all his life as an advocate for their people.

He fought under Andrew Jackson at the Battle of Horseshoe Bend, where his bravery and wounds won him the lifelong patronage of the future president. He rose to become a congressman and governor of Tennessee before personal ruin sent him west.

In Texas he took command of the rebel army in the revolution against Mexico. After the fall of the Alamo, he retreated, drilled his men, and then turned on his pursuers at the San Jacinto River in April 1836, routing them in eighteen minutes and capturing the Mexican leader to secure Texan independence. He served as president of the new republic, and after its annexation became one of the first United States senators from Texas and later its governor.

A staunch Unionist, he refused to swear allegiance to the Confederacy in 1861 and was removed from the governorship, warning in vain that the South could not win. He died in 1863, his name borne ever after by the great city of Houston. The fall of the Alamo that steeled his revolution had claimed the frontier legend Davy Crockett.
