Francis Xavier

Jesuit missionary, "Apostle of the Indies" (1506–1552)

Francis Xavier, born in 1506 in the kingdom of Navarre, was one of the greatest Christian missionaries in history and a co-founder of the Society of Jesus. As a student at the University of Paris he fell under the influence of his fellow Basque, Ignatius of Loyola, and was among the small band of companions who vowed themselves to God's service and formed the nucleus of the Jesuit order.

In 1541, at the request of the king of Portugal, Xavier set sail for the East as a papal envoy, and for the next decade he carried the Christian faith across Asia with tireless, almost superhuman energy. He laboured in Portuguese Goa, among the pearl-fishers of southern India, and in the Spice Islands of the Malay archipelago, baptising, it was said, many thousands.

His boldest venture came in 1549, when he became the first Christian missionary to Japan. There he spent more than two years, struggling with the language and the unfamiliar culture but planting a Christian community that would endure for generations. He came to believe that to convert Japan he must first reach China, the civilisation the Japanese revered.

That ambition undid him. In 1552 Xavier reached the small island of Shangchuan off the Chinese coast, but could find no way to enter the closed empire, and there he fell ill and died, gazing toward a China he never reached. His body was carried to Goa, where it remains an object of veneration. Canonised in 1622 and named patron of missions, he had opened the path that Matteo Ricci would one day complete.
