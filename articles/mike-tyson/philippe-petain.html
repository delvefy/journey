Philippe Pétain

Marshal of France and head of the Vichy regime (1856–1951)

Philippe Pétain was the most revered French soldier of the First World War who, in old age, became the head of the collaborationist regime that disgraced his name forever.

A career infantry officer who rose slowly through the peacetime army preaching the supremacy of firepower, he was unknown to the public until 1916, when he took command at the battle of Verdun. His steady defence and his care for the troops made him a national hero; after the great mutinies of 1917 he restored the army's morale, and in 1918 he was raised to Marshal of France. Through these years the French war effort was driven by the indomitable premier Georges Clemenceau, under whose government he held supreme command.

Honoured for two decades as the saviour of Verdun, Pétain was called to power in June 1940 as the armies of Adolf Hitler overran France. He sought an armistice, and from the spa town of Vichy he ruled as “Chief of the French State,” meeting Hitler at Montoire to embrace a policy of collaboration and presiding over an authoritarian order complicit in the deportation of Jews. His regime warred with its former ally when Winston Churchill ordered the destruction of the French fleet, even as Franklin D. Roosevelt kept up relations with Vichy until the Allied landings in North Africa.

Among the young officers he had once mentored was Charles de Gaulle, who broke with him utterly and led the resistance against everything Vichy stood for. At war's end Pétain was tried for treason and sentenced to death, a sentence de Gaulle commuted to life imprisonment; he died on the Île d'Yeu in 1951.
