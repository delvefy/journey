John Milton

English poet and polemicist (1608–1674)

John Milton was the greatest English poet after Shakespeare and the author of Paradise Lost, the supreme epic of the English language. Born in London and educated at Cambridge, he steeped himself in classical and biblical learning, producing in his youth the lyric companions L'Allegro and Il Penseroso, the masque Comus, and the elegy Lycidas. On a tour of Italy in 1638 he visited the aged Galileo, then a prisoner of the Inquisition—the one contemporary he would name in his great poem.

When civil war engulfed England, Milton turned from verse to fierce political prose. He defended the liberty of the press in Areopagitica, argued for the right of divorce, and, after the execution of King Charles I, wrote ringing justifications of the people's right to depose a tyrant. These pamphlets won him the post of Secretary for Foreign Tongues to the revolutionary Commonwealth, serving its Lord Protector, Oliver Cromwell.

The labor cost him his sight; by 1652 he was wholly blind. The Restoration of 1660 left him in danger and disgrace, the cause he had championed in ruins. Yet it was now, blind and defeated, that he dictated his masterpiece. Paradise Lost, telling of the fall of man and the rebellion of Satan, fused the grandeur of Homer and Virgil with Christian scripture into something wholly new. He followed it with Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes.

Milton died in 1674, his epic securing his immortality. The Puritan statesman and general he had served, and whose cause his pen defended above all others, was Oliver Cromwell.
