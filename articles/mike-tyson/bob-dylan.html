Bob Dylan

American songwriter and Nobel laureate (born 1941)

Bob Dylan, born Robert Zimmerman in Minnesota, is widely held to be the greatest songwriter of the modern age. Arriving in the Greenwich Village folk scene of New York in 1961, he idolized the ailing folk troubadour Woody Guthrie, whom he sought out and visited, and soon surpassed every model with songs of startling originality.

As the 1960s caught fire he became the reluctant voice of a generation. “Blowin' in the Wind” and “The Times They Are a-Changin'” became anthems of the civil-rights and antiwar movements. Then, at the Newport Folk Festival in 1965, he scandalized the folk purists by “going electric,” fusing folk and rock and unleashing the torrential “Like a Rolling Stone” and the surreal imagery of “Mr. Tambourine Man” and “Subterranean Homesick Blues.”

His achievement was to bring to popular song a literary depth and poetic ambition it had never known, changing what a song could be and influencing nearly every musician who followed. Over the decades he reinvented himself again and again—rock, country, gospel, and a touring life that never seemed to end. In 2016 the Swedish Academy awarded him the Nobel Prize in Literature.

In the mid-1960s Dylan and the rising stars of British rock circled each other in mutual fascination, each remaking the other's art: he drove their lyrics toward introspection, and they helped pull him toward the electric sound. The leader of the band he most influenced in that exchange was John Lennon.
