Lucian of Samosata

Greek satirist of the Roman Empire (c. 125–after 180)

Lucian of Samosata was the supreme satirist of the ancient world, a Greek-writing wit from the Syrian frontier whose mocking dialogues have delighted and instructed readers for nearly two thousand years.

Born at Samosata on the Euphrates, he trained as a public speaker and travelled the Roman Empire as a lecturer before turning to the comic prose that made his name. In a sparkling Attic Greek he composed dialogues that ridiculed pompous philosophers, credulous believers, and the follies of gods and men alike—the Dialogues of the Dead, Philosophies for Sale, and his exposés of religious frauds.

His most famous work, the True History, is a straight-faced parody of travellers' tall tales, recounting a voyage beyond the ocean and up to the Moon, with a war between the peoples of Sun and Moon. Because of it he is often called the earliest writer of science fiction.

Half-forgotten as antiquity faded, Lucian was joyfully rediscovered by the Renaissance. Erasmus and Thomas More translated him into Latin together; Rabelais filled his giants with Lucian's spirit; and long after, Voltaire turned his weapons to the same ends.

His fantastical lunar voyage echoes down to the great satire of a later age, the Gulliver's Travels of Jonathan Swift, in whom Lucian's deadpan invention found a worthy heir.
