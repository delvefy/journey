Dara Shikoh

Mughal crown prince, mystic and scholar (1615–1659)

Dara Shikoh was the eldest son of Shah Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal, and his father's chosen heir to the throne of the Mughal Empire. But he was a scholar and mystic far more than a soldier or statesman. A devotee of the Sufi path, he spent his life seeking the hidden unity behind the world's religions, convinced that Islam and Hinduism drank from the same spring of truth.

He set down this conviction in his book The Mingling of the Two Oceans, which argued the deep agreement of Sufi and Hindu mystical thought. More lasting still was his patronage of learning: he gathered Brahmin scholars and oversaw the translation of the ancient Upanishads from Sanskrit into Persian, calling them “the Greatest Secret.” Through a later Latin rendering, this work carried the wisdom of India into Europe and became the most treasured book of the philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer.

His tolerant vision had powerful enemies, above all his austere and orthodox younger brother. When Shah Jahan fell gravely ill in 1657, the four sons fell upon one another in a war for the throne. Though backed by his father and the imperial capital, Dara was no match in the field for his brother's generalship and was crushed at the battle of Samugarh in 1658.

A hunted fugitive, he was betrayed, captured, paraded in chains through the streets of Delhi, condemned as a heretic, and put to death in 1659. The brother who defeated, displaced, and destroyed him—and seized the empire—was Aurangzeb. His work, meanwhile, lived on in the West through the philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer.
