George Cayley

English engineer, "father of aeronautics" (1773–1857)

Sir George Cayley, a Yorkshire baronet, scientist, and member of Parliament, founded the science of flight a full century before the airplane took to the air. Turning a clear and original mind to a problem long left to dreamers, he was the first to set out the principles on which all flying machines would be built: he identified the forces of lift, drag, thrust, and weight, and grasped the decisive idea—abandoned by those still imitating flapping birds—that lift and propulsion should be separated, with a fixed wing to bear the craft and a separate means to drive it forward.

He put theory into practice. His little model glider of 1804 had the layout of a modern aeroplane, with wing and tail; his treatise On Aerial Navigation laid down the new science; and in 1853 he built a glider that carried a man—reportedly his reluctant coachman—across a valley, the first heavier-than-air flight to bear a human being.

A restless inventor besides, Cayley worked on the caterpillar track, tension wheels, self-righting lifeboats, and an early form of the seat belt. But it is as the founder of aeronautics that he endures, for it was upon his principles that Otto Lilienthal and, after him, the Wright brothers built. Wilbur Wright himself paid tribute to the Englishman who had carried the science of flight further than anyone before.

The other great pioneers of his own century lifted humanity off the ground not with wings but with gas and fire—the inventors of the balloon, the brothers Montgolfier, beginning with Joseph Montgolfier.
