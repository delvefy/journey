Crassus

Roman general and financier (c. 115–53 BC)

Marcus Licinius Crassus was reputed the richest man in Rome, and his wealth was as much a weapon as any legion. He built his fortune by ruthless means—buying up the confiscated estates of the proscribed, and the burning houses of the city through a private brigade that would put out the flames only once the owner had sold cheap. Rising to power under the dictator Sulla, he turned his gold into political influence on a scale no one could match.

His great military command came against the slave army of Spartacus. Given charge of the war when Spartacus had already shamed several Roman armies, Crassus drove the rebels to their final defeat in 71 BC and lined the Appian Way with six thousand crucified captives—only to see Pompey return from Spain and claim the credit, kindling a lasting rivalry between them.

To bind that rivalry, Crassus joined Pompey and Julius Caesar, whose early career he had bankrolled, in the First Triumvirate that ruled Rome. But wealth and power could not satisfy his hunger for the military glory his partners had won. Seeking it in the East, he led an army against the Parthian empire and was lured to catastrophe at Carrhae in 53 BC, where his legions were destroyed by Parthian horse-archers and he himself was slain—the Parthians, it was said, mocking his greed with molten gold.

His death shattered the balance of the triumvirate and set Rome on the road to civil war. His most famous adversary was the rebel gladiator Spartacus.
