Émile Zola

French novelist and leader of Naturalism (1840–1902)

Émile Zola was the foremost French novelist of his generation and the founder of the literary movement called Naturalism, which sought to study human beings with the cold eye of a scientist, shaped by heredity and environment. His monument is the Rougon-Macquart, a cycle of twenty novels following one family across the Second Empire—among them Germinal, his shattering epic of a coal miners' strike, the gutter tragedy of L'Assommoir, and the courtesan's tale of Nana. He had grown up in Aix as the closest friend of the painter Cézanne, and championed the new art of Manet and the Impressionists.

But Zola earned his greatest fame as a fighter for justice. When the Jewish army officer Alfred Dreyfus was framed for treason and the army closed ranks to hide its guilt, Zola thundered back with an open letter splashed across a Paris front page under the headline “J'accuse...!” He accused generals and ministers by name of conspiracy and antisemitism, and was convicted of libel and forced to flee to England—but his courage turned the tide and helped at last to clear Dreyfus.

He died in 1902, asphyxiated by a blocked chimney—perhaps an accident, perhaps murder by his enemies—and was later enshrined among the nation's heroes in the Panthéon.

The towering predecessor whose vast, interlocking chronicle of French society had been the model for his own great cycle was Honoré de Balzac.
