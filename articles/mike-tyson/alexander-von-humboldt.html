Alexander von Humboldt

Prussian naturalist and explorer (1769–1859)

Alexander von Humboldt was the most celebrated scientist of his age and a founder of the modern view of nature as a single, interconnected whole. Born into the Prussian aristocracy in Berlin, he poured his fortune into a vast scientific expedition through Spanish America from 1799 to 1804 with the botanist Aimé Bonpland.

Over five years they navigated the Orinoco and confirmed its natural link to the Amazon basin, climbed high on the Andean volcano Chimborazo to a record altitude, and gathered measurements of plants, rocks, climate, and magnetism across a continent. From these labours Humboldt drew pioneering ideas: that vegetation is arranged in zones by altitude and latitude, that climate can be mapped by lines of equal temperature, and that living things and their environment form a great web. His name still marks the cold current along South America's Pacific coast.

His travel narratives and his grand late synthesis, Kosmos, made him world-famous and fired the imaginations of younger naturalists; Charles Darwin carried his account aboard the Beagle and called him the greatest scientific traveller who ever lived. A friend of Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, and a visitor to Thomas Jefferson in the young United States, he moved among the leading minds of two continents.

He died in Berlin in 1859, mourned across the world. Among those his writings on the New World had stirred was the young Venezuelan he met in Paris, the future liberator of South America, Simón Bolívar.
