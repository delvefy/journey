Richard Strauss

German composer and conductor (1864–1949)

Richard Strauss was the last great master of German Romanticism and one of its supreme orchestral colourists. Born in Munich, the son of a celebrated horn player, he was reared on the classics but soon embraced the progressive path of Richard Wagner and Franz Liszt, taking up and expanding Liszt's idea of the symphonic poem.

In a dazzling series of tone poems—Don Juan, Death and Transfiguration, the impish Till Eulenspiegel, Don Quixote, and the autobiographical Ein Heldenleben—he made the orchestra tell stories with unmatched vividness. The most famous of them, Also sprach Zarathustra, drew its title and idea from Friedrich Nietzsche and opened with a sunrise fanfare that later generations would know from the cinema.

He then conquered the opera house, scandalising and thrilling audiences with the lurid Salome and Elektra before turning, in the gilded comedy Der Rosenkavalier, to a tender homage to the world of Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. With the librettist Hugo von Hofmannsthal he forged one of opera's great partnerships.

His conduct during the Nazi years remains debated: he briefly held an official musical post before falling from favour while shielding his Jewish daughter-in-law and grandchildren. In a serene old age he wrote the radiant Four Last Songs. He died at Garmisch in 1949. His closest peer among the post-Wagnerian composer-conductors, friend and sometime champion of his operas, was Gustav Mahler.
