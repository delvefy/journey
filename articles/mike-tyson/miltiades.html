Miltiades

Athenian general, victor of Marathon (c. 550–489 BC)

Miltiades, of the great Athenian house of the Philaids, is forever bound to one of the most celebrated battles in history. In his earlier life he had ruled a small domain in the Thracian Chersonese as a vassal of Persia, even following Darius the Great on campaign, before the rising tide of Persian power drove him home to Athens, where his arrogant past made him as many enemies as friends.

His hour came in 490 BC. When Darius sent a fleet and army across the Aegean to punish Athens, Miltiades, one of the city's ten elected generals, argued passionately for marching out to meet the invaders on the plain of Marathon rather than awaiting a siege. Given his chance, he drew up the Athenian line with a deliberately thinned centre and powerful wings, let the Persian center push forward, then closed the wings inward to envelop and rout the enemy. The victory at Marathon saved Athens and became the proudest memory of the Greeks.

Glory proved fleeting. The next year a botched expedition against the island of Paros, in which he was badly wounded in the leg, turned the fickle city against him; he was prosecuted, fined an enormous sum, and died soon after of his festering wound, his triumph curdled into disgrace.

His deeds were recorded for all time by Herodotus, and the war-leadership of Athens passed in time to Themistocles. His own legacy was carried on by his son, the soldier-statesman Cimon.
