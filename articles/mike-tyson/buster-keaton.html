Buster Keaton

American silent-film comedian and director (1895–1966)

Buster Keaton was the “Great Stone Face,” the silent-film comedian and director whose deadpan daring and astonishing physical invention rank him with the greatest of all filmmakers.

A vaudeville child star, he learned the movies from the comedian Roscoe “Fatty” Arbuckle and went on to write, direct and star in a string of silent masterpieces—Sherlock Jr., The General, Steamboat Bill, Jr.—in which his unsmiling everyman survived collapsing buildings, runaway trains and cyclones. He performed his own perilous stunts, including the famous gag of a house facade falling around him as he stood, untouched, in the gap of an open window.

The coming of sound and a ruinous studio contract cost him his independence, and his career sank into years of obscurity and drink. But he was rediscovered late in life, sharing a poignant scene with his old peer Charlie Chaplin in Limelight and starring, near the end, in the experimental short Film written by Samuel Beckett. His mastery of the body in motion would inspire generations of physical comedians, not least Jackie Chan.

The third member of the great triumvirate of silent comedy—the bespectacled go-getter forever remembered dangling from the hands of a clock high above the street—was Harold Lloyd.
