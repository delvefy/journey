Vladimir Lenin

Russian revolutionary, founder of the Soviet state (1870–1924)

Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, known to history as Lenin, was born in 1870 in Simbirsk on the Volga and became the architect of the world's first communist state. His path to revolution was sealed in part by the execution of his elder brother for plotting against Tsar Alexander III. Drawn to the writings of Karl Marx, Lenin became a militant Marxist, was exiled to Siberia, and spent long years organizing abroad.

He led the Bolshevik faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party and developed a distinctive doctrine—later called Leninism—centered on a disciplined vanguard party that would seize power on behalf of the working class. When the February Revolution of 1917 toppled the tsar, Lenin returned to Russia in a sealed train provided by Germany and agitated against the Provisional Government.

In October 1917 his Bolsheviks overthrew that government, and Lenin became head of the new Soviet state. He pulled Russia out of the First World War through the punishing Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, then led the Reds to victory in a brutal civil war, employing the Red Terror and the secret police against opponents. After the ruinous policy of War Communism, he introduced the more pragmatic New Economic Policy and founded the Communist International to spread revolution abroad.

A series of strokes incapacitated Lenin from 1922, and he died in 1924, his embalmed body placed on permanent display in Moscow. He left behind a one-party state and a revolutionary creed that would shape much of the twentieth century, even as his chosen successors fought over his legacy.
