Zeuxis

Greek painter (late 5th century BC)

Zeuxis of Heraclea was among the most celebrated painters of classical Greece, renowned for an illusionism so convincing that it became the stuff of legend. Building on the new techniques of light and shadow, he modelled his figures with such depth and roundness that they seemed to stand free of the panel, and his fame and wealth grew so great that he was said to give his pictures away, declaring that no price could buy them.

The most famous story of ancient art concerns his contest with his rival Parrhasius. Zeuxis painted a cluster of grapes so true to life that birds flew down to peck at them, and he turned in triumph to his rival, asking him to draw back the curtain that veiled his own picture—only to discover that the curtain itself was painted. Zeuxis conceded with grace: he had deceived the birds, he said, but Parrhasius had deceived Zeuxis.

Asked to paint Helen of Troy for a Greek city, Zeuxis is said to have chosen the five most beautiful maidens of the place and combined the finest feature of each into a single ideal form—an early statement of the idea that perfect beauty is gathered from nature rather than found whole within it.

Not one of his paintings survives, and we know him only through such tales. His name is forever joined to that of his great rival, Parrhasius.
