Al-Khwarizmi

Persian mathematician of the House of Wisdom (c. 780–850)

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, the great academy of translation and research that the Abbasid caliphs maintained at the height of the Islamic golden age. There, amid scholars rendering the wisdom of Greece, Persia, and India into Arabic, he produced books so influential that the very vocabulary of mathematics still echoes his name.

His treatise on solving equations by “completion and balancing” gave the world the word algebra, drawn from its Arabic title. In it he set out, in plain and general rules, how to handle linear and quadratic equations—a systematic science of the unknown that earlier mathematicians had only approached piecemeal. From the Latin form of his own name, in turn, came the word algorithm.

He did as much for arithmetic. In a work on calculation he expounded the Hindu decimal system of nine digits and a zero, and when it was later translated in Europe it carried that system westward, slowly displacing the clumsy numerals of Rome. He also compiled astronomical tables and wrote a geography of the known world, correcting and extending the great Ptolemy.

Through these books al-Khwarizmi became a teacher of both civilizations, the Islamic and, centuries later, the Christian. The Italian merchant's son who carried his arithmetic and his numerals into medieval Europe, and whose own name now graces a famous sequence, was Fibonacci.
