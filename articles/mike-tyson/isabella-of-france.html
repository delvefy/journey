Isabella of France

Queen of England, “the She-Wolf” (c. 1295–1358)

Isabella of France was a daughter of one king, the wife of another, and the maker of a third. Child of the formidable Philip IV of France, she was married while still a girl to Edward II of England, a union meant to bind the two crowns but soured almost from the start by the king's devotion to his favorites, who pushed the young queen aside.

For years she endured neglect and humiliation. Sent to France in 1325 on a diplomatic errand, she refused to come home. There she found a partner in the exiled English baron Roger Mortimer, and the two became lovers and conspirators. In 1326 they sailed for England with a small army; Edward's hated government dissolved before them, and the king himself was hunted down.

In 1327 Edward II was deposed and the couple's young charge crowned as Edward III, with Isabella and Mortimer ruling as regents. Their power was real but brief and resented—a forced peace with Scotland, an execution of a royal uncle, the lingering suspicion of the old king's murder. In 1330 the teenaged Edward III rose against them, seized Mortimer, and sent him to the gallows, while sparing his mother an honorable retirement.

Her deepest mark on history was made by blood. When her brothers, the last Capetian kings, died one after another without sons, the throne of France passed to a cousin—but Isabella's claim, carried by her son, became the spark of the Hundred Years' War. The royal father from whom that claim descended was Philip IV of France.
