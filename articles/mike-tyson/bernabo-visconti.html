Bernabò Visconti

Lord of Milan (1323–1385)

Bernabò Visconti was the most feared and the most notorious of the Milanese lords, a byword in his own time for tyranny. On the division of the Visconti inheritance he took the eastern half of the dominions and ruled from Milan, while his brother Galeazzo II held the west from Pavia. For thirty years his will was law over a great swath of Lombardy.

His rule was famous for its cruelty and its caprice. He devised elaborate punishments, taxed his subjects mercilessly, and indulged a mania for hunting so extreme that, by tradition, he billeted thousands of boarhounds upon the citizens, who answered with their skins for any animal that sickened. His quarrels with the Avignon papacy were ceaseless; excommunicated again and again, he is said to have forced the legates who brought one such decree to eat the document, seal, cord, and all.

Yet he was no mere brute. He married Beatrice della Scala of Verona, kept a vigorous court, and married his many children into the royal houses of Europe, weaving the Visconti into the bloodlines of Bavaria, Austria, and France. The poet Petrarch had served the Visconti court and lent it lustre in the previous decades.

His end came from within his own family. In 1385 his nephew Gian Galeazzo, feigning friendship, seized him and shut him in the castle of Trezzo, where he died, poison suspected. So vivid was his reputation that Geoffrey Chaucer set his fall among the tragedies of the Monk's Tale. The brother with whom he had divided Milan, and who founded the University of Pavia, was Galeazzo II Visconti.
