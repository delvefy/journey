John von Neumann

Hungarian-American mathematician (1903–1957)

John von Neumann was born in Budapest in 1903 and revealed a prodigious mind in childhood, reportedly able to divide eight-digit numbers in his head and to recall whole books. He studied under David Hilbert at Göttingen and, as the Nazis rose, emigrated to America, becoming one of the first faculty of the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, where Albert Einstein was a colleague.

His range was almost without parallel. He gave quantum mechanics a rigorous mathematical foundation, made deep advances in set theory and operator algebras, and, with Oskar Morgenstern, created the theory of games that reshaped economics and strategy. In computing he set down the architecture—a single store holding both data and program—that nearly every machine still follows, building on the universal machine of Alan Turing.

During the war he became a central figure of the Manhattan Project, his calculations solving the problem of the implosion lens that triggered the plutonium bomb. He went on to champion the hydrogen bomb and to shape the cold-war doctrine of nuclear deterrence, while also inventing the study of self-reproducing automata.

Tireless, worldly, and famously quick, von Neumann advised governments and militaries to the end. He died of cancer in 1957, at only fifty-three. Among his closest wartime associates was the physicist who had directed the bomb's laboratory, J. Robert Oppenheimer.
