Francisco Franco

Spanish general and dictator, the Caudillo (1892–1975)

Francisco Franco ruled Spain with an iron hand for nearly four decades. A cautious, ambitious career officer, he joined the military uprising of 1936 against Spain's elected Republican government, igniting the savage Spanish Civil War. He emerged as leader of the Nationalist cause—conservative, Catholic, and anti-communist—and won the war by 1939 with decisive aid from his fellow dictators Hitler and Mussolini, whose aircraft and troops bombed and battered the Republic into defeat.

Victory brought no mercy. Franco's regime executed and imprisoned its enemies in vast numbers and clamped a long, grey repression over the country. Shrewdly, he kept Spain out of the Second World War despite his debt to the Axis, and so outlasted the patrons who had made him, ruling on through the entire Cold War as the “Caudillo.”

In his final years he eased some controls and oversaw an economic revival, and he arranged for the monarchy to be restored after him under a prince he had groomed—who, to Franco's posthumous dismay, would guide Spain peacefully into democracy. Franco died in his bed in 1975.

The destruction of the Basque town of Guernica by his German and Italian allies in 1937 inspired the most famous protest painting of the century, the great mural by Pablo Picasso.
