Niccolò Paganini

Italian violin virtuoso and composer (1782–1840)

Niccolò Paganini was the most dazzling violinist of his age and the model of the Romantic virtuoso. Born in Genoa, he developed a technique so far beyond his contemporaries—rapid left-hand pizzicato, ringing harmonics, daring multiple stops, whole pieces played on a single string—that audiences across Europe were left astonished and a little frightened. His gaunt figure and uncanny facility fed a persistent legend that he had struck a bargain with the devil.

From the 1820s his tours through Italy, Vienna, Paris, and London made him a sensation and a wealthy man, though gambling repeatedly drained his fortune. His compositions were vehicles for that technique, above all the twenty-four Caprices for solo violin and the violin concertos, whose sparkling Campanella rondo became a favourite show-piece.

His influence on Romantic music ran far beyond the violin. The young Frédéric Chopin, hearing him, set down a Souvenir de Paganini, and Franz Liszt, electrified by his playing, resolved to become the Paganini of the piano and wrote a set of fiendish studies after him. Paganini also recognised and aided the struggling Hector Berlioz with a celebrated gift of money.

Dogged in death by the diabolical rumours, his burial was delayed for years by a doubtful Church. The leaping theme of his last Caprice proved irresistible to later composers, none more memorably than Johannes Brahms, who built a great set of variations upon it.
