Ptolemy I Soter

Macedonian general of Alexander and founder of Ptolemaic Egypt (c. 367–282 BC)

Ptolemy, son of Lagus, was a Macedonian nobleman, a boyhood companion of Alexander the Great and one of his most trusted generals and bodyguards. He marched with Alexander across Asia and later wrote a sober, soldierly history of those campaigns that became a principal source for the historian Arrian. When Alexander died at Babylon in 323 BC, Ptolemy secured for himself the richest prize of the empire—Egypt.

Governing first as satrap and then, from 305 BC, as king, he founded the dynasty that would rule Egypt for nearly three centuries until its last queen, Cleopatra. He diverted Alexander's funeral cortege to Egypt and enshrined the body at Alexandria, binding his new kingdom to the prestige of the conqueror. In the long wars among Alexander's successors he fought beside Seleucus at the great victory of Gaza in 312 BC and defended Egypt against every rival.

The surname Soter, “Saviour,” was awarded him by the Rhodians after he helped relieve their city from siege. But his most enduring work was cultural: he made Alexandria a capital of learning, founding the Museum and the Library that drew scholars from across the Greek world. There the mathematician Euclid taught, and Ptolemy is said to have asked him for an easier path to geometry, only to be told there was “no royal road.”

Late in life he passed the crown to his son Ptolemy II and died in 282 BC, the only one of Alexander's marshals to die peacefully in his own bed as king. His comrade at Gaza, who built the vast Seleucid empire across Asia, was Seleucus I Nicator.
