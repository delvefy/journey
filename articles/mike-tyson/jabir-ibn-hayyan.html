Jabir ibn Hayyan

Alchemist, "father of chemistry" (c. 721–c. 815)

Jabir ibn Hayyan, known in the Latin West as Geber, is the towering and shadowy figure at the origins of alchemy, often called the "father of chemistry." Traditionally born around 721 and active into the early ninth century, he is credited with an enormous body of works—the Jabirian corpus—though scholars believe much of it was composed by later writers under his name, and the details of his life are deeply uncertain.

By tradition Jabir was a disciple of the revered Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, who is said to have instructed him in the secrets of nature, and he is associated with the Abbasid court, working under the patronage of the powerful Barmakid viziers of the caliph Harun al-Rashid. Whatever the truth of his biography, the writings attached to his name had immense influence.

The Jabirian works describe practical laboratory operations with real sophistication: distillation, sublimation, crystallisation and calcination, and the preparation and classification of acids, salts and other substances. They set out a theory of matter built on the qualities of metals, and the goal—shared by all alchemists—of transmuting base metals into gold and finding the elixir of life. Mystical numerology and secrecy run through them as well.

When the Jabirian texts were translated into Latin in the Middle Ages, "Geber" became one of the most authoritative names in European alchemy, shaping the discipline for centuries before it gave way to modern chemistry. Half-historical, half-legendary, Jabir ibn Hayyan stands as the symbolic founder of the long quest to understand and transform the substances of the world.
