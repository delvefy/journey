Lady Jane Grey

Queen of England for nine days (1537–1554)

Lady Jane Grey was born in 1537, a great-granddaughter of Henry VII and great-niece of Henry VIII, and so close enough to the throne to be fatally useful to ambitious men. Brilliantly educated and a fervent Protestant, she won the admiration of scholars for her command of the ancient languages and her grave, bookish piety.

As the young king Edward VI lay dying in 1553, the all-powerful Duke of Northumberland married Jane to his own son, Guildford Dudley, and induced the king to name her his heir, setting aside Edward's Catholic half-sister Mary. When Edward died, Jane—reluctant and weeping—was proclaimed queen.

Her reign lasted barely nine days. Mary rallied the country to her rightful claim, the council deserted Jane, and the coup collapsed. The girl who had never wanted the crown was deposed and imprisoned in the Tower of London, while Mary I took her place.

At first the new queen was inclined to spare her young cousin. But when Jane's father joined Wyatt's rebellion early in 1554, she became too dangerous to leave alive. Jane and her husband were executed in February 1554; she was about sixteen. Composed and steadfast to the end, she passed swiftly into legend as a Protestant martyr and an emblem of innocence destroyed by the ambitions of others—not least those of the Dudley family into which she had married.
