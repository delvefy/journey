Nikita Khrushchev

First Secretary of the Soviet Communist Party (1894–1971)

Nikita Khrushchev led the Soviet Union for roughly a decade after Stalin's death, the leader who denounced his predecessor's terror and then brought the world to the edge of nuclear war over Cuba.

Born to a poor family in the village of Kalinovka, he worked as a metal fitter before joining the Bolsheviks and climbing the party ranks under the patronage of Joseph Stalin, carrying out his patron's harsh policies in Ukraine and Moscow. When Stalin died in 1953 he outmanoeuvred his rivals to take command, and in 1956 he astonished the Communist world with his “Secret Speech,” a wholesale condemnation of Stalin's cult of personality and crimes. He framed this as a return to the founding principles of Vladimir Lenin, and the cultural relaxation that followed was called the Thaw—though he crushed the Hungarian uprising of that same year without mercy.

His rule saw the launch of Sputnik and the first human spaceflight, triumphs of prestige in the contest with the West. But his bluster strained the alliance with Mao Zedong into the open Sino-Soviet split, and his gamble of placing missiles on the island of Fidel Castro provoked the 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis, a thirteen-day confrontation with John F. Kennedy that ended in Soviet retreat. Earlier his hopes of détente had collapsed when an American spy plane was shot down, wrecking his planned summit with President Dwight D. Eisenhower. Ousted in 1964 and pensioned off, he saw the de-Stalinization he had begun carried much further, decades later, by Mikhail Gorbachev.
