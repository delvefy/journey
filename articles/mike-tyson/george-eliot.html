George Eliot

English novelist (1819–1880)

George Eliot—the pen name of Mary Ann Evans—was the supreme intellectual among English novelists, whose psychological depth and moral seriousness reached their height in Middlemarch.

Born in rural Warwickshire and intensely religious as a girl, she lost her faith under the influence of freethinking friends and turned to the most demanding intellectual labour. She translated radical German theology and the philosophy of Baruch Spinoza, and as assistant editor of a leading review she moved at the centre of London's advanced thinkers. There she formed a lifelong, unmarried partnership with the critic George Henry Lewes—a scandalous union, since he could not divorce—and it was with him, while he wrote his pioneering life of Goethe, that she steeped herself in the German master she revered above all writers.

It was Lewes who urged her to attempt fiction, and she took a man's name to escape the condescension shown to “lady novelists.” The novels poured out: Adam Bede, The Mill on the Floss, Silas Marner, the Florentine historical romance Romola built around the figure of Savonarola, and finally Middlemarch, that vast and humane anatomy of provincial life that many call the greatest novel in English.

By her death in 1880 she was among the most honoured writers of the age. Years earlier, reading her first stories, the other towering novelist of Victorian England had written to praise their unknown author—and rightly guessed, against the masculine signature, that the writer was a woman. That admirer was Charles Dickens.
