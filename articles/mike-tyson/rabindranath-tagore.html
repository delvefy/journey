Rabindranath Tagore

Bengali poet and polymath, Nobel laureate (1861–1941)

Rabindranath Tagore, born in Calcutta in 1861 into a distinguished and cultured Bengali family, was a poet, novelist, composer, painter and educator of extraordinary range—the towering figure of modern Indian literature and the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

A prodigy who published verse as a boy, Tagore reshaped Bengali letters, writing poems, songs, plays, stories and novels of great lyrical beauty and humane feeling. His reputation became worldwide in 1912, when his own English rendering of Gitanjali ("Song Offerings"), a collection of mystical devotional poems, was published in London with an admiring introduction by W. B. Yeats. The following year it won him the Nobel Prize, a sensation that made him a literary celebrity across the globe.

Tagore was far more than a poet. He composed thousands of songs—the genre known as Rabindra Sangeet—and his words became the national anthems of two nations, India's "Jana Gana Mana" and Bangladesh's "Amar Shonar Bangla." He founded an experimental school and university at Santiniketan, dedicated to a humane, international ideal of education.

A thinker engaged with the great questions of his age, he debated the nature of truth and reality with Albert Einstein in a famous exchange, and maintained a deep if sometimes contentious friendship with Mahatma Gandhi, whom he helped to honour as "Mahatma" while Gandhi called him "Gurudev." He was admired by leaders of the freedom movement such as Jawaharlal Nehru. Knighted by the British, he renounced the honour in 1919 in protest at the Amritsar Massacre. Tagore died in 1941, mourned across India as its national poet; his stories would later inspire the films of Satyajit Ray.
