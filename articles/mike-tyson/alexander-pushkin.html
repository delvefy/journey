Alexander Pushkin

Russian poet, founder of modern Russian literature (1799–1837)

Alexander Sergeyevich Pushkin is universally regarded as the founder of modern Russian literature and the figure who fixed its literary language. Born in Moscow into an old noble family, he was proud of his maternal great-grandfather Abram Gannibal, an African brought to Russia and ennobled under Peter the Great. Educated at the new Imperial Lyceum at Tsarskoye Selo, he won early celebrity with the playful romance Ruslan and Lyudmila.

His liberal sympathies and stinging epigrams brought exile to the south and then to his family estate at Mikhailovskoye. He was close to many who joined the Decembrist revolt of 1825, though he took no part in it; afterward Tsar Nicholas I made himself the poet's personal censor. From these years came an astonishing range of work.

His masterpiece was the novel-in-verse Eugene Onegin, modelled in part on Byron; he also wrote the historical drama Boris Godunov, shaped by his reading of Shakespeare, the narrative poem The Bronze Horseman, and lucid prose including The Captain's Daughter and The Queen of Spades. His wit and clarity owed much to the French Enlightenment he admired, above all Voltaire.

In 1831 he married the celebrated beauty Natalia Goncharova. Provoked by gossip and an insolent French officer, Georges d'Anthès, he fought a duel in January 1837 and died two days later of his wound, at thirty-seven. Revered by Tolstoy and eulogised by Dostoevsky in a famous speech, he had earlier handed promising plots to a young writer he encouraged, Nikolai Gogol.
