Isabella I of Castile

Queen of Castile, one of the Catholic Monarchs (1451–1504)

Isabella I of Castile was one of the most consequential rulers in Spanish history, the queen who unified Spain, completed the Reconquista, and set in motion the discovery of the New World. Seizing a contested throne, she secured her position through a marriage of momentous importance: her union with Ferdinand of Aragon joined the two great crowns of the peninsula and created, in all but name, the kingdom of Spain. Together they were styled the "Catholic Monarchs."

Pious, strong-willed, and tireless, Isabella restored royal authority over a fractious nobility and pressed the long war against the last Muslim state in Spain. In 1492 her armies took Granada, ending nearly eight centuries of Moorish rule in Iberia. That same triumphant year she agreed, after long hesitation, to fund the western voyage of a Genoese mariner—Christopher Columbus—whose landfall across the Atlantic opened the age of Spanish empire and transformed the world.

Her devotion to religious unity had a darker face. She and Ferdinand established the Spanish Inquisition to enforce orthodoxy, and in 1492 they expelled the Jews from their realms, an act of lasting cruelty. Through her children's marriages she wove Spain into the dynasties of Europe: one daughter, Catherine of Aragon, married Henry VIII of England, and through another, Joanna, the crown would pass to her grandson, the Emperor Charles V.

Isabella died in 1504, leaving a unified and ascendant Spain poised to dominate the coming century. The partner who shared her throne, her policies, and her every great enterprise was her husband and co-ruler, Ferdinand II of Aragon.
