Khosrow I

Sasanian king of Persia, "the Just" (r. 531–579)

Khosrow I, surnamed Anushirvan, "of the immortal soul," and remembered ever after as "the Just," ruled the Sasanian Empire of Persia from 531 to 579 and is reckoned its greatest king. The favoured son of Kavad I, he came to the throne amid the suppression of the egalitarian Mazdakite movement, whose leaders he crushed to secure the order of society and the nobility on whom the crown depended.

As king he carried through reforms that became the model of Persian statecraft: a fresh land survey and a fixed, predictable tax system that gave the treasury stable revenue, an army paid directly by the crown rather than by the great lords, and a division of supreme military command into four regional generalships. His reputation for even-handed judgement gave him the title by which the East still remembers him.

His long reign was filled with war against the Byzantine emperor Justinian I. After concluding the "Eternal Peace" of 532, he reopened the conflict in 540, swept into Syria and sacked the great city of Antioch, carrying its people off to a new town near his capital. Decades of campaigning followed in Mesopotamia and the Caucasus, the wars chronicled by Procopius and fought on the Roman side by the general Belisarius.

Khosrow was as renowned for learning as for arms. When Justinian closed the philosophical schools of Athens, he welcomed their Neoplatonist scholars to his court; he sponsored the translation of Indian and Greek books into Persian, the arrival of the game of chess from India, and the famed academy of Gondishapur.

He left Persia at its height. To later memory he became the very archetype of the just ruler, celebrated in Firdawsi's Shahnameh, and Islamic tradition held that the Prophet Muhammad was proud to have been born in the reign of "the just king."
