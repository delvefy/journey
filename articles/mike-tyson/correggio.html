Correggio

Italian High Renaissance painter (c. 1489–1534)

Correggio—Antonio Allegri, named for his native town near Parma—was the High Renaissance master of soft light and dizzying illusion whose tender, sensuous art looked decades into the future.

Working far from Rome and Florence in the cities of Emilia, he forged a style of melting sweetness, absorbing the smoky, shadowed modelling of Leonardo da Vinci while matching, by some instinct of his own, the grace of Raphael and the heroic grandeur of Michelangelo. He was, with the Venetian Titian, among the supreme colourists and sensualists of the age, and Vasari marvelled that a provincial painter could rival the giants of the capital.

His boldest invention was the painted dome. In the cathedral of Parma he covered the cupola with an Assumption of the Virgin, a swirling spiral of figures soaring up into a luminous heaven, seen as if from directly below—a vertiginous illusion that scandalized some local clergy but prophesied the ceilings of the Baroque a century early. For the Duke of Mantua he painted the “Loves of Jupiter,” mythologies of unprecedented erotic tenderness, and in his nocturnal Nativity he made the holy light radiate from the Christ child himself.

He died, still in his prime, in 1534, back in the town that gave him his name. His luminous illusionism would be studied above all by the reformers of Bologna, among them Annibale Carracci, who carried it into a new age. The master of the Mantuan court whose hard, sculptural foreshortening had shaped Correggio's own youth was Andrea Mantegna.
