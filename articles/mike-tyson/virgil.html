Virgil

Roman poet (70–19 BC)

Publius Vergilius Maro, known as Virgil, was the greatest of the Roman poets and the author of the national epic of Rome. Born in 70 BC near Mantua in northern Italy, he came of age amid the civil wars that ended the Republic. His gifts won him the support of the wealthy patron Maecenas and the favor of the emperor Augustus, placing him at the heart of the literary flowering of the Augustan age.

His earliest works, the pastoral Eclogues and the Georgics—a richly crafted poem on farming and the rhythms of country life—already marked him as a master. But he poured the last decade of his life into his masterpiece, the Aeneid. In twelve books it follows the Trojan hero Aeneas as he escapes the ruin of Troy, endures wanderings and a tragic love in Carthage, and at last reaches Italy to plant the seed of the Roman people.

Consciously modeled on the epics of Homer, the Aeneid wove together myth, patriotism, and praise of Augustus's lineage into a profound meditation on duty, loss, and the costs of empire. Virgil died in 19 BC at Brundisium, returning from Greece, and asked on his deathbed that the still-unfinished poem be burned; Augustus countermanded the wish, and the Aeneid was preserved.

Through the Middle Ages Virgil was revered almost as a prophet, and the poet Dante would later choose him as his guide through the realms of the afterlife. His verse stands at the center of the Western literary tradition.
