Henry Clay

American statesman, the Great Compromiser (1777–1852)

Henry Clay was the most influential American legislator never to reach the presidency, a Kentucky orator whose gift for brokering bargains repeatedly held the fracturing Union together. Born in Virginia and risen as a frontier lawyer, he became a commanding Speaker of the House, leading the “War Hawks” who pushed the country into the War of 1812 and then helping to negotiate the peace at Ghent.

He gave his name to no single statute but to a whole program, the American System of protective tariffs, a national bank, and federally funded roads and canals meant to bind the nation economically. Above all he was the architect of compromise: the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the settlement that ended the Nullification Crisis, and the Compromise of 1850 each postponed civil war over slavery.

Founder and leader of the Whig Party, he served as John Quincy Adams's Secretary of State—an appointment his enemies branded a corrupt bargain—and made the Senate his great stage. He ran for president and lost more than once, declaring that he would rather be right than be president, and his fiercest rival was Andrew Jackson.

His example shaped the next generation; a young Whig named Abraham Lincoln called him the beau ideal of a statesman. He died in Washington in 1852. The colleague with whom he most shared the antebellum Senate's stage was the great orator Daniel Webster.
