Claude Debussy

French composer (1862–1918)

Claude Debussy was the central figure of musical Impressionism and one of the most influential composers of the modern age, the man who broke the spell of German Romanticism over European music and opened a wholly new world of sound.

Trained at the Paris Conservatoire, he came of age under the overwhelming influence of Richard Wagner, and then spent his career defining himself against it. In place of grand drama he offered colour, suggestion, and atmosphere—fluid harmonies, shimmering orchestration, scales borrowed from beyond the Western tradition, including the Javanese gamelan he heard at a Paris exposition.

His music drew on the Symbolist poets and the painters of his day: the dreamlike Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun, the sea-portrait La Mer, the opera Pelléas et Mélisande, and the beloved Clair de Lune. As a young man he had even spent summers as household pianist to the wealthy patroness of Tchaikovsky.

His revolution in harmony and colour cleared the way for the modern music of Stravinsky and those who came after. In France his name is forever paired with that of his great contemporary, Maurice Ravel.
