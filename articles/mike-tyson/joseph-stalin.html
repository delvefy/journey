Joseph Stalin

Leader of the Soviet Union (1878–1953)

Joseph Stalin, born Ioseb Jughashvili in 1878 in Gori, Georgia, ruled the Soviet Union for nearly three decades and turned it into an industrial and military superpower at the cost of millions of lives. A seminary student who became a Marxist revolutionary, he robbed banks to fund the Bolsheviks and rose through the party under Vladimir Lenin, gaining the seemingly modest post of General Secretary in 1922.

From that office he built a base of patronage, and after Lenin's death he outmaneuvered Leon Trotsky and other rivals to seize sole power. Promoting "Socialism in One Country," he launched the forced collectivization of agriculture, which triggered catastrophic famines—including the Holodomor in Ukraine—and drove rapid industrialization through the Five-Year Plans. In the late 1930s his Great Terror swept the party, army, and society, sending millions to execution or the Gulag labor camps.

Stalin signed a nonaggression pact with Nazi Germany in 1939, only to be invaded in 1941. The Soviet Union bore the heaviest burden of the Second World War, losing some twenty-seven million people before turning the tide at Stalingrad and driving to Berlin. As one of the Big Three with Churchill and Roosevelt, Stalin shaped the postwar settlement, extending Soviet control over Eastern Europe and helping ignite the Cold War.

Surrounded by a vast cult of personality, he ruled through fear until his death in 1953. Historians count him among the deadliest rulers in history, responsible for tens of millions of deaths through famine, terror, and repression.
