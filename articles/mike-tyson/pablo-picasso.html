Pablo Picasso

Spanish artist, pioneer of Cubism (1881–1973)

Pablo Picasso was the most influential and prolific artist of the twentieth century, a Spaniard who spent his working life in France and reinvented Western art more than once. A dazzling prodigy who could draw like a master in childhood, he passed through his melancholy Blue Period and tender Rose Period before, in 1907, shattering five centuries of convention with the jagged faces of Les Demoiselles d'Avignon and opening the way to Cubism, which he and Georges Braque developed by breaking the world into shifting geometric planes.

For the next sixty years he never stopped transforming—through collage, sculpture, ceramics, and a restless return to and reworking of the old masters. His monumental Guernica, painted in furious black, white, and grey after the bombing of a Basque town in the Spanish Civil War, became the most searing anti-war image of the age, and he vowed it would not hang in Spain while the dictator lived.

Endlessly inventive and endlessly famous, he became the very image of the modern artist, his name a synonym for genius and for the avant-garde.

Across his long career he was measured against one contemporary above all, a friend and rival with whom he was paired as the twin pole of modern art—the French master of colour, Henri Matisse.
