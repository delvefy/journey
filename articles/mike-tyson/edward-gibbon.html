Edward Gibbon

English historian (1737–1794)

Edward Gibbon was the greatest historian of the English Enlightenment and the author of one of the supreme works of history ever written. A sickly, bookish child from a Hampshire family, he was sent in his youth to Lausanne—to be cured of a brief conversion to Catholicism—and there acquired the French polish and the immense learning that would mark his life's work. He sat for years as a quiet member of Parliament, but his true vocation lay in the study.

The inspiration came to him, he later wrote, as he sat musing amid the ruins of the Capitol in Rome in 1764. The result was The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, published in six volumes between 1776 and 1789. In sweeping, ironic, magnificently balanced prose it traced the empire's collapse from the golden age of the Antonines through the fall of the West and on across the thousand years of Byzantium, drawing on ancient sources from Tacitus to Procopius.

Its skeptical, often barbed treatment of the rise of Christianity scandalized the pious and delighted the philosophers. Gibbon moved easily among the leading minds of his age, a friend of David Hume and Adam Smith and a member, alongside the wits of London, of the famous literary club.

Genial, vain, and endlessly erudite, Gibbon died in 1794. The towering man of letters who presided over that London circle—the lexicographer and critic at its very heart—was Samuel Johnson.
