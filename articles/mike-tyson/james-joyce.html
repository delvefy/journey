James Joyce

Irish modernist novelist (1882–1941)

James Joyce, born in Dublin in 1882, was the most radically innovative novelist of the twentieth century and the supreme chronicler of his native city, which he wrote about obsessively even as he spent almost his entire adult life in exile on the Continent. Educated by the Jesuits and at University College Dublin, he left Ireland in 1904 with Nora Barnacle and lived thereafter in Trieste, Zurich and Paris, poor, half-blind and utterly dedicated to his art.

His early work was comparatively conventional: the precise, melancholy short stories of Dubliners and the autobiographical A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man. But with Ulysses, published in Paris in 1922, he transformed the novel. Following a single ordinary day in the life of the Dublin advertising canvasser Leopold Bloom, the book maps its hero's wanderings onto the structure of Homer's Odyssey and unleashes a dazzling array of styles and the famous "stream of consciousness," plunging the reader directly into the flow of human thought.

Ulysses was banned for obscenity in Britain and America for years, but its influence on modern literature was immense. Joyce then spent seventeen years on his final, even more experimental work, Finnegans Wake, a dreamlike river of multilingual puns that few claim fully to understand.

Championed by writers from Ezra Pound to T. S. Eliot, and assisted in his last years by the young Samuel Beckett, Joyce became a legend in his own lifetime. He died in Zurich in 1941. Difficult, comic, encyclopaedic and profoundly humane, his work remade what fiction could do, and "Bloomsday" is still celebrated each June in Dublin and around the world.
