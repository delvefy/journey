Guru Nanak

Founder of Sikhism, first of the Sikh Gurus (1469–1539)

Guru Nanak was the founder of the Sikh religion and the first of its ten Gurus. He was born in the Punjab, into a Hindu family, in a village now honoured as Nankana Sahib. As a young man, tradition tells, he vanished into a river while bathing and returned after three days transformed, declaring that there was neither Hindu nor Muslim—only one humanity before one God.

On that conviction he built his teaching. He proclaimed a single, formless, all-pervading God, and the equality of every person regardless of caste, creed, or sex. He condemned empty ritual, superstition, and the pride of priests, and set in their place three simple duties: to remember God, to earn an honest living, and to share with others. To embody equality he founded the institution of the free communal kitchen, the langar, where all sat and ate together.

For years he travelled vast distances—across India and, by tradition, as far as Mecca and Baghdad—teaching and debating, accompanied by his faithful Muslim companion and musician, Mardana. He lived through the storm of Babur's invasion of the Punjab, and his hymns lament the cruelty and suffering it brought.

In his last years he gathered a community at the town of Kartarpur and, passing over his own sons, chose a devoted disciple to carry on his work, beginning the succession of Gurus. The tenth and last of that line, who forged the Sikhs into the warrior brotherhood of the Khalsa, was Guru Gobind Singh.
