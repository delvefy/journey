William Wilberforce

English abolitionist and politician (1759–1833)

William Wilberforce was born in 1759 into a prosperous merchant family in Hull. At Cambridge he formed a lifelong friendship with William Pitt the Younger, and in 1780, still in his early twenties, he entered Parliament, where his wealth, charm and gift for oratory promised an easy and conventional career.

Around 1785 he underwent an intense evangelical conversion that redirected his life. Encouraged by the former slave-ship captain turned clergyman John Newton, and urged by Pitt to remain in public life, he took up the cause that would define him. From 1787 he became the parliamentary leader of the campaign against the slave trade, working alongside Thomas Clarkson and the reformers of the Clapham Sect.

Year after year he introduced abolition bills, and year after year they were defeated by powerful commercial interests. Persistence finally triumphed with the Slave Trade Act of 1807, which outlawed the British traffic in human beings. Wilberforce then turned to the abolition of slavery itself throughout the empire.

A tireless social reformer, he also promoted missionary societies, Bible distribution and the prevention of cruelty to animals, and his book A Practical View of Christianity was widely read. He lived just long enough to learn that the Slavery Abolition Act had passed: he died in 1833, three days later, and was buried in Westminster Abbey near his old friend Pitt.
