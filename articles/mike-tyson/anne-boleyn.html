Anne Boleyn

Queen of England, second wife of Henry VIII (c. 1501–1536)

Anne Boleyn was the woman for whom Henry VIII broke with Rome and remade the religion of England, and whose dramatic rise and bloody fall make her one of the most famous figures in English history. Raised in part at the sophisticated French court, she returned home witty, accomplished, and self-possessed. When the king pursued her, she refused to become merely his mistress as her sister had been—she would be his wife or nothing.

Henry's determination to marry her, and to discard his first wife Catherine of Aragon, drove the great crisis of the age. When the pope would not grant an annulment, the king broke England's thousand-year bond with the Roman Church, declared himself Supreme Head of the Church of England, and married Anne in 1533. Their union opened the door to the English Reformation.

In September 1533 Anne bore not the promised son but a daughter—the future Elizabeth I, who would become England's greatest queen. Further pregnancies ended in loss, and Henry's affection curdled into impatience. The king's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, assembled charges of adultery, incest, and treason that almost no one believed.

In May 1536, not yet queen for three years, Anne was beheaded within the Tower of London by a French swordsman. Her true legacy was her daughter and the religious revolution her marriage had set in motion. Among the great men who refused to bend to that revolution—declining to accept the new marriage and the king's supremacy, and paying with his head—was the scholar and statesman Thomas More.
