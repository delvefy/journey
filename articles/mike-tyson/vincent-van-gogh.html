Vincent van Gogh

Dutch post-impressionist painter (1853–1890)

Vincent van Gogh is among the most beloved and famous painters who ever lived—and one of the most tragic, for in his lifetime he sold almost nothing and was thought a failure. He came to art only after stumbling as an art dealer and as a preacher among poor miners, and he worked for barely a decade, sustained through poverty and despair by his devoted brother Theo, to whom he poured out his soul in hundreds of letters.

Steeped in the Dutch tradition and worshipping Rembrandt above all, he forged a style entirely his own: thick, urgent brushstrokes and colours of blazing intensity that seemed to make the very fields and skies pulse with life. In the bright light of Arles he painted his sunflowers and his Yellow House, and invited a fellow artist to join him there; but their stormy companionship collapsed in the crisis during which van Gogh severed part of his own ear.

Tormented by mental illness, he committed himself to an asylum, where he painted the swirling Starry Night, and in 1890 he died of a gunshot wound at the age of thirty-seven. Within a generation he was revered as a genius and a martyr to his art, his canvases among the most valued on earth.

The fellow painter who had shared his house in Arles, and whose visit ended in his breakdown, was Paul Gauguin.
