Johann Sebastian Bach

German Baroque composer (1685–1750)

Johann Sebastian Bach was the supreme master of the Baroque and, to many, the greatest composer who ever lived. Born in Eisenach into a sprawling German dynasty of musicians, orphaned young, he taught himself in large part by copying the scores of others. He spent his life as a working church and court musician—organist and music director in a succession of German towns, and finally, for twenty-seven years, cantor of St. Thomas's Church in Leipzig.

From this laborious round of duties arose a body of music of inexhaustible richness and unrivaled contrapuntal mastery. The Brandenburg Concertos, the suites for solo cello and violin, the Well-Tempered Clavier with its preludes and fugues in every key, the Goldberg Variations, the towering St. Matthew Passion and Mass in B minor, and hundreds of cantatas—all bear the stamp of a mind that found infinite invention within strict form. He was also a legendary organist and improviser.

In 1747 he visited the court of the Prussian king at Potsdam, who challenged him with a difficult theme; Bach improvised upon it and later wove it into the intricate Musical Offering. Devout, prolific in music and in family alike, he fathered twenty children, several of whom became noted composers.

Little celebrated as a composer in his own day and fully recognized only generations later, Bach died in 1750. The royal patron who set him that famous theme—soldier, statesman, and flute-playing philosopher-king—was Frederick the Great.
