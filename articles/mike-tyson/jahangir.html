Jahangir

Fourth Mughal emperor, patron of the arts (1569–1627; r. 1605–1627)

Jahangir—a title meaning “Seizer of the World”—was the fourth of the Mughal emperors and the son of the great Akbar, into whose vast and prosperous empire he was born as Prince Salim. Impatient for the throne, he rebelled against his father in his youth, but succeeded him peacefully in 1605 and ruled for more than two decades over the wealthiest realm of the age.

He is remembered above all as a lover and connoisseur of beauty. Under his patronage Mughal painting reached its finest flowering, prized for its exquisite portraits and studies of birds, beasts, and flowers. He was himself an acute observer of nature and a curious collector, and he left a remarkable personal memoir recording his pleasures, his judgements, and his ailments with unusual candour.

Yet the cares of empire often slipped from his grasp, for he was given to wine and opium, and in his later years real power passed to his formidable wife, who issued decrees and struck coins in her own name. He maintained much of his father's tolerance, though he ordered the execution of the Sikh Guru Arjan, and he received at his court the English ambassador Sir Thomas Roe, opening the way to European trade.

When his own son Prince Khurram rose in revolt, the aging emperor's grip weakened further, and he died in 1627 on the road from Kashmir. The brilliant and commanding empress who ruled in his name was Nur Jahan.
