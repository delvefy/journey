Spartacus

Thracian gladiator and leader of a slave revolt (died 71 BC)

Spartacus was a Thracian, by some accounts once a soldier, who was enslaved and trained as a gladiator at a school in Capua. In 73 BC he led a breakout of a few dozen gladiators, armed at first with kitchen knives and stolen weapons, and took refuge on the slopes of Vesuvius. There escaped slaves flocked to him by the thousand, and within months he commanded an army that shook Italy to its foundations.

For two years his followers—tens of thousands strong—defeated one Roman force after another, ranging the length of the peninsula. Spartacus proved a leader of rare skill and humanity, though his aims remain uncertain: whether to lead his people over the Alps to freedom or to strike at Rome itself. Rome at last turned to its richest man, Crassus, who hemmed the rebels into the south and crushed them in 71 BC; Spartacus fell fighting, and six thousand of his men were crucified along the Appian Way, while Pompey claimed the glory of finishing the war.

Defeated and killed, Spartacus was never forgotten. He became the enduring emblem of the oppressed rising against their masters, hailed by Karl Marx as the finest figure of all antiquity.

His name was taken up by later rebels in his honour—most famously by the revolutionary Spartacus League of early twentieth-century Germany, whose fiery leader was Rosa Luxemburg.
