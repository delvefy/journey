Ovid

Roman poet (43 BC–17 AD)

Publius Ovidius Naso, known as Ovid, was a Roman poet of the Augustan age whose witty, sensuous verse and inexhaustible storytelling made him one of the most influential writers of antiquity. Born in 43 BC at Sulmo, he abandoned a public career for poetry and quickly won fame with playful works of love: the Amores, the verse letters of the Heroides, and the cheekily instructive Art of Love.

His masterpiece is the Metamorphoses, an epic in fifteen books that gathers hundreds of Greek and Roman myths into a single flowing narrative united by the theme of transformation, running from the creation of the world down to the deification of Julius Caesar. Alongside it he composed the Fasti, a poetic tour of the Roman calendar and its festivals.

At the height of his success, in 8 AD, the emperor Augustus abruptly banished him to Tomis, a bleak outpost on the Black Sea. Ovid blamed his ruin cryptically on "a poem and a mistake," and despite years of mournful, pleading verse written from exile in the Tristia and Letters from Pontus, he was never pardoned and died far from Rome.

Yet his afterlife was glorious. The Metamorphoses became the great storehouse of classical myth for later ages, feeding the imaginations of medieval and Renaissance poets and painters. Dante honored him among the supreme poets, and Shakespeare mined his tales again and again. Few writers have shaped the Western imagination so deeply.
