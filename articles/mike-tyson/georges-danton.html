Georges Danton

French revolutionary (1759–1794)

Georges Danton was the great orator and force of nature of the French Revolution, as warm, earthy, and impulsive as his rival Robespierre was cold and precise. A Paris lawyer with a booming voice and a commanding presence, he rose through the radical clubs to become one of the leaders who toppled the monarchy, driving the insurrection of August 1792 that swept away the throne of Louis XVI.

As the armies of Europe closed in on the young Republic, it was Danton who rallied the nation with the cry that became immortal: "Audacity, and again audacity, and always audacity!" He served as the first president of the Committee of Public Safety and was for a time the most powerful man in France.

Yet as the guillotine's work grew ever bloodier, Danton sickened of it and began to call for mercy and an end to the Terror. That plea for clemency was his undoing. Robespierre and the Committee turned on him, and though his thunderous defense shook the courtroom, he was condemned on trumped-up charges and sent to the scaffold in April 1794, only months before Robespierre himself would follow. To the executioner he flung his last, characteristic command: "Show my head to the people—it is well worth seeing."

His closest comrade in the early days of the radical cause, and a fellow firebrand of the revolutionary press, was Jean-Paul Marat.
