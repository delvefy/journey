William Wordsworth

English Romantic poet (1770–1850)

William Wordsworth was the central figure of English Romanticism, the poet who taught his age to find the sublime in nature and in the feelings of the human heart. Born in the Lake District and orphaned young, he was educated at Cambridge and, as a young man, thrilled to the early promise of the French Revolution—an enthusiasm that later soured into disillusion and, in old age, conservatism.

The decisive event of his life was his friendship with Samuel Taylor Coleridge. Together, in 1798, they published Lyrical Ballads, a collection whose famous preface called for poetry written in "the real language of men" and drawn from humble life and the natural world. With that manifesto the Romantic movement in English poetry was born.

Settling among the lakes and fells he loved, Wordsworth wrote much of the finest verse in the language: "Tintern Abbey," the daffodils of "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud," the great "Ode: Intimations of Immortality," and his vast autobiographical epic The Prelude, which traced "the growth of a poet's mind" in conscious emulation of Milton.

Honored at last as Poet Laureate, he died in 1850, long outliving the younger Romantics who had once measured themselves against him. The dear friend with whom he had launched the Romantic age, and whose own genius burned as brightly as his own, was Samuel Taylor Coleridge.
