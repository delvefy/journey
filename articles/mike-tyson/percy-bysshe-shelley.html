Percy Bysshe Shelley

English Romantic poet (1792–1822)

Percy Bysshe Shelley was one of the supreme lyric poets of the English Romantic movement, a radical idealist whose visionary verse and rebellious life made him a legend. Born into a wealthy landed family, he was expelled from Oxford for co-authoring a pamphlet on The Necessity of Atheism, a defiance of convention that set the pattern of his career. Steeped in the revolutionary philosophy of William Godwin, he eloped with Godwin's daughter, the future author of Frankenstein.

Settling in Italy, the Shelleys lived near Lord Byron, with whom Percy formed a deep friendship. There he produced an astonishing body of work: the sonnet Ozymandias, on the ruin of tyrants' pride; the soaring Ode to the West Wind and To a Skylark; the cosmic drama Prometheus Unbound; and the fierce political poem The Masque of Anarchy, with its cry, "Ye are many—they are few." In his essay A Defence of Poetry he called poets "the unacknowledged legislators of the world."

When the young poet John Keats died in Rome, Shelley poured his grief into Adonais, one of the great elegies in the language. His own end came soon after: in 1822, not yet thirty, he drowned when his small boat foundered in a storm off the Italian coast, and his body was burned on the shore.

The brilliant fellow Romantic whose early death Shelley lamented in his most famous elegy was John Keats.
