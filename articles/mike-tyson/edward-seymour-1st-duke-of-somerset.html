Edward Seymour, 1st Duke of Somerset

Lord Protector of England (c. 1500–1552)

Edward Seymour, born about 1500, owed his meteoric rise to his sister Jane Seymour, whose marriage to Henry VIII in 1536 and birth of the future Edward VI made her brother uncle to the heir of the throne. Already a soldier and courtier, he gathered titles and lands, becoming Earl of Hertford and a leading figure on the council in Henry's final years.

When Henry VIII died in 1547, Seymour swiftly out-manoeuvred his colleagues to have himself named Lord Protector and Duke of Somerset, ruling England in the name of his nine-year-old nephew. A convinced evangelical, he allowed Thomas Cranmer to drive the Reformation forward, dissolving the chantries and issuing the first English Book of Common Prayer in 1549.

Abroad he pursued the "Rough Wooing," the attempt to force the marriage of Edward VI to the infant Mary, Queen of Scots. He smashed a Scottish army at the Battle of Pinkie in 1547, but the costly garrisons that followed yielded nothing, and the young queen was spirited away to France.

His protectorate unravelled in 1549. Religious change and enclosure provoked the Prayer Book Rebellion in the west and Kett's Rebellion in Norfolk, and his rivals, led by John Dudley, blamed his rule for the disorder. He had already sent his own brother Thomas to the block; now he himself was stripped of the protectorate. Briefly restored to the council, he intrigued again, was condemned on doubtful charges, and was beheaded on Tower Hill in January 1552. Remembered by some as the "Good Duke" for his sympathy with the commons, he was in truth an austere, overreaching man undone by the faction he could not master.
