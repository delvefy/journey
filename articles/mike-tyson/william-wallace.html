William Wallace

Scottish patriot and Guardian of Scotland (c. 1270–1305)

William Wallace rose from the minor Scottish gentry to become the embodiment of his nation's defiance. When Edward I overran Scotland in 1296, deposed its king, and demanded the homage of its people, Wallace answered with violence, killing the English sheriff of Lanark in 1297 and gathering around him a rebellion of commoners and lesser knights.

His great moment came that September at Stirling Bridge. Together with Andrew Moray he trapped a far larger English army as it funneled across the narrow span and destroyed it, a victory that swept the occupiers out of much of the country. Moray died of his wounds, but Wallace was knighted and made Guardian of Scotland, ruling in the name of the absent king.

The triumph did not last. In 1298 Edward returned in force and met Wallace at Falkirk, where English longbowmen tore apart the Scottish spear-rings. Defeated, Wallace gave up the Guardianship and slipped into the life of a hunted outlaw, keeping resistance alive while seeking help abroad.

In 1305 he was betrayed and handed to the English. Dragged to London and condemned as a traitor—a charge he denied, for he had never sworn loyalty to Edward—he was hanged, disemboweled, and quartered, his head set above London Bridge. But his example did not die with him. The cause he had carried passed to a greater lord, who would at last win the crown and the freedom of Scotland—Robert the Bruce.
