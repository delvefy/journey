Edward I

King of England, “Longshanks” (1239–1307)

Edward I, tall enough to earn the nickname Longshanks, was the most formidable English king of the Middle Ages. As a young prince he saved his father Henry III's crown by crushing the rebel baron Simon de Montfort at Evesham in 1265, then took the cross and fought in the Holy Land. He was still returning from crusade when, in 1272, news reached him that he was king.

His reign was one of conquest and law. He subdued Wales by force, killing the last native prince and ringing the country with a chain of mighty stone castles—Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech—that stand to this day, and bestowing the title Prince of Wales on his heir. At home he earned the name “the English Justinian” for his sweeping statutes and his summoning of the Model Parliament of 1295, while in 1290 he ordered the expulsion of England's Jews.

He loved his queen, Eleanor of Castile, with rare devotion, marking the route of her funeral cortege with carved crosses. But it is for Scotland that he is best remembered. Drawn in to judge a disputed succession, he claimed overlordship, provoked revolt, and earned on his tomb the grim title Malleus Scotorum, Hammer of the Scots.

He defeated the Scots in pitched battle and hunted their leaders without mercy, yet the resistance he could never quite stamp out outlived him; he died in 1307 on the march north, with Robert the Bruce already in rebellion. The Scottish patriot whose rising he broke at Falkirk, and whom he put to a traitor's death in London, was William Wallace.
