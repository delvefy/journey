Galileo Galilei

Italian astronomer and physicist (1564–1642)

Galileo Galilei, born in Pisa in 1564, was an Italian astronomer, physicist, and mathematician often called the father of modern science. Studying motion with unprecedented rigor, he showed that bodies fall with uniform acceleration regardless of their weight, investigated the swing of pendulums, and grasped the principle of inertia, laying foundations on which Isaac Newton would later build.

In 1609 Galileo learned of a new Dutch invention, the telescope, and quickly built far better instruments of his own. Turning them to the sky, he made a cascade of discoveries that he announced in 1610: four moons circling Jupiter, the phases of Venus, mountains and craters on the Moon, and countless unseen stars in the Milky Way. These observations provided powerful evidence for the Copernican view that the Earth orbits the Sun.

His advocacy of heliocentrism brought him into conflict with the Catholic Church. Warned in 1616 not to defend the idea, he nonetheless published his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems in 1632, a brilliant and barely veiled argument for Copernicus that offended Pope Urban VIII. The Roman Inquisition tried him in 1633, forced him to recant on his knees, and condemned him to house arrest for the rest of his life; tradition holds that he muttered "and yet it moves."

Confined and going blind, Galileo nevertheless completed his greatest scientific work, Two New Sciences, which set out the modern study of motion and materials. He died in 1642, the year of Newton's birth, his vindication left to posterity.
