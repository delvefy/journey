Herodotus

Greek historian (c. 484–425 BC)

Herodotus, born around 484 BC in Halicarnassus on the coast of Asia Minor, is honored as the "Father of History," the title bestowed on him in antiquity by Cicero. His great work, the Histories, was the first sustained prose attempt in the Western tradition to record the past through systematic inquiry rather than myth or epic, and indeed the Greek word he used, historia, meaning "inquiry," gave the discipline its name.

The Histories set out to explain the origins and course of the great wars between the Greeks and the Persian Empire. To do so, Herodotus ranged across the known world, traveling through Egypt, Babylon, and the lands around the Black Sea, gathering accounts of peoples, customs, geography, and marvels. His narrative traces the rise of Persia under Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius, and Xerxes, and the desperate Greek resistance at Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, and Plataea.

His method mingled careful investigation with digression, anecdote, and the occasional tall tale, and later critics both admired and chided him for it. Yet his curiosity about other cultures and his effort to weigh competing accounts marked a profound advance in human thought.

Herodotus moved in the brilliant circles of his day, counting the playwright Sophocles among his friends and spending time in the Athens of Pericles. He is said to have joined the new Greek colony of Thurii in southern Italy. He died around 425 BC, leaving a work that remains our richest window onto the ancient Mediterranean and Near Eastern world.
