Guru Gobind Singh

Tenth Sikh Guru, founder of the Khalsa (1666–1708)

Guru Gobind Singh was the tenth and last of the human Gurus of the Sikhs, a warrior, poet, and spiritual leader who gave the faith its final form. He became Guru as a child of nine, after his father, the ninth Guru, was beheaded at Delhi on the orders of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb for defending the persecuted—a martyrdom that set the boy at the head of a community under siege.

In 1699, on the spring festival of Baisakhi, he founded the Khalsa, the brotherhood of the “pure.” Calling for volunteers willing to die for their faith, he initiated his first disciples with sweetened water stirred by a sword, gave the men the surname Singh, “lion,” and the women Kaur, and bound them to the five articles of faith and to the defence of justice and the weak. He himself was then initiated by them in turn.

His life was one of almost continual war against the Mughal armies and the allied kings of the hills. The cost to him was terrible: all four of his sons perished, the two elder falling in battle, the two younger captured as children and walled up alive. Yet he held to his cause, a fierce soldier who was also a refined writer and patron of learning.

Before his death by an assassin's blade in 1708, he ended the succession of living Gurus, proclaiming the sacred scripture itself the eternal Guru of the Sikhs. His father, who had given his life to the executioner rather than abandon freedom of conscience, was Guru Tegh Bahadur.
