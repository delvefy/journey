Scipio Africanus

Roman general, conqueror of Hannibal (236–183 BC)

Publius Cornelius Scipio rose to greatness amid the darkest years Rome had ever known, when Hannibal stalked Italy and one army after another was destroyed. As a youth he had fought at the Ticinus and survived the slaughter of Cannae, rallying the broken remnants; and where older men despaired, he burned to carry the war back to the enemy.

Given command in Spain while still young, he stunned the world by seizing New Carthage in a single bold stroke and clearing the peninsula of Carthaginian power. Then, against the caution of the Senate, he crossed to Africa itself, winning over the Numidian king Masinissa and threatening Carthage so directly that Hannibal had at last to be recalled from Italy to defend his homeland.

The two greatest soldiers of the age met in 202 BC on the field of Zama, and there Scipio at last defeated the until-then unconquered Hannibal, ending the long war and winning the surname Africanus. Charismatic and aloof, he let it be believed that he enjoyed the special favor of the gods.

His later years were soured by the envy of rivals like Cato, who hounded the Scipios over money and pride until the embittered hero withdrew to his country estate and died asking that ungrateful Rome should not hold his bones; through his daughter Cornelia his blood passed to the reforming Gracchi. The brilliant, relentless Carthaginian commander whom he alone among Romans could defeat was Hannibal.
