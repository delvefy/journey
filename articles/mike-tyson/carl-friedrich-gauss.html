Carl Friedrich Gauss

German mathematician and physicist (1777–1855)

Carl Friedrich Gauss was born in Brunswick in 1777 to a poor family and revealed a prodigious gift for calculation as a small child. Supported by the Duke of Brunswick, he proved while still a teenager that a regular seventeen-sided polygon could be constructed with compass and straightedge, and at twenty-four published the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, the work that founded modern number theory.

His public fame came in 1801, when he used a new method of least squares to compute the orbit of the newly discovered minor planet Ceres from a handful of observations and successfully predicted where it would reappear. The triumph won him the directorship of the observatory at Göttingen, which he held for the rest of his life.

Gauss's reach extended across mathematics and physics. He gave the normal distribution its central place in the theory of errors, transformed the differential geometry of curved surfaces with his Theorema Egregium, and laid groundwork later carried forward by his student Bernhard Riemann. With Wilhelm Weber he investigated magnetism and built an early electric telegraph, and he joined Alexander von Humboldt in organizing a worldwide survey of the Earth's magnetic field.

A demanding perfectionist whose motto was “few, but ripe,” he published only a fraction of what he discovered, and revered the work of Archimedes and Newton above all. Known in his lifetime as the Prince of Mathematicians, he died at Göttingen in 1855. Among the contemporaries whose work most engaged his own was the French astronomer and mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace.
