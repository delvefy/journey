Protagoras

Greek Sophist (c. 490–c. 420 BC)

Protagoras of Abdera was the first and greatest of the Sophists, the traveling teachers who offered the ambitious young men of Greece instruction in argument, persuasion, and the conduct of public life—for a substantial fee. He was the first to call himself by that name and to charge for his teaching, and he grew famous and wealthy plying his trade from city to city.

His thought was as provocative as his profession. “Man is the measure of all things,” he declared, meaning that truth is relative to the perceiver, with no fixed standard above human judgment. Of the gods he was frankly agnostic, writing that he could not know whether they existed or what they were like, since the question was obscure and life too short—words that, by tradition, had his book burned and brought a charge of impiety.

For all his skepticism he was honored by the greatest statesman of the age, Pericles, who befriended him and entrusted him with drafting the laws for the new colony of Thurii. The two were said to have once argued a fine point of responsibility for a whole day.

Plato made him the title figure of a famous dialogue, setting him in courteous debate with Socrates over whether virtue can be taught—securing his memory even as it questioned his doctrines. Among the rival teachers who shared his calling, the Sophist celebrated for his fable of Heracles at the crossroads and his fine distinctions between words was Prodicus.
