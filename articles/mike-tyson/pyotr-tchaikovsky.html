Pyotr Tchaikovsky

Russian composer (1840–1893)

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky was the first Russian composer to win lasting international fame, fusing the emotional directness of Western Romanticism with a distinctly Russian melodic gift. Born in Votkinsk to a mining engineer's family, he was first set on a bureaucrat's path, studying at the School of Jurisprudence in St. Petersburg and working as a clerk in the Ministry of Justice before abandoning the civil service for music.

He entered the new St. Petersburg Conservatory under Anton Rubinstein, then joined the faculty of the Moscow Conservatory founded by Nikolai Rubinstein. For thirteen years he was sustained by the wealthy widow Nadezhda von Meck, who financed him generously on the condition that they never meet, and they never did. His brief, calamitous marriage to Antonina Milyukova collapsed within weeks and brought him to a nervous breakdown.

His output became a permanent part of the concert and theatre repertoire: the ballets Swan Lake, The Sleeping Beauty, and The Nutcracker; the operas Eugene Onegin and The Queen of Spades; six symphonies culminating in the despairing Pathétique; the First Piano Concerto, the Violin Concerto, the Romeo and Juliet overture, and the 1812 Overture. He stood apart from the nationalist Mighty Handful, more cosmopolitan in craft.

Above all composers he revered Mozart, calling him a musical Christ and paying homage in his Mozartiana suite. He died in St. Petersburg in 1893, nine days after conducting the premiere of the Pathétique, amid a cholera epidemic and circumstances still debated. His two greatest operas were drawn from the verse of Russia's national poet, Alexander Pushkin.
