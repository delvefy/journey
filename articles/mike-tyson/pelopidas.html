Pelopidas

Theban general and statesman (c. 410–364 BC)

Pelopidas was the soldier who set Thebes free and helped raise it, for one brilliant decade, to the leadership of Greece. When a Spartan garrison and a puppet oligarchy held his city, he led a handful of exiles back in secret—some disguised as revelers—to strike down the tyrants in a single night and restore Theban liberty.

He took command of the Sacred Band, the famed corps of paired warriors, and with it shattered the myth of Spartan invincibility, defeating a larger Spartan force at Tegyra. Together with his lifelong friend Epaminondas he broke the power of Sparta for good and made Thebes, briefly, the first city of Greece.

His career carried him far beyond the battlefield. He intervened in the affairs of Thessaly, where the cruel tyrant of Pherae once seized him by treachery until Epaminondas marched to his rescue; and he undertook a celebrated embassy deep into the Persian empire, winning the favor of the Great King for Thebes. In 364 BC, charging too rashly against the Pheraean tyrant at Cynoscephalae, he won the battle but lost his life.

The Theban military system he and Epaminondas perfected was studied by a royal hostage then living in the city, the young Philip of Macedon, who would carry its lessons to greatness. Plutarch paired his life with that of the Roman Marcellus. The Persian Great King at whose court at Susa Pelopidas pleaded the cause of Thebes was Artaxerxes II.
