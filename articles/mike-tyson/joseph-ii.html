Joseph II

Holy Roman Emperor (1741–1790)

Joseph II was the most radical of the "enlightened despots," a ruler who tried to remake an empire by reason and decree. The eldest son of Maria Theresa, he became Holy Roman Emperor in 1765 and co-regent of the Habsburg lands, then ruled them alone from his mother's death in 1780. Impatient and idealistic, he believed a monarch should be the servant of his people and used his power to impose sweeping reform from above.

In a torrent of decrees he abolished serfdom, granted toleration to Protestants and Jews, curbed the power of the Church and closed contemplative monasteries, swept away torture, and tried to weld his many peoples into a single, German-speaking, rationally governed state. But reform forced on subjects faster than they would bear provoked bitter resistance in Hungary and the Netherlands, and much of his work was undone even before he died.

A devoted patron of music in his glittering Vienna, he supported Gluck and Mozart—he is the emperor of legend who complained that a Mozart opera had "too many notes." He was also the brother of Marie Antoinette, whose marriage in France he watched over with anxious advice, and an ally of Catherine the Great.

Exhausted and embittered by failure, Joseph died in 1790, asking that his epitaph record a man who "failed in all he undertook." The great composer whom he raised to be his court Kapellmeister, and who transformed the art of opera, was Christoph Willibald Gluck.
