Peter Paul Rubens

Flemish Baroque painter and diplomat (1577–1640)

Peter Paul Rubens was the prince of Baroque painters, a Flemish master whose vast, surging canvases of saints, gods, and full-bodied beauties defined the exuberant art of the age. Raised in Antwerp, he spent eight formative years in Italy, where he absorbed the grandeur of Michelangelo and the glowing color of the Venetians, copying the old masters until their lessons were in his bones.

Returning home, he set up a studio that became a kind of factory of genius, employing scores of assistants to meet a flood of commissions from across Europe. His output was enormous and unmistakable: the muscular drama of The Raising of the Cross and Descent from the Cross, the swirling mythologies, the great cycle glorifying Marie de' Medici, and the radiant late landscapes and portraits of his second wife. So characteristic was his ideal of abundant, fleshly beauty that the word "Rubenesque" entered the language.

Rubens was also a learned gentleman and trusted diplomat. Fluent in several languages, he carried out delicate missions for the Spanish crown, helping negotiate peace between Spain and England, and was knighted by both Philip IV and Charles I—for whom he painted the ceiling of the Banqueting House in Whitehall.

Honored, wealthy, and happy in his work to the last, he died at Antwerp in 1640, the most influential painter north of the Alps. Above every other artist he studied, the Venetian whose color and freedom he revered, copied throughout his life, and never ceased to call his master, was Titian.
