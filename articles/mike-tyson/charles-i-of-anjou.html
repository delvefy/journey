Charles I of Anjou

King of Sicily and Naples (1226/27–1285)

Charles I of Anjou, born in 1226 or 1227, was the youngest son of King Louis VIII of France and Blanche of Castile, and the brother of the saintly Louis IX. Ambitious, cold and tireless, he rose from the county of Anjou and, by marriage, Provence, to become one of the most formidable rulers in Europe and the founder of a Mediterranean empire.

His great opportunity came from the papacy, which, locked in its long struggle with the Hohenstaufen, offered Charles the crown of Sicily if he could win it. He did. In 1266 he crushed and killed Manfred, the Hohenstaufen king, at the Battle of Benevento, and in 1268 he destroyed the last of the line, the young Conradin, at Tagliacozzo—then had the sixteen-year-old beheaded in the marketplace of Naples. Charles was now king of Sicily and Naples.

From this base his ambitions swelled. He made himself the dominant power in Italy, championed the pro-papal Guelph cause, and dreamed of conquering the restored Byzantine Empire and ruling from Constantinople. For a time he seemed unstoppable.

Then came catastrophe. His French officials, harsh and rapacious, were hated in Sicily, and on Easter Monday of 1282 the island exploded in the rising known as the Sicilian Vespers, in which thousands of Frenchmen were massacred. The Sicilians offered their crown to Peter III of Aragon, and Charles, for all his power, could never win the island back. He died in 1285, his grand designs broken, though his dynasty would rule Naples for generations—and left, among many monuments, a famous portrait statue carved by Arnolfo di Cambio.
