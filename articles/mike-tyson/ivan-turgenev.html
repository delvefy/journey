Ivan Turgenev

Russian novelist (1818–1883)

Ivan Sergeyevich Turgenev was the Russian novelist who did most to introduce his country's literature to Western Europe. Born at Oryol into wealthy gentry, he was raised by a domineering, serf-owning mother whose cruelty bred in him a lifelong hatred of serfdom. After study in Moscow, St. Petersburg, and Berlin, he turned to letters.

His breakthrough was A Sportsman's Sketches, tender portraits of peasant life that helped turn educated opinion against serfdom; tradition holds the future Alexander II was among its readers. The book, together with a warm obituary he wrote for Nikolai Gogol, brought him a month's arrest and confinement to his estate. There followed the novels that made his name: Rudin, Home of the Gentry, On the Eve, and his masterpiece Fathers and Sons, whose hero Bazarov fixed the word “nihilist” in the world's vocabulary.

A liberal Westerniser, Turgenev quarrelled bitterly with the more nationalist Leo Tolstoy—the two nearly fought a duel before reconciling years later—and with Fyodor Dostoevsky, who pilloried him in The Devils. He revered Alexander Pushkin and spoke at the dedication of the poet's monument in 1880.

For much of his life he lived abroad, drawn to the singer Pauline Viardot, and in Paris he became a fixture of its literary world. There his closest friend was the great French novelist Gustave Flaubert, whose company and craft he prized above almost any other. He died near Paris in 1883.
