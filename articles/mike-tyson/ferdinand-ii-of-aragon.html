Ferdinand II of Aragon

King of Aragon, one of the Catholic Monarchs (1452–1516)

Ferdinand II of Aragon was the cunning and formidable king whose marriage to Isabella of Castile forged the kingdom of Spain and whose statecraft made it the leading power of Europe. King of Aragon, Sicily, and Naples, he and Isabella ruled as the "Catholic Monarchs," pooling their crowns and their armies in a partnership of rare effectiveness while keeping their realms legally distinct.

With Isabella he completed the Reconquista at Granada in 1492, financed the voyage of Christopher Columbus that opened the Americas, and enforced a harsh religious unity through the Inquisition and the expulsion of the Jews. Abroad, Ferdinand proved a master of war and diplomacy. His brilliant general Gonzalo de Córdoba wrested the rich kingdom of Naples from France, and he seized much of Navarre, extending Spanish power deep into Italy and across the Pyrenees.

After Isabella's death in 1504 he clung to power, governing Castile as regent in the name of their unstable daughter Joanna and continuing to direct Spain's rise. So skillful and ruthless was his blend of guile, religion, and force that Niccolò Machiavelli singled him out in The Prince as the supreme example of the successful "new prince" of the modern age.

Ferdinand died in 1516, leaving the united Spanish inheritance to pass at last to his grandson, the Emperor Charles V. Among his children who shaped the history of Europe was the daughter who became the first queen of Henry VIII and the mother of Mary I—Catherine of Aragon.
