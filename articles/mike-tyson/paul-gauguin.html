Paul Gauguin

French post-impressionist painter (1848–1903)

Paul Gauguin abandoned a comfortable career as a stockbroker—and, in time, his wife and children—to give himself wholly to painting, a reckless devotion that produced some of the most original art of his century. Tutored at first within Impressionism, he soon broke free toward a bolder vision: broad areas of flat, unnatural colour and simplified, symbolic forms that left mere appearance behind.

His friendship with van Gogh, with whom he briefly shared the Yellow House at Arles, ended in the famous crisis that cost van Gogh part of his ear. Restless and convinced that European civilization had corrupted art, Gauguin sailed to the South Seas, settling in Tahiti and finally the Marquesas, where he painted Polynesian life in glowing, dreamlike canvases and posed the great questions—where do we come from, what are we, where are we going—on his largest work.

He died poor and ailing on a distant island, little honoured in his lifetime. Yet his liberation of colour and his hunger for the “primitive” reverberated through the next generation, feeding directly into the art of Matisse and the Fauves and the breakthroughs of Picasso.

The painter he revered and collected above all others, whose structured way of seeing would underpin all of modern art, was Paul Cézanne.
