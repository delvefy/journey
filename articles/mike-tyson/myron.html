Myron

Greek sculptor (5th century BC)

Myron of Eleutherae was the sculptor who taught bronze to move. Working in Athens in the middle of the fifth century, in the same generation as Phidias and Polyclitus and, by tradition, under the same Argive master, he turned from the frozen poise of earlier statues to the capture of action at its sharpest instant.

His masterpiece, the Discobolus, shows an athlete coiled at the very moment before he unwinds to hurl the discus—body twisted, arm flung back, every muscle gathered in a spring of stored energy, yet the face serenely calm. In it the Greeks felt that a sculptor had at last seized the fleeting movement of a living body and fixed it forever in metal.

He was equally admired for a bronze group of Athena and the satyr Marsyas, and, most famously of all in antiquity, for a bronze cow set up on the Athenian Acropolis, so lifelike that poets wrote dozens of teasing epigrams pretending that calves and bulls and even thieves were deceived by it.

Like the other masters of his age his bronzes are lost, known only through Roman marble copies and the praises of later writers. While Myron and his fellow sculptors gave the age its statues, the supreme master of the companion art of painting, who filled the public halls of Athens with crowded, noble murals, was Polygnotus.
