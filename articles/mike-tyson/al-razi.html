Al-Razi

Persian physician and polymath, "Rhazes" (c. 854–925)

Al-Razi, known to the Latin West as Rhazes, was born around 854 in the Persian city of Ray, near modern Tehran, and became one of the greatest physicians of the medieval world. A polymath of restless curiosity, he wrote on medicine, alchemy, philosophy and much else, and directed hospitals in Ray and Baghdad, where he was famous for his clinical skill and his compassion for the poor.

His medical writings were monumental. His vast encyclopedia, the Kitab al-Hawi—known in Latin as the Liber Continens—gathered the whole of Greek, Indian and Arabic medical knowledge alongside his own case notes, and remained a standard reference in Europe for centuries. His treatise on smallpox and measles gave the first clear clinical description distinguishing the two diseases, a landmark of careful observation.

Al-Razi was an empiricist who trusted experience over authority. Steeped in the Greek medicine that Hunayn ibn Ishaq's generation had translated, he revered Galen yet dared to challenge him, writing a bold work titled Doubts about Galen. In alchemy he was equally practical, classifying substances and describing chemical apparatus and processes with a precision that anticipated later chemistry.

He was also a freethinking philosopher whose rationalist and unorthodox views on religion scandalised many. In his last years he went blind, reportedly refusing treatment, and he died around 925. Together with Avicenna, who followed a few generations later, al-Razi stands as one of the two supreme physicians of the Islamic golden age, and his works, carried into Latin, helped shape European medicine down to the Renaissance.
