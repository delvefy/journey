Daniel Webster

American statesman and orator (1782–1852)

Daniel Webster was the greatest orator of antebellum America and one of its foremost defenders of the Union. Born in New Hampshire and risen as a brilliant lawyer, he argued landmark cases before the Supreme Court—the Dartmouth College case, McCulloch v. Maryland, and Gibbons v. Ogden—that strengthened contract, the national bank, and federal power over commerce.

In the Senate he became the voice of American nationalism. His Second Reply to Hayne in 1830, a magnificent answer to the doctrine that states might nullify federal law, closed with words schoolchildren would memorise for generations: “Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable.” The speech made him the conscience of the Union and prefigured the cause for which Abraham Lincoln would later fight.

A leader of the Whig Party and twice Secretary of State—he settled a tense border dispute with Britain in the Webster-Ashburton Treaty—he often clashed with Andrew Jackson over the national bank even as the two stood together against nullification. With Henry Clay he formed two-thirds of the Senate's celebrated Great Triumvirate.

His support of the Compromise of 1850, including its harsh Fugitive Slave Law, cost him dearly among antislavery northerners and shadowed his final years. Like Clay, he died in 1852, his presidential ambitions never fulfilled. The third member of that famous triumvirate, and his great philosophical adversary, was John C. Calhoun.
