Callimachus

Hellenistic poet and scholar of Alexandria (c. 310–240 BC)

Callimachus of Cyrene was the presiding genius of literary Alexandria, poet and scholar at the great Library under the Ptolemies. As a scholar he compiled the Pinakes, a vast catalogue of the authors and works held in the Library—in effect the first systematic bibliography, and a foundation of all later literary scholarship.

As a poet he proclaimed a new ideal that would echo for centuries. Against the fashion for sprawling imitations of epic he insisted that "a big book is a big evil," prizing instead the short, the polished, the learned, and the original—the slender, exquisitely crafted poem. His Aetia, an elegiac collection on the origins of rites and names modelled on Hesiod, his Hymns, his Iambi, and his sparkling epigrams embodied that creed; one of those epigrams praised the wakeful art of Aratus.

His doctrine provoked one of the most famous literary feuds of antiquity, with his own pupil Apollonius of Rhodes, who defied the master by composing a full-length epic on the voyage of the Argonauts.

Callimachus's influence on Roman poetry was immense. His aesthetic of refinement and allusion guided Virgil and Ovid and inspired the younger Roman poets who styled themselves his heirs—none more devotedly than Catullus, who turned his "Lock of Berenice" into Latin and made the Callimachean manner the height of fashion at Rome. Against the grand old epic of Homer, Callimachus had set a new and lasting measure of poetic art.
