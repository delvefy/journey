Pausanias the Geographer

Greek traveler and writer (c. 110–180 AD)

Pausanias was a Greek of the Roman age who set himself the task of describing his ancestral homeland in patient, methodical detail. The result, his Description of Greece, is a ten-book guide that leads the reader region by region through the temples, statues, tombs, and sacred groves of the old country, recording at every step the local legends and histories attached to them.

He wrote as an antiquarian in love with the classical past, lingering over ancient shrines and works of art while passing lightly over the Roman present. For the history of art his book is priceless: it preserves the only full ancient account of the colossal gold-and-ivory Zeus that Phidias made for Olympia, and describes countless other masterpieces long since lost.

So precise were his directions that modern archaeologists have used them as a map, digging where he said a statue or temple stood and finding it still there. His pages helped recover Olympia and Delphi from the earth, and remain a chief witness to a vanished world.

He traveled and wrote under the emperors of the Antonine peace, noting with approval the benefactions that Hadrian had lavished upon Greece. Among the sculptors whose works he catalogued with a connoisseur's care—rival in fame to Phidias himself, and famous for fixing the ideal proportions of the human form—was Polyclitus.
