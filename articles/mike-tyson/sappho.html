Sappho

Archaic Greek lyric poet (c. 630–570 BC)

Sappho of Lesbos was the most celebrated woman poet of the ancient world, so admired that Plato is said to have hailed her as the tenth Muse. Writing in the soft Aeolic dialect of her island around 600 BC, she composed intimate songs to be sung to the lyre, in a verse form so distinctive that one of its meters still bears her name. Her subject was, above all, the human heart—desire, longing, jealousy, and tenderness—rendered with a directness and emotional truth unmatched in Greek poetry.

Only fragments of her work survive, recovered from quotations and tattered scraps of papyrus, yet even these have an astonishing power: the bittersweet prayer to Aphrodite, and the breathless description of a lover trembling and burning at the sight of the beloved. She drew on the heroic world of Homer only to overturn its values, declaring that the fairest thing on earth is not armies or fleets but whatever one loves.

Much of her life is lost in legend. Tradition gave her a husband, a daughter, and a wayward brother, an exile to Sicily, and a despairing leap to her death from a cliff for love of the ferryman Phaon—a story later retold by Ovid. From her and her island come the very words "sapphic" and "lesbian."

Her lyric art was reborn in Latin: Catullus rendered one of her odes into his own tongue, and the Aeolic measures of Sappho would be claimed as a proud conquest by the Roman poet Horace.
