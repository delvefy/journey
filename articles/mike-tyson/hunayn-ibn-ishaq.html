Hunayn ibn Ishaq

Arab translator and physician (809–873)

Hunayn ibn Ishaq, born in 809, was the greatest translator of the Islamic golden age and a distinguished physician—an Arab Christian of the Church of the East whose work transmitted the science and philosophy of ancient Greece into the Arabic-speaking world. Working in Baghdad under the patronage of the caliph al-Ma'mun and his successors, he led the translation effort at the House of Wisdom and was honoured as the "sheikh of the translators."

His output was prodigious and meticulous. Where earlier translators had rendered Greek crudely, Hunayn perfected the craft, hunting down the best manuscripts, comparing versions, and producing clear, accurate Arabic and Syriac texts. He devoted himself above all to medicine, translating around a hundred and thirty works of Galen, as well as Hippocrates, and so making the entire corpus of Greek medicine available to Islamic physicians. He and his school also translated Aristotle, Plato and Ptolemy, carrying philosophy and astronomy along with healing.

Hunayn was a learned physician in his own right, serving caliphs at court—a perilous post, for he once fell from favour and was imprisoned—and writing original works, including an influential treatise on the eye that founded the Arabic tradition of ophthalmology.

He died in 873, leaving a body of translations that became the foundation of Arabic science and medicine for centuries. The great physicians who followed, from al-Razi to Avicenna, built upon the Greek learning he had preserved; and when those Arabic texts were later rendered into Latin, Hunayn's labours reached medieval Europe as well. Few scholars have done more to carry the knowledge of one civilisation into another.
