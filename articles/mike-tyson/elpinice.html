Elpinice

Athenian noblewoman (5th century BC)

Elpinice was one of the few women of classical Athens whose name and words survive, and she owed that rare visibility to her birth, her boldness, and the scandal that clung to her. She was the daughter of Miltiades, the victor of Marathon, and the elder sister of Cimon, and when their father died in disgrace and debt, brother and sister were left to face the city's hostility together.

So close were the two that gossip whispered of an improper bond between them, and it was said that Elpinice was at last married off to Callias, the wealthiest man in Athens, on the understanding that he would discharge the family's crushing debt. Her name was linked, too, with the great painter Polygnotus, who is reported to have set her likeness among the captive Trojan women in his murals at Delphi.

She did not keep the silence expected of Athenian wives. When her brother Cimon was put on trial and Pericles stood among his accusers, Elpinice went in person to plead for mercy, and Pericles softened. Years later, when he returned in triumph from crushing the revolt of Samos and was being praised to the skies, she cut through the flattery to ask bitterly whether it was so fine a thing to have lost the city so many of its sons.

Her sharp tongue and her lineage made her a figure the historians could not ignore. The immensely rich husband she married, who would later negotiate a famous peace with the Persian king, was Callias.
