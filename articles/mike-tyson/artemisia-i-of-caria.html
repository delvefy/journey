Artemisia I of Caria

Queen of Halicarnassus (5th century BC)

Artemisia I ruled the Greek city of Halicarnassus and its Carian territories as a vassal of Persia, a widowed queen governing in the name of her young son. When Xerxes I launched his great invasion of Greece in 480 BC, she alone among his subject rulers came in person at the head of her own squadron of ships, the only woman to command in the Persian fleet.

Her fame rests on the vivid portrait drawn by her fellow citizen of Halicarnassus, the historian Herodotus. He tells how, before the sea battle at Salamis, she alone had the courage to advise Xerxes against fighting the Greeks at sea—counsel he admired but overruled. In the battle itself, hard pressed and hemmed in, she rammed and sank a ship of her own side to slip free; the pursuing Athenian, supposing her an ally, turned away, while Xerxes, watching from the shore and told she had sunk an enemy, is said to have cried that his men had become women and his women men.

So galling was it to the Athenians that a woman should war against them that they set a great price on her capture, which none collected. After the defeat she counselled Xerxes to return home and leave his general Mardonius to carry on the war, and was entrusted with conveying the royal children to safety.

The bold queen of Halicarnassus was long remembered, and her city would in a later age be ruled by a far more famous house—that of Mausolus.
