J. Robert Oppenheimer

American theoretical physicist (1904–1967)

J. Robert Oppenheimer was born in New York in 1904 into a cultivated and wealthy family. After Harvard and an unhappy spell at Cambridge, he found himself at Göttingen under Max Born, where the two produced the approximation in quantum chemistry that still bears their names. Restless and widely read—he taught himself Sanskrit to read the Bhagavad Gita—he returned to build American theoretical physics at Berkeley, and did pioneering early work on the gravitational collapse of stars into what are now called black holes.

In 1942 General Leslie Groves chose him to lead the secret weapons laboratory at Los Alamos. There Oppenheimer marshaled a brilliant and fractious community of scientists and drove the design of the atomic bomb to its test in the New Mexico desert in July 1945, a flash at which he recalled the scripture's words, “Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds.”

After the war he became the most famous scientist in America and director of the Institute for Advanced Study, the home of Albert Einstein. He counseled restraint in the nuclear arms race and opposed a crash program for the hydrogen bomb.

In 1954, in the fearful climate of the McCarthy era, his enemies engineered a hearing that stripped him of his security clearance and his influence. He died, partly vindicated, in 1967. Throughout his life he revered the great Danish physicist Niels Bohr.
