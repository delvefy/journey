Bertrand Russell

British philosopher and logician (1872–1970)

Bertrand Russell was born in 1872 into one of England's great Whig aristocratic families; his grandfather, Lord John Russell, had twice been prime minister. Orphaned young and raised by a stern grandmother, he found refuge in mathematics, which he studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, and which he hoped would offer certainty in an uncertain world.

His ambition was to show that all of mathematics could be derived from logic. In 1901 he discovered the paradox that bears his name, which exposed a contradiction at the heart of set theory and shook the foundations of the subject. Undeterred, he laboured with his former teacher Alfred North Whitehead on the monumental Principia Mathematica (1910–1913), extending the logical tradition that ran back through Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz to Aristotle. His essay "On Denoting" became a landmark of analytic philosophy.

Russell was also a fearless public figure. A pacifist during the First World War, he was fined, stripped of his Cambridge fellowship, and imprisoned in 1918. He wrote prolifically for general readers—his History of Western Philosophy (1945) was a bestseller—and won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950.

In old age he became the conscience of the nuclear era, issuing the Russell–Einstein Manifesto, leading the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, and protesting the Vietnam War. He died in Wales in 1970 at the age of ninety-seven, having shaped logic, philosophy and political conscience alike.
