Phidias

Athenian sculptor (c. 480–430 BC)

Phidias of Athens is commonly judged the greatest sculptor of ancient Greece, the artist who gave the classical age its image of the gods. The friend and chosen artist of Pericles, he was made overseer of the vast building programme that crowned the Acropolis, and under his direction rose the Parthenon, with the frieze, metopes, and pediments that remain the supreme achievement of Greek sculpture.

His own masterpieces were two colossal statues of gold and ivory. Within the Parthenon he set a towering Athena, armed and serene; and at Olympia he fashioned the enthroned Zeus, more than twelve metres high, so awesome in its majesty that the Greeks counted it among the Seven Wonders of the world and said it was a misfortune to die without having seen it. In these works he fixed for all time the very faces of the Olympian gods.

His closeness to power proved dangerous. The enemies of Pericles struck at the statesman through his artist, accusing Phidias of stealing gold meant for Athena and of impiety for carving his own likeness and that of Pericles upon her shield. He was brought down and is said to have died in prison or in exile around 430 BC.

Yet his art set the standard that every later sculptor strove to reach, among them the master of the following century, Praxiteles.
