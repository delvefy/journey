James Hutton

Scottish geologist, father of modern geology (1726–1797)

James Hutton was born in Edinburgh in 1726. He studied medicine and chemistry, then made a comfortable fortune from a farm and a chemical works producing sal ammoniac, which left him free to follow his curiosity into the rocks of his native Scotland. He moved in the brilliant company of the Edinburgh Enlightenment, counting Adam Smith among his close friends.

From years of patient field observation Hutton arrived at a grand vision of the Earth as a self-renewing machine. Rocks, he argued, are worn down by erosion, their debris laid as sediment beneath the sea, consolidated by the Earth's internal heat, then raised again into new land to be eroded once more, in cycle after cycle. At sites such as Siccar Point he read in tilted and overlying strata the evidence of almost unimaginable time, declaring that he could see “no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end.”

He set out these ideas in his Theory of the Earth in 1788 and at greater length in 1795, but his tangled prose left them neglected, until later writers, and above all Charles Lyell, carried the doctrine of deep time and gradual change into the heart of geology.

Hutton's closest intellectual companion was the chemist Joseph Black, with whom he shared the discoveries of the age. He died in Edinburgh in 1797, his true stature recognized only after his death.
