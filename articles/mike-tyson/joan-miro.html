Joan Miró

Catalan painter and sculptor (1893–1983)

Joan Miró was the Catalan painter whose playful universe of floating signs, biomorphic shapes and pure bright colour made him one of the great modern artists.

Born in Barcelona and rooted all his life in the Catalan landscape, he moved to Paris in the 1920s, where his compatriot Pablo Picasso took him under his wing. There he fell in with the Surrealists gathered around André Breton, who hailed him as “the most Surrealist of us all,” though Miró kept his distance from their doctrines. Drawing on the poetic, sign-laden imagery of Paul Klee, he evolved an instantly recognizable language of suns, stars, birds and dreaming figures, set against fields of blue and yellow and red.

He spoke of wanting to “assassinate painting,” breaking its old rules, and ranged across sculpture, ceramics, murals and prints in a career of restless invention. He lived through the Spanish Civil War as a fierce opponent of Franco, and later settled in Mallorca, his work growing ever bolder and more monumental. He died there in 1983, and his influence ran on into the Abstract Expressionists.

The other great Catalan painter of the Surrealist movement—the flamboyant younger countryman whom Miró helped introduce to the Paris avant-garde—was Salvador Dalí.
