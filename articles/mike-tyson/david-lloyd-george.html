David Lloyd George

British Prime Minister, 1916–1922 (1863–1945)

David Lloyd George was the Welsh radical who laid the foundations of the British welfare state and led the United Kingdom through the final, victorious years of the First World War.

Raised in modest circumstances in north Wales and trained as a solicitor, he entered Parliament as a fiery Liberal and made his name as Chancellor of the Exchequer. His “People's Budget” of 1909 and the National Insurance Act of 1911 introduced old-age pensions and social insurance, and his battle to push them past the House of Lords curbed that chamber's power for good. He served the Crown of George V throughout, a king wary of his restless energy.

When the war ground into stalemate he displaced the cautious Asquith to become prime minister in December 1916, bringing a galvanic urgency to the war effort and pressing for a unified Allied command. Among the ministers he drove hardest was Winston Churchill. At the Paris Peace Conference he was the supple middle man of the Big Four, manoeuvring between the idealism of Woodrow Wilson and the severity of Georges Clemenceau—a settlement the economist John Maynard Keynes denounced as a recipe for ruin.

His last great act was to end the Irish war: in 1921 he negotiated the Anglo-Irish Treaty that created the Irish Free State, partitioning the island and granting the south self-government. The deal fractured his coalition, and in 1922 he fell from power, never to hold office again. The Irish leader who sat across the table from him, and who signed the treaty knowing it might cost him his life, was Michael Collins.
