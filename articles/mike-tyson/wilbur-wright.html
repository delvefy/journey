Wilbur Wright

American aviation pioneer (1867–1912)

Wilbur Wright was the elder of the two brothers who gave the world the airplane, and by many accounts the leader and deeper thinker of the pair. A bookish, reserved young man whose early plans had been upset by an injury, he turned around 1899 to the problem that had defeated every previous experimenter—how a human being might fly—reading everything he could find and drawing his brother Orville into the quest.

It was Wilbur who grasped that the central problem was not power but control, and who hit upon the idea of twisting, or "warping," the wings to balance and bank the craft. Through patient seasons of glider tests at Kitty Hawk and careful measurement in their own wind tunnel, the brothers mastered flight about all three axes, making the powered triumph of 1903 possible.

Wilbur's finest hour came in France in 1908, where his long, banking, effortlessly controlled flights at Le Mans astonished a skeptical Europe and made the brothers world-famous, hailed as the first true aviators. But the strain of demonstrations, business, and bitter patent battles wore him down, and in 1912 he died of typhoid fever at only forty-five, a grievous loss to his brother and to the new art of flight.

The brothers had been set on their path, in part, by the daring and the death of the great German glider pioneer whose example first showed that a man might ride the air—Otto Lilienthal.
