Akbar the Great

Mughal emperor (1542–1605)

Akbar, grandson of the empire's founder Babur, was the greatest of the Mughal emperors and the true architect of their power in India. He came to the throne in 1556 as a boy of thirteen, after the sudden death of his father Humayun, and over a reign of nearly fifty years he expanded a precarious inheritance into a wealthy, centralized empire spanning most of northern and central India.

A bold commander, Akbar was an even greater administrator. He overhauled the system of land taxation, organized his nobility and army under the ranked mansabdari system, and bound a vast, diverse realm into efficient government. Though himself unable to read, he gathered scholars, artists, and poets, built the splendid palace-city of Fatehpur Sikri, and had his reign chronicled by his friend and minister Abul Fazl.

His most striking legacy was a policy of tolerance rare in any age. Ruling a land of Muslims, Hindus, and many other faiths, Akbar abolished the hated jizya tax on non-Muslims, took Rajput princesses as wives and Rajput nobles as allies, sponsored debates among teachers of every religion, and even sought to fashion a syncretic faith of his own at court.

Under him the Mughal Empire entered a golden age of stability and splendor. He died in 1605 and was succeeded by his son Jahangir, beginning the line that would lead to his grandson Shah Jahan and the building of the Taj Mahal.
