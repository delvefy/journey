D. H. Lawrence

English novelist and poet (1885–1930)

David Herbert Lawrence was born in 1885 in the Nottinghamshire mining village of Eastwood, the son of a barely literate coal miner and an ambitious former schoolteacher. The clash between his rough father and his refined, possessive mother marked him for life and supplied the substance of his early autobiographical novel Sons and Lovers (1913). A sickly, clever boy, he won a scholarship, trained as a teacher, and began to write while working in a school near London.

In 1912 he eloped with Frieda Weekley, the German-born wife of one of his former professors, and the pair married after her divorce. Their restless, quarrelsome union carried them across Italy, Australia, Ceylon, Mexico and the deserts of New Mexico, where Lawrence sought societies less crushed by industrial modernity than his own.

His major novels—The Rainbow (1915), suppressed as obscene, and its sequel Women in Love (1920)—explored sexuality, instinct and the deadening effect of mechanized society with a frankness that scandalized readers. He was also a gifted poet and a sharp critic, whose Studies in Classic American Literature (1923) championed writers like Walt Whitman and Herman Melville long before they were canonical.

Dogged by tuberculosis, censorship and accusations of obscenity, he produced his most notorious book, Lady Chatterley's Lover, in 1928; it could not be published unexpurgated in Britain until a celebrated 1960 trial, thirty years after his death at Vence in southern France in 1930, aged forty-four.
