Henry II of England

First Plantagenet king of England (1133–1189)

Henry II, born in 1133, was the first king of the Plantagenet dynasty and one of the most energetic and able rulers in English history. The son of Geoffrey of Anjou and the Empress Matilda, he inherited Normandy and Anjou, married the formidable Eleanor of Aquitaine in 1152, and two years later won the English crown, ending the long civil war of his predecessor Stephen's reign. His combined lands—the Angevin Empire—stretched from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees.

A restless, brilliant administrator, Henry rebuilt royal authority after years of anarchy. He curbed the over-mighty barons, reformed the courts, and laid the foundations of the English common law, expanding the use of royal justice and trial by jury. Few medieval kings did more to shape the machinery of the state.

His reign was overshadowed by his quarrel with Thomas Becket, his former chancellor and friend whom he raised to Archbishop of Canterbury. Becket defended the Church's independence against the king's claims, and the bitter feud ended in 1170 when four of Henry's knights, taking his angry words as a command, murdered the archbishop in Canterbury Cathedral. The crime shocked Christendom; Becket was swiftly made a saint, and Henry did public penance at his tomb.

Henry's last years were embittered by his own family. His sons, encouraged by their mother Eleanor, rose against him repeatedly. He kept Eleanor imprisoned for years, but the rebellions never ceased. He died in 1189, heartbroken at the treachery of his sons, and was succeeded by Richard the Lionheart. His empire fractured, but his legal reforms long outlived him.
