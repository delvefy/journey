Erasmus Darwin

English physician, poet, and natural philosopher (1731–1802)

Erasmus Darwin was born in Nottinghamshire in 1731 and educated at Cambridge and Edinburgh before settling at Lichfield, where he built a celebrated medical practice. So great was his reputation that George III invited him to be royal physician, an honor he declined, preferring his provincial life and wide-ranging interests.

A man of immense curiosity, Darwin was a founder of the Lunar Society of Birmingham, the circle of inventors and thinkers that included James Watt and Joseph Priestley, and he corresponded with Benjamin Franklin. He sketched designs for carriages, engines, and speaking machines, and championed canals and the new industry.

In his medical and biological treatise Zoonomia he advanced bold ideas about the common descent and gradual transformation of living things, anticipating in outline the evolutionary theory his grandson Charles Darwin would later establish on firm ground. He was also a popular poet of science: his long verses The Botanic Garden and The Loves of the Plants set out for English readers the sexual system of classification devised by Carl Linnaeus, whose works he had helped translate.

Darwin founded a remarkable dynasty, grandfather both of Charles Darwin and of the statistician Francis Galton. He died in 1802, remembered as one of the most original minds of the English Enlightenment and a forerunner of the science of life.
