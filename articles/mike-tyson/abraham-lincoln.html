Abraham Lincoln

16th President of the United States (1809–1865)

Abraham Lincoln, born in a Kentucky log cabin in 1809 and almost wholly self-taught, rose from frontier poverty to become the president who preserved the American Union and ended slavery within it. A country lawyer and politician in Illinois, he gained national fame in his 1858 debates against Stephen Douglas, where he argued that a "house divided against itself cannot stand" and that the spread of slavery must be checked.

His election in 1860, as the first president of the new Republican Party, prompted the Southern states to secede and plunged the nation into the Civil War. For four terrible years Lincoln held the Union to its purpose with a mixture of patience, political genius, and moral resolve. In 1863 the Emancipation Proclamation turned the war into a crusade against slavery, and at Gettysburg he distilled the meaning of the struggle into a few immortal sentences, recalling the nation to the promise that "all men are created equal" and to a government "of the people, by the people, for the people."

Through dark years of defeat he searched for a general who could win, and found him at last in Ulysses S. Grant—"I cannot spare this man; he fights." Victory and the abolition of slavery came together in the spring of 1865, only for Lincoln to be shot days later at Ford's Theatre, dying a martyr to the cause he had served.

The moral charter of his life's work was the Declaration of Independence of Thomas Jefferson, and his memory was revered by later presidents such as Theodore Roosevelt. The soldier who delivered him victory was Ulysses S. Grant.
