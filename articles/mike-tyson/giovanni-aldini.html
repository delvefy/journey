Giovanni Aldini

Italian physicist (1762–1834)

Giovanni Aldini was born in Bologna in 1762, a nephew of Luigi Galvani, and became professor of physics in the same city. He made it his mission to defend and extend his uncle's theory of animal electricity against the rival claims of Alessandro Volta, and to do so he carried galvanism out of the laboratory and onto the public stage.

Travelling through Europe, Aldini applied powerful electric currents to the freshly killed bodies of animals and of executed criminals, making their muscles contract as if with life. His most notorious demonstration came in London in 1803, when he electrified the corpse of the hanged murderer George Forster before the Royal College of Surgeons; the dead man's jaw quivered, an eye opened, and a fist clenched, to the astonishment and horror of the crowd.

Such spectacles spread the fame of galvanism and seized the public imagination, contributing to the atmosphere of dread and possibility from which Mary Shelley would draw her novel Frankenstein. Aldini insisted his aim was serious: he pioneered the use of electric shocks to treat mental illness, an ancestor of later electrotherapy.

In his later years he turned to practical concerns, promoting fireproofing with asbestos and improvements in lighthouse lighting, and was honored by Napoleon and the Austrian emperor. He died in 1834. Others, among them Andrew Ure, would repeat his electrical experiments on the dead.
