John Calvin

French reformer of Geneva (1509–1564)

John Calvin was, after Martin Luther, the second great founder of Protestantism and the most systematic mind of the Reformation. Born in Noyon in northern France and trained in law and humanist learning, he embraced the reformed faith as a young man and was forced to flee his Catholic homeland. In exile he published, at the age of twenty-six, the Institutes of the Christian Religion, a work of such clarity and force that it became the foundation of Reformed theology, expounding the absolute sovereignty of God and the doctrine of predestination.

Calvin found his life's work in Geneva, which he labored to remake into a model Christian commonwealth—a disciplined, godly city that admirers called a "Protestant Rome" and enemies a grim theocracy. He preached tirelessly, organized its church, and founded an academy that drew students from across Europe and sent them home as reformers.

His influence radiated outward to the Huguenots of France, the Dutch rebels, the Puritans of England, and the Presbyterians of Scotland, shaping churches and even nations for centuries. Yet his Geneva could be merciless: the burning of the heretic Michael Servetus in 1553 left a lasting shadow on his name.

Austere, brilliant, and indomitable, Calvin died in 1564, having given the international Protestant movement its theology and its discipline. Among the many who came to Geneva to sit at his feet, the fiery preacher who carried his teaching home and made an entire kingdom Calvinist was the Scotsman John Knox.
