Walter Raleigh

English courtier, explorer, and writer (c. 1552–1618)

Walter Raleigh was the dazzling embodiment of the Elizabethan age—soldier, sea-adventurer, courtier, poet, and historian all at once. A Devon man who first won fame fighting in Ireland, he rose to become a favorite of Elizabeth I, showered with knighthood, estates, and lucrative monopolies. Legend, perhaps invented, has him laying his cloak across a puddle so the queen need not wet her feet.

His restless ambition turned to the New World. Raleigh sponsored the first English attempts to plant a colony in North America, at Roanoke in the land he named Virginia for his virgin queen—ventures that ended in failure and mystery. He helped make tobacco fashionable in England and dreamed of the fabled golden kingdom of El Dorado, leading expeditions up the rivers of Guiana in search of it.

A man of letters as much as action, Raleigh wrote poetry and moved among the freethinking intellectuals of his day. But his fortunes turned with his sovereigns. A secret marriage to one of the queen's maids of honor cost him Elizabeth's favor, and the accession of James I proved fatal. Distrusted by the new king, he was imprisoned in the Tower for thirteen years, during which he composed his ambitious History of the World.

Freed for one last doomed search for El Dorado, he returned empty-handed and was executed in 1618 to appease the Spanish. Among the bold mariners of Elizabeth's reign with whom Raleigh shared the dream of plunder and discovery, the most celebrated was Francis Drake.
