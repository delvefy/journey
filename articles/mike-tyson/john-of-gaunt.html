John of Gaunt

Duke of Lancaster (1340–1399)

John of Gaunt was the richest and most powerful nobleman of fourteenth-century England, a prince who never wore a crown yet stood at the center of the realm for thirty years. The third surviving son of Edward III, he took his name from his birthplace, Ghent, and his vast power from his marriage to the heiress of Lancaster, which made him a duke and the master of estates spread across the kingdom.

A seasoned commander in the Hundred Years' War with France, he also pursued, through a second marriage, a claim to the throne of Castile. As his father Edward III sank into senility and the crown passed to the boy-king Richard II, his nephew, John of Gaunt became the dominant figure in English government—respected, feared, and often resented by commons and nobles alike.

He used his power to shield others. He gave lasting protection to the reforming theologian John Wycliffe, and he was the patron and kinsman of England's first great poet. His descendants would shape the nation's destiny: his son Henry Bolingbroke seized the throne as Henry IV, founding the House of Lancaster, while his legitimated Beaufort line would in time produce the Tudors.

Shakespeare would later put into his dying mouth the famous praise of England as "this sceptred isle." Among the men who flourished under his patronage was the poet who first made the English tongue a vehicle for great literature—Geoffrey Chaucer.
