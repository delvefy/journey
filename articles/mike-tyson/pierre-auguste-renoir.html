Pierre-Auguste Renoir

French Impressionist painter (1841–1919)

Pierre-Auguste Renoir was the great sensualist of Impressionism, a painter of warmth, gaiety, and the human figure bathed in dappled light. Born at Limoges, he began as a decorator of porcelain, a trade that left him with a lifelong love of luminous, glowing colour. In Charles Gleyre's studio he fell in with Claude Monet, Alfred Sisley, and Frédéric Bazille, and with Monet he worked out the bright, broken brushwork of the new style at the river resort of La Grenouillère.

His Paris is sunlit and convivial: the open-air dance of the Moulin de la Galette and the relaxed gathering of the Luncheon of the Boating Party brim with friends, food, and flirtation. Yet Renoir grew restless with Impressionism's dissolving forms.

A journey to Italy in 1881 changed him. Stirred by the frescoes of Raphael and the antique paintings of Pompeii, he turned toward firmer drawing and a more classical line, then softened again into the opulent, rose-fleshed nudes of his later years, openly in the lineage of Peter Paul Rubens. Crippled by arthritis, he painted on in the south of France with brushes bound to his stiffened hands.

By his death in 1919 he was revered by the moderns who followed—Henri Matisse, who visited him in his last years, and Pablo Picasso, who collected and learned from him—while his son Jean Renoir would carry the family genius into the cinema. Among his Impressionist comrades, none was a sharper, more complex contemporary than Edgar Degas.
