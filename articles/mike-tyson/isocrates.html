Isocrates

Athenian rhetorician and educator (436–338 BC)

Isocrates was the most influential teacher of rhetoric in classical Athens and, in a sense, a founder of the Western tradition of liberal education. Too shy and weak-voiced to speak in the assembly himself, he worked instead through the written word and through his pupils. Having studied under the great sophist Gorgias, he opened around 392 BC a school that rivalled the Academy of Plato and trained a generation of statesmen and orators in the art of eloquent, practical wisdom.

He held that the proper aim of education was not the abstract philosophy of Socrates and his followers but the cultivation of sound judgement and persuasive speech in the service of the city. His own polished, balanced prose became a model of style for antiquity, and through admirers such as Cicero his ideal of the cultivated orator-citizen shaped Roman and, later, Renaissance schooling.

Isocrates is remembered above all as the prophet of Greek unity. In his Panegyricus and in his open letter To Philip he pleaded with the feuding city-states to lay aside their quarrels and turn their swords against the Persian empire, looking finally to Philip II of Macedon as the leader who might unite Greece in that cause.

He lived to the great age of ninety-eight, and tradition holds that he died of grief and self-starvation in the days after Chaeronea, when Philip's victory extinguished the old freedom of the Greek cities. His teacher, the dazzling and controversial Gorgias, had been among the first to make rhetoric an art.
