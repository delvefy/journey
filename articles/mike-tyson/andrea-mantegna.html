Andrea Mantegna

Early Renaissance painter and engraver (c. 1431–1506)

Andrea Mantegna was the great archaeologist of Renaissance painting, who filled his pictures with the hard grandeur of ancient Rome and pioneered the bold illusions of perspective.

Born near Padua, he trained in the workshop of Francesco Squarcione, who steeped his pupils in fragments of classical antiquity. The decisive influence on the young painter, however, was the sculptor Donatello, then working in Padua, whose chiselled, monumental figures taught Mantegna to paint as if carving stone. From the first his art was severe, precise and saturated with antiquarian learning.

In 1453 he married into the most famous artistic family of Venice, and the connection drew him into fertile exchange with its painters. From 1460 he served as court painter to the Gonzaga rulers of Mantua for nearly half a century. There he created the Camera degli Sposi, whose ceiling opens in a painted oculus to a sky crowded with figures peering down—the first illusionistic ceiling of its kind—and the wrenching Lamentation over the Dead Christ, with its body violently foreshortened toward the viewer.

His crisp, linear engravings carried his manner across the Alps, where the young Albrecht Dürer copied them and absorbed their lessons into the art of the North. Mantegna died in Mantua in 1506, having shaped the visual language of generations, the distant master who would later mould even Correggio. The Venetian brother-in-law with whom he traded so much, and who would father the whole Venetian school, was Giovanni Bellini.
