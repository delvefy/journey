Titian

Venetian Renaissance painter (c. 1488–1576)

Titian—Tiziano Vecellio—was the supreme painter of the Venetian Renaissance and one of the most influential artists who ever lived. Born in the mountain town of Pieve di Cadore, he trained in Venice in the workshop of Giovanni Bellini and worked in his youth alongside the brilliant, short-lived Giorgione. From them he learned the Venetian art of color and light, which he would carry to heights no one had reached.

Over a career of more than sixty years he transformed nearly every kind of painting. His soaring Assumption of the Virgin made his name; his mythologies such as Bacchus and Ariadne and the sensuous Venus of Urbino set the standard for the genre; and his portraits laid bare the souls of popes, princes, and emperors. Above all he revolutionized the very handling of oil paint, building his images from loose, glowing, broken strokes that later masters would study forever.

His fame carried him to the summit of European patronage. The Holy Roman Emperor Charles V made him a court painter and a count, and—so the story goes—once stooped to retrieve a brush the great man had dropped. For Charles he painted the majestic equestrian portrait after the victory at Mühlberg.

In old age his brushwork grew ever freer and more emotional, culminating in the tragic Pietà left unfinished at his death in plague-stricken Venice in 1576. The royal patron of his later years, for whom he created his most ravishing mythological "poesie," was Charles's son and heir, Philip II of Spain.
