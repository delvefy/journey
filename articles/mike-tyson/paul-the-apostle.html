Paul the Apostle

Apostle and founder of Christian theology (c. 5–c. 65 AD)

Paul of Tarsus, born around 5 AD, was the most important figure in the spread of early Christianity after Jesus himself. A Jew and a Roman citizen, trained as a strict Pharisee, he began as a fierce persecutor of the followers of Jesus. Then, on the road to Damascus, he experienced a blinding vision of the risen Christ that turned the persecutor into the faith's most tireless apostle.

Over some three decades Paul crisscrossed the eastern Mediterranean on a series of missionary journeys, preaching in the cities of Asia Minor and Greece and founding or nurturing congregations at Corinth, Ephesus, Philippi, Thessalonica, and beyond. His decisive conviction was that the message of Jesus was for all humanity, not Jews alone, and that Gentile converts need not first adopt the full Jewish law—a stand that brought him into sharp dispute with Peter and the Jerusalem leadership but that made Christianity a universal religion.

The letters he wrote to these churches—Romans, the Corinthian and Galatian epistles, and others—became a large portion of the New Testament and the foundation of Christian theology, with their teaching on grace, faith, and the meaning of Christ's death and resurrection.

Arrested and exercising his right as a citizen to appeal to Caesar, Paul was taken to Rome, where tradition holds he was martyred under Nero. His thought would echo down the centuries, shaping Augustine and, fourteen hundred years later, igniting the Reformation of Martin Luther.
