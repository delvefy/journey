Socrates

Greek philosopher (c. 470–399 BC)

Socrates, born around 470 BC in Athens, was the philosopher whose life and death set the course of Western thought. The son of a stonemason and a midwife, he served bravely as a hoplite in the Peloponnesian War before devoting himself to a life of questioning. Indifferent to wealth and comfort, he spent his days in the marketplace of Athens, engaging citizens of every kind in conversation.

His method—persistent questioning to expose contradictions and false certainties—became known as the Socratic method, or elenchus. Professing that he himself knew nothing, he sought to define virtue, justice, and the good, and insisted that the care of the soul mattered above all else. When the oracle at Delphi declared no man wiser than Socrates, he concluded that his only wisdom lay in knowing the extent of his own ignorance.

Socrates wrote nothing; he is known through the dialogues of his pupil Plato, the memoirs of Xenophon, and the mocking caricature in Aristophanes' comedy The Clouds. His relentless scrutiny of Athenian leaders and his association with controversial figures earned him powerful enemies.

In 399 BC he was tried on charges of impiety and corrupting the youth of Athens. Refusing to flatter the jury or abandon his principles, he was condemned to death. He declined the chance to escape and drank the hemlock with serene composure, discoursing on the immortality of the soul to the last. His death made him the enduring symbol of the philosopher martyred for free inquiry.
