Albert Camus

French-Algerian writer and philosopher (1913–1960)

Albert Camus was the French-Algerian novelist and philosopher of the absurd, whose lucid, sun-struck prose and moral courage won him the Nobel Prize at forty-three.

Born into deep poverty in French Algeria, raised by a deaf, illiterate mother after his father died in the First World War, he escaped through education until tuberculosis cut short his ambitions. He became a journalist and, during the Second World War, edited the clandestine Resistance newspaper Combat. From these years came his great “cycle of the absurd”: the cool, shocking novel The Stranger and the essay The Myth of Sisyphus, which confronted a meaningless universe and concluded that one must imagine Sisyphus happy.

His thought drew on the tragic vision of Friedrich Nietzsche and the spiritual torment of Fyodor Dostoevsky, whose Demons he adapted for the stage. With The Plague and the long essay The Rebel he turned to the theme of revolt—and his condemnation of revolutionary terror and Soviet communism shattered his friendship with Jean-Paul Sartre in a famous public feud.

Awarded the Nobel Prize in 1957, he died three years later in a car crash, an unfinished novel in his bag, only forty-six years old. In The Myth of Sisyphus he had devoted a searching final meditation to another writer who had made the absurd his native country—the Prague author of nightmarish, faceless bureaucracies, Franz Kafka.
