Lorenzo Valla

Italian humanist and philologist (c. 1407–1457)

Lorenzo Valla was the sharpest critical mind of the early Renaissance, a scholar who turned the new mastery of ancient Latin into a weapon against error and forgery. Serving as secretary to King Alfonso of Naples, he enjoyed the protection of a powerful patron locked in his own quarrels with Rome—a circumstance that emboldened Valla's boldest stroke.

In 1440 he wrote a treatise demonstrating that the Donation of Constantine, the document by which the medieval Church claimed its vast temporal authority, was a forgery. He proved it not by theology but by language, showing that its Latin and its references belonged to a far later age than Constantine's—a triumph of historical criticism that shook one of the foundations of papal power.

His other works were scarcely less influential. His Elegances of the Latin Language taught Europe to write classical Latin again, and his annotations on the New Testament, comparing the Latin Vulgate against the Greek, pioneered the textual study of Scripture. He waged ferocious literary feuds, none more venomous than with his fellow humanist Poggio.

Though he flirted with heresy and the Inquisition's notice, he was at last reconciled with Rome and ended his days as a papal secretary. His method outlived his quarrels: Erasmus would publish his biblical annotations and build upon them, and Reformers like Martin Luther would seize on his exposure of the Donation. His great rival in the republic of letters, the book-hunter who recovered lost classics from forgotten libraries, was Poggio Bracciolini.
