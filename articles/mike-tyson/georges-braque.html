Georges Braque

French painter, co-founder of Cubism (1882–1963)

Georges Braque was the French painter who, with Picasso, invented Cubism, one of the most revolutionary movements in the history of art.

Trained first as a house decorator like his father, he came to painting and began as one of the Fauves, working in the hot colours of Henri Matisse's circle. But it was the example of Paul Cézanne—his analysis of nature into solid geometric forms—that redirected him entirely. His severe landscapes of 1908, reduced to little cubes, gave the new movement its very name.

There followed the most famous artistic partnership of the century. For some six years Braque and Pablo Picasso worked side by side—“roped together like mountaineers,” as Braque put it—fracturing objects into shifting planes and, in Braque's hands, introducing collage and stencilled lettering into serious painting. Their canvases of these years are often hard to tell apart. Their work was tirelessly promoted by collectors like Gertrude Stein, who hung it on her famous walls.

The Great War interrupted everything; Braque was gravely wounded in the head and, on recovering, developed a quieter, more lyrical art of still lifes and birds that occupied his long later career. He died in 1963, honoured as a master. The poet who, more than any other writer, explained and celebrated the Cubist revolution to the world was Guillaume Apollinaire.
