Patrick Pearse

Irish teacher, poet and leader of the 1916 Rising (1879–1916)

Patrick Pearse was the schoolmaster and poet whose proclamation of an Irish Republic in 1916, and his execution that followed, transformed him into the foremost martyr of Irish nationalism.

Born in Dublin to an English stonemason and an Irish mother, he trained as a barrister but gave his heart to the Irish language, editing the journal of the Gaelic League and founding St Enda's, a bilingual school meant to raise a new generation in the old Gaelic spirit. A gifted orator, he electrified nationalist Ireland with his graveside oration at the funeral of the old Fenian O'Donovan Rossa, warning that a nation which kept its patriot dead could never be at peace.

Convinced that a blood sacrifice was needed to reawaken the national spirit, he joined the secret military council that planned an insurrection. On Easter Monday 1916 he stood before the General Post Office as commander-in-chief and president of the provisional government and read aloud the Proclamation of the Irish Republic. Among the volunteers who fought that week were the young Michael Collins, in the GPO itself, and Éamon de Valera at his outlying post.

The Rising was crushed within a week, and Pearse surrendered to spare the city further destruction. Court-martialled and shot by firing squad in May 1916, he and his fellow leaders were mourned in W. B. Yeats's great poem “Easter, 1916,” which declared that “a terrible beauty is born.” The executions turned a sceptical public toward the cause for which he died. His comrade at the head of the Rising, the socialist who commanded beside him in the Post Office, was James Connolly.
