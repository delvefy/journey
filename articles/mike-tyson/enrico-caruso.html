Enrico Caruso

Italian operatic tenor (1873–1921)

Enrico Caruso was the most celebrated tenor in the history of opera and the first true superstar of recorded sound. Born into poverty in Naples, he rose through the Italian theatres on the strength of a voice of unusual warmth, power, and baritonal richness, and by the early years of the twentieth century commanded the world's great stages.

His artistic home became the Metropolitan Opera in New York, where across some eighteen seasons he sang hundreds of performances and made the house the centre of operatic life. His repertoire ranged across the Italian masters—the Duke in Verdi's Rigoletto, the doomed clown of Leoncavallo's Pagliacci with its sob-choked “Vesti la giubba”—and he created roles for the living composers of his day.

Above all, Caruso married his art to a new machine. His early gramophone recordings sold in numbers no musician had ever approached, carrying his voice into ordinary homes and making both the singer and the phonograph famous together. He was performing in San Francisco when the great earthquake struck in 1906, and fled the city vowing never to return.

Worn down by illness, he died in Naples in 1921, only forty-eight. He had created a leading role for Giacomo Puccini and sung the heart of Giuseppe Verdi's tenor repertory; and his recorded legacy set the standard for every tenor who followed, none more devotedly than Luciano Pavarotti.
