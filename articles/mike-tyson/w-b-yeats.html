W. B. Yeats

Irish poet, Nobel laureate (1865–1939)

William Butler Yeats, born near Dublin in 1865, was the towering figure of the Irish Literary Revival and one of the greatest poets of the twentieth century. Steeped from childhood in Irish myth, folklore and the western landscape of Sligo, he made the legends and politics of his nation into high art, and his long career bridged the dreamy lyricism of the late Victorian age and the hard, modern style of his maturity.

In his youth he was drawn to the symbolist movement and to mysticism, joining occult societies and pursuing a lifelong fascination with magic and the supernatural. He was also consumed by an unrequited passion for the revolutionary beauty Maud Gonne, who inspired some of his finest love poetry and repeatedly refused him.

Yeats was a maker of institutions as well as poems. With Lady Gregory and others he founded the Abbey Theatre in Dublin, the national theatre of Ireland, nurturing playwrights such as J. M. Synge. He championed younger and foreign writers too, famously introducing the poems of Rabindranath Tagore to the West. After Irish independence he served as a senator of the new Free State.

His greatest poetry came late. Works such as "Easter, 1916," "The Second Coming," and "Sailing to Byzantium" confront revolution, ageing and the disintegration of the modern world in language of stark grandeur—"Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold." Awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1923, he wrote powerfully to the end. Yeats died in France in 1939, beneath the epitaph he composed: "Cast a cold eye / On life, on death. / Horseman, pass by!" He remains the national poet of Ireland and a colossus of modern verse.
