Harun al-Rashid

Abbasid caliph of the Islamic golden age (c. 763–809)

Harun al-Rashid, born around 763, was the fifth and most famous of the Abbasid caliphs, whose reign from 786 to 809 is remembered as the golden age of the Islamic empire centred on Baghdad. Under his rule the caliphate reached the height of its wealth, power and cultural brilliance, and his court became a byword for splendour.

Baghdad, the great round city founded by his grandfather al-Mansur, flourished as the richest metropolis of the age, a hub of trade stretching from Spain to India and China. Harun and his court patronised poets, musicians and scholars, and laid the foundations of the translation movement and the famed House of Wisdom that his son al-Ma'mun would carry to its peak, drawing the learning of Greece, Persia and India into Arabic.

His reign was not without darkness. He governed at first through the brilliant Persian family of viziers, the Barmakids, but in 803 he abruptly destroyed them, executing or imprisoning the men who had served him so well. He also waged repeated war against the Byzantine Empire and exchanged embassies and gifts with the Frankish emperor Charlemagne, to whom he reputedly sent an elephant and an intricate water clock.

Above all, Harun al-Rashid became a legend. He appears as a central figure in the One Thousand and One Nights, the great cycle of Arabian tales, where he is imagined wandering the streets of Baghdad in disguise to learn the lives of his people. He died in 809 on campaign in the east, and his death was soon followed by civil war among his sons—but his name has endured as the very emblem of Islam's classical golden age.
