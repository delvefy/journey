Lysippos

Greek sculptor of Sicyon (4th century BC)

Lysippos of Sicyon was the last of the three supreme sculptors of late classical Greece and the bridge to the Hellenistic age. Working always in bronze, he was astonishingly prolific—the ancients credited him with some fifteen hundred statues—and he transformed the proportions of the human figure, making his men taller and leaner, with smaller heads, so that they seemed to move and breathe. Where an earlier master had fixed men "as they were," Lysippos, he said, made them "as they appear to be."

His fame was sealed by the greatest patron of the age. Alexander the Great so admired him that he decreed Lysippos alone should sculpt his likeness, just as Apelles alone should paint it. From his hand came the official image of the conqueror—the tilted head, the upward gaze, the tumbling lion's mane of hair—that fixed Alexander's features for posterity.

Among his celebrated works were the Apoxyomenos, an athlete scraping the dust from his arm; a weary, slumping Heracles leaning on his club; and a figure of Opportunity, swift and balanced on a razor's edge. His pupil Chares of Lindos would raise the towering Colossus of Rhodes, yet another of the world's Wonders.

Like the sculptors before him, Lysippos is known chiefly through Roman copies and ancient praise. His counterpart at Alexander's court, the painter whom the king alone allowed to portray him, was Apelles.
