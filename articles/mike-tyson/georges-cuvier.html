Georges Cuvier

French naturalist, founder of comparative anatomy and paleontology (1769–1832)

Georges Cuvier was born at Montbéliard in 1769 and rose by sheer ability to the Museum of Natural History in Paris, where he became the dominant naturalist of his generation. He built the science of comparative anatomy on the principle that an animal's body is an integrated whole, so that from a single bone a skilled anatomist could reconstruct the entire creature, a method he turned on the great Aristotle's ancient study of animals to make rigorous.

Applying it to fossils, he founded vertebrate paleontology. By comparing the bones of mammoths and other lost creatures with living kinds, he proved that whole species had truly become extinct, a notion many had resisted. To explain the changing record of life he proposed catastrophism: a series of sudden revolutions of the Earth's surface, between which species remained fixed. This conviction set him firmly against the transmutation theories of his colleague and rival Lamarck.

A formidable administrator as well as a scientist, Cuvier served Napoleon and the regimes that followed as a councillor of state and a reformer of public education, and as permanent secretary of the Academy of Sciences. The naturalist Alexander von Humboldt was among his friends and supporters in Paris.

His paleontology revealed the deep history of life that Charles Darwin would later explain, even though his fixed species opposed evolution. He died in the cholera epidemic of 1832. The new geology of Charles Lyell would rise in part against his world of catastrophes.
