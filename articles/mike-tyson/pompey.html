Pompey

Roman general and statesman (106–48 BC)

Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus—Pompey the Great—was for a generation the most celebrated soldier of Rome. Rising young in the bloody wars of the dictator Sulla, he won victory after victory until even his jealous patron granted him a triumph and the surname "the Great." He broke the rebels in Spain, helped stamp out the last embers of the slave revolt, swept the pirates from the Mediterranean in a single dazzling campaign, and in the East shattered the power of King Mithridates, reorganising whole provinces and entering Jerusalem itself.

At the height of his glory he joined with Julius Caesar and the immensely rich Crassus in the First Triumvirate that dominated Rome, sealing the bond by marrying Caesar's beloved daughter Julia. While she lived, the alliance held; but her death in childbirth and the fall of Crassus far away in Parthia loosened the ties between the two surviving giants.

Drifting back toward the Senate, Pompey at last stood against Caesar. When Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC, Pompey withdrew to Greece to gather his forces, only to be outgeneralled and crushed at the battle of Pharsalus. He fled by sea to Egypt to seek refuge, and was stabbed to death as he stepped ashore, betrayed by the courtiers of the boy-king who ruled beside Cleopatra; his severed head was carried to Caesar, who is said to have wept.

His career was chronicled by Cicero's letters and by Plutarch. The third member of the great triumvirate, the wealthiest man in Rome, was Crassus.
