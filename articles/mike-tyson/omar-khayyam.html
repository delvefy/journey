Omar Khayyam

Persian mathematician, astronomer, and poet (1048–1131)

Omar Khayyam, born at Nishapur in Persia, was a master of several arts at once, though the world has come to know him chiefly for the one he may have practiced least seriously. In his own time he was honored above all as a mathematician and astronomer, a thinker of rigorous and original mind.

In mathematics he wrote a celebrated treatise on algebra in which he classified cubic equations and solved them by the elegant intersection of conic sections, going far beyond the algebra he had inherited. He also probed the foundations of geometry, wrestling with Euclid's troublesome postulate on parallel lines and his theory of proportion in ways that later mathematicians would recognize as prophetic.

Summoned to Isfahan by Sultan Malik-Shah and his vizier, Khayyam led the astronomers who reformed the Persian calendar, producing a reckoning of the year more precise than the Gregorian one Europe adopted centuries afterward. In philosophy he counted himself a disciple of the great Avicenna, and tradition holds that he died with Avicenna's book open before him.

Yet his immortality came from a sheaf of quatrains, the Rubaiyat, brooding and wine-soaked verses on fate, doubt, and the fleeting sweetness of life—though scholars dispute how many are truly his. Obscure for centuries, they burst upon the West in a Victorian translation that made “Omar Khayyam” a household name. The earlier master on whose foundational work the whole science of algebra rested, and from whose name we draw the very word, was Al-Khwarizmi.
