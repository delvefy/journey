Anwar Sadat

President of Egypt, 1970–1981 (1918–1981)

Anwar Sadat was the Egyptian president who waged one last war against Israel and then astonished the world by making peace with it—a gamble that won him the Nobel Prize and cost him his life.

A Free Officer alongside Gamal Abdel Nasser in the revolution of 1952, he served long in his predecessor's shadow before succeeding him on Nasser's death in 1970. Underestimated at first, he soon proved bold and unpredictable. He expelled the Soviet advisers his predecessor had welcomed, and in October 1973 launched a surprise attack across the Suez Canal that, though ultimately checked, shattered the myth of Israeli invincibility and restored Arab pride.

From that position of recovered honour he made his most dramatic move: in 1977 he flew to Jerusalem and addressed the Israeli parliament, the first Arab leader ever to do so. The following year, at Camp David, he and the Israeli prime minister hammered out the framework for a peace treaty, regaining the Sinai for Egypt. He shared the 1978 Nobel Peace Prize for it.

The price was steep: much of the Arab world denounced him as a traitor, and Egypt was cast out of the Arab League. At home his crackdowns bred bitter enemies, and in October 1981 he was gunned down by Islamist soldiers during a military parade. The American president who had brought him and the Israelis together at Camp David, and staked his own presidency on that peace, was Jimmy Carter.
