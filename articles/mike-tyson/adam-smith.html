Adam Smith

Scottish economist and philosopher (1723–1790)

Adam Smith, born in 1723 in the Scottish port town of Kirkcaldy, was a moral philosopher who became the founding figure of modern economics. He studied at the University of Glasgow and at Oxford, then returned to Glasgow as professor of moral philosophy, lecturing on ethics, jurisprudence, and political economy. A leading light of the Scottish Enlightenment, he formed a deep and lasting friendship with the philosopher David Hume.

In 1759 Smith published The Theory of Moral Sentiments, which grounded morality in human sympathy—our capacity to imagine ourselves in another's position. Its success won him a post tutoring a young Scottish duke on a grand tour of France, where he met the physiocrats and other thinkers whose ideas on wealth and production sharpened his own.

Back in Scotland, Smith spent a decade writing his masterpiece, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, published in 1776. In it he argued that the division of labor drives prosperity and that individuals pursuing their own interest are guided, as if by an "invisible hand," to benefit society as a whole. He attacked the mercantilist system of monopolies and trade restrictions, making the case for free markets and competition.

The Wealth of Nations became the cornerstone of classical economics, shaping debates over capitalism ever since—including the critiques later mounted by Karl Marx. Smith spent his final years as a customs commissioner in Edinburgh and died in 1790, having asked that most of his unpublished manuscripts be burned.
