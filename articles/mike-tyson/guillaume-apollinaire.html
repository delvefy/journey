Guillaume Apollinaire

French poet and art critic (1880–1918)

Guillaume Apollinaire was the French poet and critic who stood at the heart of the Parisian avant-garde and gave modern art two of its names.

Born in Rome of mixed and illegitimate parentage, he made his way to Paris and became the irrepressible champion and friend of its young artists. He was at the center of Pablo Picasso's circle—so close to it that he was briefly and wrongly arrested in connection with the theft of the Mona Lisa from the Louvre. As a critic he did more than anyone to explain and defend the new painting, christening it “Cubism” in his writings, and he later coined the word “Surrealism” that a younger generation would adopt as its banner.

His own poetry was as inventive as the art he loved. Alcools abandoned punctuation and ranged freely through memory and the modern city, while in Calligrammes he arranged words into pictures on the page. Through it all ran his love affairs, most famously with the painter Marie Laurencin.

He volunteered for the Great War and suffered a head wound in 1916; weakened, he was carried off by the influenza pandemic in 1918, only thirty-eight, two days before the Armistice. Among the artists he had done most to champion—the self-taught painter of dream-jungles whom the avant-garde adored—was Henri Rousseau.
