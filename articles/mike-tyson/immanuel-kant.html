Immanuel Kant

German philosopher (1724–1804)

Immanuel Kant, born in 1724 in the Prussian city of Königsberg, was the pivotal figure of modern philosophy. He spent his entire long life in his home town, so regular in his habits that neighbors were said to set their clocks by his afternoon walks, while his mind ranged over the deepest questions of knowledge, morality, and reality.

By his own account, it was the skepticism of David Hume that roused him from his "dogmatic slumber," and the writings of Rousseau that taught him to honor the dignity of ordinary people. His response was a "Copernican revolution" in philosophy, set out in the formidable Critique of Pure Reason. There he argued that the mind does not passively receive the world but actively shapes it: space, time, and the categories of understanding are structures the mind imposes, so that we can know things as they appear to us but never things as they are in themselves.

In ethics he was equally revolutionary. The categorical imperative commanded that one act only on principles one could will to become universal laws, and that one treat humanity always as an end and never merely as a means. He wrote also on beauty, on history, and—reaching back to Newton—on the formation of the cosmos.

Kant died in 1804, having reset the agenda of Western thought. His work gave rise to German Idealism and to thinkers such as Hegel, and his influence on epistemology and ethics endures undiminished.
