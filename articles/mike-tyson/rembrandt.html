Rembrandt

Dutch Golden Age painter (1606–1669)

Rembrandt van Rijn was the supreme master of the Dutch Golden Age and one of the greatest painters in the history of art. Born in Leiden, the son of a miller, he rose swiftly in Amsterdam to become the most sought-after portraitist and history painter of the booming Dutch republic. From the start he commanded an unrivaled gift for light and shadow—learned in part from the followers of Caravaggio—and for the inner life of his sitters.

His range was vast: the startling group portrait The Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Tulp, the huge and revolutionary Night Watch, tender and terrible biblical scenes, and a lifelong series of self-portraits in which he recorded, with merciless honesty, the journey from confident youth to careworn old age. He was also among the greatest etchers who ever lived.

Success did not last. Living lavishly and collecting art beyond his means, and stricken by the deaths of his wife Saskia and several children, he fell into bankruptcy and lost his fine house. Yet adversity only deepened his art; his late works, rough and glowing with humanity, are his most profound.

He died in Amsterdam in 1669, poor but undimmed, having absorbed the lessons of Rubens and the glowing color of Titian into a vision wholly his own. The other towering genius of that Dutch golden age, master of quiet light-filled interiors, was Johannes Vermeer.
