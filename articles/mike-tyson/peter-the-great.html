Peter the Great

Tsar and first Emperor of Russia (1672–1725)

Peter the Great, born in 1672, transformed Russia from an inward-looking realm on Europe's edge into a great power of the Continent. A giant of a man, restless and ferociously energetic, he came to the throne as a boy under the regency of his half-sister Sophia, whom he pushed aside to rule in his own right. From the first he was determined to drag his country into the modern age.

In a remarkable Grand Embassy of 1697–98 he travelled through the Dutch Republic and England, often in disguise, labouring in shipyards with his own hands to learn the crafts of the West and recruiting engineers, sailors, and scholars to carry home. There he absorbed the science and technique of Europe; in later years the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz advised him on organising a Russian academy of sciences.

Back home he remade the state by force of will: building a navy and a modern conscript army, reordering the administration and the ranks of service, subordinating the Orthodox Church to the crown, and compelling his nobles to shave their beards and dress in the European style. He even had his own son and heir, Alexei, imprisoned and broken for resisting his designs.

His long Great Northern War against Sweden brought early disaster and then, at Poltava in 1709, a shattering victory that won Russia the Baltic coast. On newly conquered marshland he raised a wholly new capital, Saint Petersburg, his "window on the West," at a terrible cost in lives. Proclaimed Emperor in 1721, he died in 1725, leaving an empire whose later sovereigns—Catherine the Great above all—claimed his mantle. His great rival in that war was the warrior-king Charles XII of Sweden.
