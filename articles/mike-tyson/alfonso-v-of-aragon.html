Alfonso V of Aragon

King of Aragon and Naples, “the Magnanimous” (1396–1458)

Alfonso V inherited the wide Mediterranean realms of the Crown of Aragon—Aragon itself, Valencia, Catalonia, the Balearics, Sardinia, and Sicily—but his heart was set on a prize farther south. When the childless Queen Joanna II of Naples adopted him as her heir, he was drawn into a long and bitter struggle for that kingdom against the rival house of Anjou, a war that consumed two decades of his life.

It nearly ruined him. At the sea-battle of Ponza in 1435 he was captured and handed to Filippo Maria Visconti of Milan; but the prisoner so charmed and out-argued the duke that he was released and turned his captor into an ally. In 1442 he at last took Naples by force and made the city his favorite seat, ruling his scattered empire from beneath its sunlit bay.

There he earned his title of Magnanimous. He poured his wealth into learning and art, gathering at his court the leading humanists of the day, founding an academy, and raising the great marble triumphal arch at the Castel Nuovo. A famous story held that he was once cured of illness simply by hearing the histories of Livy read aloud.

On his death in 1458 he left Naples to a natural son and his Spanish kingdoms to his brother, whose own son would unite Spain—Alfonso's nephew, Ferdinand II of Aragon. The brilliant humanist whom Alfonso sheltered at Naples, and who there exposed a famous forgery that had propped up papal power, was Lorenzo Valla.
