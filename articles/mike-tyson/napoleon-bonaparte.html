Napoleon Bonaparte

Emperor of the French (1769–1821)

Napoleon Bonaparte, born on Corsica in 1769, rose from obscure beginnings to become Emperor of the French and the dominant figure of his age. Trained as an artillery officer, he made his name in the chaos of the French Revolution—at the siege of Toulon, in defending the Republic with a "whiff of grapeshot," and above all in a dazzling campaign in Italy. After a bold expedition to Egypt, he seized power in the coup of 1799, becoming First Consul, and in 1804 crowned himself Emperor.

He was among the greatest commanders in history. Victories such as Austerlitz, Jena, and Friedland left him master of continental Europe, its map redrawn and its thrones filled with his relatives. Yet his ambitions reached beyond the battlefield. The reforms he imposed on France—the Napoleonic Code of civil law, a centralized administration, a concordat with the Church, new schools, and the Bank of France—outlasted his empire and shaped legal systems across the world. He consciously measured himself against Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar.

His power had limits. At sea the British under Nelson destroyed his fleet at Trafalgar. The catastrophic invasion of Russia in 1812 consumed his Grande Armée in snow and retreat, and a coalition of his enemies drove him to abdicate in 1814 and into exile on Elba.

He escaped for one last gamble, the Hundred Days, only to be defeated decisively by the Duke of Wellington and the Prussians at Waterloo in 1815. Banished to the remote Atlantic island of St. Helena, he died there in 1821, leaving a legend and an influence that have never faded.
