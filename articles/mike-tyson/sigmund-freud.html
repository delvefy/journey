Sigmund Freud

Austrian neurologist, founder of psychoanalysis (1856–1939)

Sigmund Freud transformed how the modern world understands the mind. Born in Moravia and raised in Vienna, he trained as a neurologist and studied hysteria under Jean-Martin Charcot in Paris before developing, with Josef Breuer, the “talking cure.” From it grew psychoanalysis, a theory and therapy built on the idea that hidden, largely sexual, unconscious forces shape thought and behaviour.

In a stream of influential books—The Interpretation of Dreams, The Psychopathology of Everyday Life, Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality, and Civilization and Its Discontents—he introduced concepts that became part of everyday language: repression, the unconscious, the ego and the id, and the Oedipus complex, which he named for the Sophoclean hero. He read culture through this lens, analysing Shakespeare's Hamlet and writing a celebrated psychological study of the childhood of Leonardo da Vinci.

He gathered disciples into a movement, though Carl Jung and Alfred Adler would break away to found schools of their own. Among the troubled who sought him out was the composer Gustav Mahler, with whom he once walked and talked for an afternoon.

A Jew, Freud fled Vienna for London after the Nazi annexation of Austria in 1938; he died there the next year of the jaw cancer that had tormented his last years. He liked to say that science had dealt three blows to human self-importance: the cosmological blow of Copernicus, the psychological blow of his own work, and the biological blow struck by Charles Darwin.
