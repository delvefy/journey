Aristarchus of Samos

Greek astronomer (c. 310–230 BC)

Aristarchus of Samos was the boldest astronomer of antiquity, the first known thinker to take the Sun from the train of wandering bodies and set it, motionless, at the centre of the universe. In his system the Earth was no longer the fixed hub of creation but a planet like the others, spinning on its axis each day and circling the Sun each year—an idea that would not be revived for some eighteen centuries.

His heliocentric proposal survives chiefly because Archimedes summarised it in The Sand Reckoner, noting that Aristarchus had placed the stars at an immense distance to explain why they showed no shift as the Earth moved. The single complete work that remains under his name, On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon, applies clever geometry to the half-lit Moon to argue that the Sun is far larger than the Earth and very far away—a conclusion that may have led him to doubt that so great a body could revolve about so small a one.

The age was not ready for him. His moving Earth clashed with the physics of Aristotle and with the plain evidence of the senses, and one Stoic declared that Aristarchus ought to be indicted for impiety for disturbing the hearth of the world. His insight lay neglected until Nicolaus Copernicus revived it.

Aristarchus drew on an older current of thought, the Pythagorean tradition of a cosmos in which the Earth itself is in motion—a tradition whose first daring exponent had been Philolaus.
