Ptolemy

Greco-Roman astronomer and geographer (c. 100–170 AD)

Claudius Ptolemy, who worked in Roman Alexandria, was the most influential astronomer and geographer of antiquity, a scholar whose authority would shape the Western and Islamic view of the cosmos for some fourteen centuries. Gathering and perfecting the work of earlier observers, above all Hipparchus, he set out in his great treatise—long known by its Arabic name, the Almagest—a complete mathematical model of the heavens.

In Ptolemy's system the Earth stood motionless at the centre of the universe, while the Moon, Sun, planets, and stars wheeled around it on intricate combinations of circles, epicycles riding upon deferents, contrived to reproduce the observed wanderings of the planets with impressive accuracy. Resting on the physics and cosmology of Aristotle, this geocentric scheme became the unquestioned picture of the world until it was at last overthrown.

Ptolemy was also the great geographer of the ancient world. His Geography listed the coordinates of thousands of places and taught how to project the round Earth onto a flat map, building on the foundations laid by Eratosthenes. Its figures, which made the globe too small and Asia too broad, would centuries later encourage Christopher Columbus to believe he could reach the East by sailing west.

He wrote, too, the standard ancient handbook of astrology and works on optics and music. His towering system endured until the astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus dared to move the Earth from the centre and set it in motion around the Sun—a revolution completed by Galileo Galilei.
