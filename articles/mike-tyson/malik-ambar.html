Malik Ambar

Ethiopian regent and general of Ahmadnagar (c. 1548–1626)

Malik Ambar was one of the most remarkable figures of the Indian Deccan: an Ethiopian sold into slavery as a boy who rose to become the regent and military genius of the Sultanate of Ahmadnagar, and for a generation the chief obstacle to the Mughal conquest of the south.

Born in Ethiopia and carried through the slave markets to India, he entered the service of the Habshi—African—nobility of Ahmadnagar and steadily climbed to power. When the Mughals overran the kingdom's capital, Malik Ambar took up its cause, set a prince of the old dynasty on the throne as his puppet, and from a new base rebuilt the sultanate's resistance.

His genius lay in war. Against the heavy Mughal armies he waged a mobile campaign of light cavalry, swift raids, and scorched earth—the guerrilla method that the Marathas would later perfect under Shivaji. For some twenty-five years he held the imperial forces at bay, frustrating the emperor Jahangir, who hated him so bitterly that he had himself painted shooting the African's severed head upon a spear. Even the future Shah Jahan, sent as a prince to subdue him, could not break him.

Malik Ambar was also a builder and administrator. He founded the city later called Aurangabad, constructed famous waterworks, and reformed the land revenue of his state on a just and lasting model. He drew Maratha captains into his service, among them Shahaji, the father of Shivaji.

He died in 1626, still unconquered, and within a few years the kingdom he had upheld fell at last. Yet his example—an African slave who became a kingmaker, and the warfare he pioneered—left a deep mark on the Deccan. His resistance built on that begun by the warrior-queen Chand Bibi before him.
