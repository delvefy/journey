William Faulkner

American novelist (1897–1962)

William Faulkner was the great novelist of the American South, who turned a small corner of Mississippi into a universe and won the Nobel Prize for it.

Born and raised in Mississippi, encouraged early by the writer Sherwood Anderson, he invented the fictional Yoknapatawpha County—a whole imagined world of planters, poor whites, and the descendants of the enslaved, through which he traced the burden of Southern history, guilt and decay. To tell its story he adopted the daring techniques of literary modernism, the spiraling interior monologue he had learned above all from James Joyce, in dense masterpieces like The Sound and the Fury, As I Lay Dying, and Absalom, Absalom!

For years he was admired more by critics than by the public, supporting himself with screenwriting in Hollywood, until the Nobel Prize of 1949 sealed his stature; in his famous acceptance speech he declared that mankind would not merely endure but prevail. His influence radiated outward across the world, shaping Toni Morrison's vision of the haunted American past and inspiring Gabriel García Márquez to build his own mythical town.

He died in 1962 in the Mississippi he had immortalized. His great contemporary and rival for the title of the age's finest American novelist—his opposite in style, spare where Faulkner was torrential—was Ernest Hemingway.
