Democritus

Greek atomist philosopher (c. 460–c. 370 BC)

Democritus of Abdera, building on the teaching of his master Leucippus, conceived one of the most prophetic ideas in the history of thought: that all things are made of countless tiny, indivisible particles—atoms—moving forever through empty space. The endless variety of the world, he held, arises merely from how these atoms differ in shape and how they combine, collide, and rearrange. Nothing comes from nothing; nothing truly perishes; only the eternal atoms and the void are real.

His curiosity was universal. He wrote on ethics, mathematics, music, biology, and the heavens—a vast body of work now almost entirely lost, surviving only in fragments and reports. In ethics he prized cheerfulness and inner contentment, and so vividly did he embody good humor that later ages called him the laughing philosopher, who smiled at the follies of mankind.

Legends gathered about him. By one famous tale his fellow citizens, alarmed by his constant laughter, summoned the physician Hippocrates to cure his supposed madness—only for the doctor to declare him the sanest of men. Plato is said to have so disliked his godless system that he never named him and wished his books burned, while Aristotle took the atomic theory seriously enough to argue against it at length.

Though eclipsed in his own time by the schools of Athens, his vision passed to Epicurus and, through Lucretius, to the modern world that would prove him right. His most celebrated fellow townsman of Abdera, whom tradition makes his pupil and who became the greatest of the Sophists, was Protagoras.
