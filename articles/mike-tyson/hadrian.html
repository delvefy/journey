Hadrian

Roman emperor (76–138)

Hadrian was the cultured and restless emperor who turned Rome from conquest to consolidation, fixing the boundaries of the empire and adorning it with some of its most enduring monuments. A kinsman and ward of Trajan, he succeeded him in 117 and at once abandoned his predecessor's overstretched conquests in the East to concentrate on defensible frontiers. In the far north of Britain he raised the great stone barrier still called Hadrian's Wall.

More than any emperor he made himself a traveler, journeying for years through the provinces to inspect his armies and cities. A devoted lover of Greek art and learning, he rebuilt the Pantheon with its vast dome, completed the Temple of Olympian Zeus at Athens, and created the sprawling pleasure-villa at Tivoli and the towering mausoleum that became the Castel Sant'Angelo. The early death by drowning of his beautiful companion Antinous, whom he had deified, scattered statues of the youth across the Roman world.

His reign was not without ferocity: he crushed the great Jewish revolt of Bar Kokhba with terrible severity, renaming the province and barring Jews from Jerusalem. Learned, suspicious, and contradictory, Hadrian governed with skill while never quite shedding the distrust of the Senate.

To secure the future he arranged a careful succession, adopting a mild and upright senator on the condition that he in turn adopt the promising young Marcus Aurelius. That chosen heir, who would give the empire a long age of peace, was Antoninus Pius.
