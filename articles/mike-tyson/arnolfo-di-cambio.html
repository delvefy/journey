Arnolfo di Cambio

Florentine sculptor and architect (c. 1240–1310)

Arnolfo di Cambio, born around 1240, was the greatest architect and one of the finest sculptors of late-thirteenth-century Italy, the man who gave Florence its monumental face. He learned his craft as a pupil and assistant of Nicola Pisano, working with him on the pulpit of Siena Cathedral and the fountain at Perugia, before striking out on his own as a sculptor of tombs, altars and statues.

He worked for a time in Rome and Naples, where he carved a celebrated seated portrait of his patron, King Charles of Anjou—one of the first lifelike statues of a living ruler since antiquity. He designed elegant Gothic ciboria and tomb monuments that set a fashion across Italy.

But it is as the founding architect of Florence that Arnolfo is remembered. In the 1290s the prosperous city set him to work on a series of vast projects that still define it: the great Franciscan church of Santa Croce, the soaring Palazzo Vecchio that houses its government, and, above all, the immense new cathedral of Santa Maria del Fiore, begun in 1296. He conceived the cathedral on a scale so ambitious that its central space could not be roofed by the technology of his day.

Arnolfo died around 1310, his cathedral far from finished. The bell tower beside it would be designed by Giotto, and the crowning dome—the problem Arnolfo had left unsolved—would wait more than a century for Filippo Brunelleschi to conquer it. Yet the grandeur of Renaissance Florence rose upon the foundations this sculptor-architect had laid.
