Diogenes the Cynic

Greek Cynic philosopher (c. 412–323 BC)

Diogenes of Sinope, the most famous of the Cynic philosophers, turned his life into a relentless performance of philosophical protest. Born around 412 BC in the Black Sea city of Sinope, he was exiled—by his own account for defacing the coinage—and made his way to Athens, where he embraced a doctrine of radical simplicity descended from Antisthenes, a pupil of Socrates.

Diogenes rejected wealth, comfort, reputation, and social convention as so many chains. He owned almost nothing, slept in a large earthenware jar in the marketplace, and threw away his only cup when he saw a child drinking from its cupped hands. To live "according to nature," shamelessly self-sufficient, was for him the whole of virtue. Plato reportedly called him "a Socrates gone mad."

His life bristles with famous anecdotes. He is said to have walked the streets with a lamp in daylight, claiming to search for an honest man. When Plato defined man as a featherless biped, Diogenes plucked a chicken and produced it as "Plato's man." Most celebrated of all, when Alexander the Great stood before him and offered to grant any wish, Diogenes asked only that the conqueror step out of his sunlight—prompting Alexander to remark that, were he not Alexander, he would wish to be Diogenes.

Captured by pirates and sold into slavery, he reportedly told his buyer that his trade was "governing men." He died at Corinth around 323 BC. Through his pupil Crates, his Cynicism became the seedbed of Stoicism.
