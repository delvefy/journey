Aurangzeb

Mughal emperor (1618–1707)

Aurangzeb, the sixth Mughal emperor, was the last of the dynasty's great rulers and among its most controversial. The son of Shah Jahan, he seized the throne in 1658 through a savage war of succession, defeating and executing his liberal elder brother Dara Shikoh, killing his other rivals, and imprisoning his own ailing father in the fort at Agra.

Under his long reign the Mughal Empire swelled to its greatest extent, covering almost the whole of the Indian subcontinent. A disciplined and tireless administrator, Aurangzeb lived with conspicuous personal austerity, reportedly supporting himself by copying the Quran and stitching caps. But where his ancestor Akbar had ruled through tolerance, Aurangzeb governed as a strict and orthodox Muslim. He reimposed the jizya tax on non-Muslims, executed the Sikh guru Tegh Bahadur, and pursued policies toward Hindus that remain bitterly debated to this day.

His later decades were consumed by relentless, grinding warfare in the Deccan against the Marathas, whose founder Shivaji had built a formidable Hindu power in resistance to Mughal rule. These wars bled the treasury and exhausted the army without ever securing a lasting peace.

Aurangzeb died in 1707, nearly ninety years old, after almost half a century on the throne. The vast empire he left behind, overstretched and drained, soon fell into decline, fragmenting into regional powers and opening the door to the eventual ascendancy of the British in India.
