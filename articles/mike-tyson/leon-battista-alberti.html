Leon Battista Alberti

Italian humanist and architect (1404–1472)

Leon Battista Alberti was the nearest thing the early Renaissance had to a universal man. Born in Genoa, the illegitimate son of an exiled Florentine merchant family, he took a degree in canon law, entered the service of the papal court, and turned his restless intelligence to letters, mathematics, cryptography, painting, and building, excelling at each.

His treatise On Painting, written in 1435 and dedicated in its Italian version to Brunelleschi, was the first to set out the mathematics of linear perspective as a teachable method, giving painters the rational framework that Brunelleschi had only demonstrated. He followed it with works on sculpture and, above all, On the Art of Building, the first modern treatise on architecture, modeled closely on the ancient Roman writer Vitruvius and dedicated to the revival of classical principles.

Alberti did not merely theorize. He designed the soaring marble facade of Santa Maria Novella in Florence and the rusticated Palazzo Rucellai, recast a medieval church at Rimini as the classical Tempio Malatestiano for the warlord Sigismondo Malatesta, and raised the great church of Sant'Andrea in Mantua for the Gonzaga, a building that would shape church design for centuries.

Beyond art he wrote a famous dialogue on the family, devised one of the first polyalphabetic ciphers, and studied mapmaking and optics. He died in Rome in 1472, the model of the cultivated, many-sided genius the age admired. The single ancient authority on whom his whole architectural revival rested—the only Roman writer on building whose work survived—was Vitruvius.
