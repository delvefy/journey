Manuel I of Portugal

King of Portugal, "the Fortunate" (1469–1521)

Manuel I, called the Fortunate, ruled Portugal at the very summit of its power and presided over the discoveries that made his small kingdom, for a season, the wealthiest in Europe.

He inherited a realm already straining toward the ocean, and under him the great enterprise bore fruit. In 1497 he dispatched Vasco da Gama, who two years later returned having opened the sea-route to India around Africa. He sent Pedro Álvares Cabral westward in 1500, whose fleet touched the coast of Brazil before sailing on to the Indies. And he backed the eastern conquests of Afonso de Albuquerque, who built the empire of fortresses from Goa to Malacca.

The spices and gold of these ventures flowed into Lisbon, and Manuel spent the riches lavishly, giving his name to the ornate Manueline style of architecture seen in the monastery of the Jerónimos and the tower of Belém.

His dynastic ambitions bound Portugal to Spain: he married in turn two daughters of the Catholic Monarchs, Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon. As a price demanded for these Spanish marriages, he ordered in 1496 the expulsion or forced conversion of Portugal's Jews and Muslims, a cruel act that darkened his glittering reign.

Manuel died in 1521, leaving a kingdom at its zenith. Of all the captains who carried his flag across the world, none had a stranger fortune than the man who first reached Brazil under his orders, Pedro Álvares Cabral.
