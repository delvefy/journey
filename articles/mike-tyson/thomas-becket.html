Thomas Becket

Archbishop of Canterbury and martyr (c. 1119–1170)

Thomas Becket, born in London around 1119, rose from a merchant family to become the most powerful churchman in England—and, in death, its most famous saint. A brilliant and worldly administrator, he won the favour of Theobald, Archbishop of Canterbury, and then of the young King Henry II, who made him Lord Chancellor. For years the two were close friends and companions, and Becket served the king's interests with energy and splendour.

In 1162 Henry, expecting a pliant ally, had Becket made Archbishop of Canterbury. But Becket underwent a remarkable transformation. He resigned the chancellorship, took up an austere life, and became a fierce defender of the rights and independence of the Church against the king's attempts to bring clergy under royal courts. The former friends became bitter enemies, and Becket fled into exile in France for six years.

A fragile reconciliation brought him home in 1170, but the quarrel flared again at once. Hearing of fresh defiance, the enraged king is said to have cried words his courtiers took as a command. Four knights crossed to Canterbury and, on 29 December 1170, cut Becket down at the altar of his own cathedral.

The murder horrified Christendom. Becket was canonised within three years, and his shrine at Canterbury became the greatest pilgrimage site in England—the destination of the pilgrims in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales—while Henry II did public penance for the crime. Centuries later Henry VIII, breaking with Rome, destroyed the shrine and ordered Becket's name struck out as a traitor. Yet the martyred archbishop endured in memory, retold across the ages, most famously in T.S. Eliot's drama Murder in the Cathedral.
