Aratus

Greek didactic poet (c. 315–240 BC)

Aratus of Soli was the most widely read poet of the Hellenistic age after Homer himself. Educated at Athens, where he studied under the Stoic philosopher Zeno of Citium, he was drawn to the court of the Macedonian king Antigonus Gonatas, a friend of poets and philosophers, and there composed the work that made him famous.

That work was the Phaenomena, a long poem in epic hexameters that set out the constellations and the turning of the starry sphere, followed by a guide to the signs of coming weather. For its astronomy Aratus versified the prose treatise of Eudoxus of Cnidus, and for its form he looked back to the old farmer's almanac of Hesiod, opening with a serene Stoic hymn to Zeus, in whom "we live and move." The blend of learning, piety, and polished verse delighted the ancient world, and the poem became a school text for centuries.

Its fame spread to Rome and beyond. The young Cicero translated it into Latin, its weather-lore coloured the Georgics of Virgil, and a single line of it—"for we too are his offspring"—was quoted by the Apostle Paul to the philosophers of Athens, lodging Aratus forever in the pages of Scripture.

His verse was admired by the connoisseurs of his own day, and the exacting Alexandrian poet Callimachus praised the wakeful craftsmanship of the Phaenomena in a famous epigram.

  • The road also leads back to Paul the Apostle.
  • The road also leads back to Cicero.
  • The road also leads back to Virgil.
  • The road also leads back to Hesiod.
  • The road also leads back to Zeno of Citium.
  • The road continues to Callimachus.
