Porphyry

Neoplatonist philosopher (c. 234–305)

Porphyry was the great disciple and editor of Plotinus, and a philosopher and scholar of formidable range in his own right. Born at Tyre in Phoenicia, he studied first under the learned Longinus in Athens before traveling to Rome and attaching himself to Plotinus, whose teaching transformed him. To Porphyry the later world owes the survival of his master's thought: he gathered the scattered treatises into the six sets of nine known as the Enneads and wrote an affectionate Life of Plotinus.

His own works ranged over logic, religion, diet, and philosophy. By far the most influential was the Isagoge, a short "introduction" to the logical categories of Aristotle. Translated into Latin by Boethius, it became the standard textbook of logic for the entire Middle Ages and set off the long scholastic debate over the nature of universals.

Porphyry was also the most formidable pagan critic of the new religion. His massive polemic Against the Christians was so damaging that the Christian emperors later ordered it burned—it survives only in fragments—and it drew refutations from the greatest Christian minds, Augustine among them.

A defender of the old learning at the very moment Christianity was rising to dominance, Porphyry died around 305. The Roman statesman and philosopher who carried his logic, and so much of ancient thought, across the threshold into the Latin Middle Ages was Boethius.
