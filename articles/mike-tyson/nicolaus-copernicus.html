Nicolaus Copernicus

Polish astronomer (1473–1543)

Nicolaus Copernicus, born at Toruń in 1473, set in motion the greatest revolution in the history of astronomy by daring to move the Earth from the centre of the universe. Educated in Poland and then in the Italian universities of Bologna and Padua, he spent his working life as a canon of the cathedral chapter at Frombork, administering its estates, practising medicine, and pursuing astronomy in his spare hours.

Dissatisfied with the cumbersome system of Ptolemy, with its ever-multiplying circles, Copernicus revived and worked out in mathematical detail an idea hinted at in antiquity: that the Sun, not the Earth, lies at the centre, and that the Earth is merely one of the planets, turning daily on its axis and circling the Sun once a year. This single bold rearrangement explained with new elegance the puzzling backward loops of the planets across the sky and overturned the ancient cosmos of Aristotle.

Fearful of ridicule and controversy, he withheld his great book for decades, and only as he lay dying in 1543 did he receive the first printed copy of On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres. An anxious editor had prefaced it as a mere calculating device, but its implications could not be contained.

The Copernican system was carried forward and confirmed by those who came after: defended by Galileo Galilei through the telescope, given its true elliptical form by Johannes Kepler, and at last explained by the gravitation of Isaac Newton. Its boldest champion would refine it into the laws of planetary motion.
