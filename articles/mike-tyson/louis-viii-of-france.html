Louis VIII of France

King of France, "the Lion" (1187–1226)

Louis VIII of France, born in 1187, was the only son of Philip II Augustus and reigned only three years, yet he played a remarkable part in the dramas of his age. Brave and capable, he was nicknamed "the Lion." In 1200 he married Blanche of Castile, a union that would prove enormously consequential for the French monarchy.

His most extraordinary episode came in England. During the baronial revolt against the tyrannical King John, the rebel barons, despairing of their own king, offered the English crown to Louis. In 1216 he crossed the Channel, entered London, and was proclaimed king; for a time he controlled more than half of England. But when John died and the boy Henry III rallied loyalist support, Louis was defeated at the Battle of Lincoln and forced to withdraw, abandoning his English claim.

He returned to France and at last succeeded his father in 1223. As king he continued the Capetian expansion, conquering territory from the English in the south-west and leading a royal crusade against the Cathar heretics of the south—the Albigensian Crusade—bringing Languedoc more firmly under the crown.

Louis VIII died of dysentery in 1226, after only three years on the throne, leaving the kingdom to his young son under the regency of his redoubtable widow, Blanche of Castile. Though his own reign was brief, he was the father of Louis IX, the sainted king, and of Charles of Anjou, the future king of Sicily—a vital link in the chain of the Capetian ascendancy.
