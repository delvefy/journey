Henry David Thoreau

American writer and naturalist (1817–1862)

Henry David Thoreau was born in Concord, Massachusetts, in 1817 and educated at Harvard. Falling under the influence of his neighbor Ralph Waldo Emerson, he became a leading Transcendentalist, supporting himself by turns as a teacher, surveyor, and maker of pencils while devoting his real energy to thought and to the close observation of nature.

For two years he lived alone in a small cabin he built beside Walden Pond, on Emerson's land, and from the experience drew his masterpiece, Walden. Part memoir and part meditation, it praised simplicity, self-reliance, and deliberate living, and recorded the woods and waters around him with a naturalist's precision that makes him a forefather of ecological writing.

Thoreau's other great legacy was political. Jailed for a night for refusing to pay a tax he judged complicit in slavery and an unjust war, he wrote the essay “Civil Disobedience,” arguing that a just person must withhold cooperation from an unjust state. Its influence proved immense, shaping the nonviolent campaigns of Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. and the moral writings of Leo Tolstoy.

A fervent abolitionist, he rose to defend the militant John Brown when others recoiled. He died of tuberculosis in 1862, only forty-four, his fame growing steadily in the century after his death. Among his Concord neighbors was the novelist Nathaniel Hawthorne.
