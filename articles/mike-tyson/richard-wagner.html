Richard Wagner

German composer who reinvented opera (1813–1883)

Richard Wagner reshaped the course of Western music. Dissatisfied with conventional opera, he created vast “music dramas” in which music, poetry, drama, and staging fused into what he called a total work of art. He wove his scores from recurring themes—leitmotifs—associated with characters and ideas, and stretched harmony into long, restless, unresolved currents that, in Tristan und Isolde, opened the door to the music of the coming century.

His works are monuments of ambition: Lohengrin, Tristan und Isolde, Die Meistersinger, and above all the four-evening epic of gods and heroes, Der Ring des Nibelungen, which occupied him for a quarter of a century. To stage his dramas as he wished, he built his own theatre at Bayreuth, a project made possible by the devotion of the young king Ludwig II of Bavaria.

He revered Beethoven above all musicians and steeped himself in the philosophy of Schopenhauer, whose vision colours his mature operas. He married Cosima, by whom his household became a centre of European culture. Yet his legacy is shadowed: his venomous antisemitic writings and his later adoption as a hero of German nationalism left a stain that endures.

His tempestuous friendship with the young Nietzsche, who once saw in him the rebirth of art, ended in bitter estrangement. The dazzling virtuoso pianist and composer who was his father-in-law and tireless champion was Franz Liszt.
