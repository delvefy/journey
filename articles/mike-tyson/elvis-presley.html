Elvis Presley

American singer, the “King of Rock and Roll” (1935–1977)

Elvis Presley was the “King of Rock and Roll,” the explosive performer who carried the new music to a mass white audience and became one of the most famous people of the twentieth century.

Born poor in Mississippi and raised in Memphis, he recorded at the legendary Sun Records and burst into national fame in 1956 with “Heartbreak Hotel” and “Hound Dog.” His gyrating hips scandalized parents and electrified the young, and he fused the Black rhythm and blues he loved with country and gospel into a sound that conquered the world. After army service he returned—welcomed back on a television special hosted by Frank Sinatra—to make films, then triumphed again with his 1968 comeback and his Las Vegas years.

His fame touched everyone. The Beatles idolized him, and John Lennon and Paul McCartney made a pilgrimage to meet him in 1965; in 1970 he turned up unannounced at the White House to be photographed with President Richard Nixon, in what became one of history's most requested images. He drew, always, on the founders of the music, among them his road's own forebear, Chuck Berry.

He died at Graceland in 1977, only forty-two, and his legend has only grown. The other electrifying pioneer of early rock and roll—the flamboyant, shrieking architect of its wildest energy, whose songs Elvis covered—was Little Richard.
