J. M. Synge

Irish playwright (1871–1909)

John Millington Synge was the playwright who distilled the speech and harsh poetry of the Irish countryside into a handful of masterpieces before dying at thirty-seven.

Born near Dublin into a Protestant family, he studied at Trinity College and drifted to Paris, intending a life of literary criticism. There in 1896 he met W. B. Yeats, who gave him the advice that changed his life: abandon Paris, go to the Aran Islands, and write of a life that had never found expression. Synge did exactly that, spending summers among the islanders, learning their Irish and absorbing their idiom, and producing the prose book The Aran Islands.

From that immersion came his plays for the Abbey Theatre, which he helped direct alongside Yeats and Lady Gregory: the stark one-act tragedy Riders to the Sea, and above all The Playboy of the Western World, whose earthy language and irreverent portrait of the peasantry provoked nights of rioting in Dublin in 1907. While in Paris he had also befriended a young James Joyce, who admired and memorized passages of Riders to the Sea and later helped bring it to the stage.

Tubercular and ill for much of his short life, Synge died in 1909 with his last play, Deirdre of the Sorrows, unfinished. His spare, rhythmic theatre of tramps and outcasts and his bleak coastal landscapes left a deep mark on the drama that followed—not least on the bare, haunted stages of Samuel Beckett.
