Gautama Buddha

Founder of Buddhism (traditionally c. 563–483 BC)

Siddhartha Gautama, later known as the Buddha—“the Awakened One”—was by tradition a prince of the Shakya clan, born at Lumbini in the foothills of the Himalaya and raised in sheltered luxury at Kapilavastu. Tradition holds that, venturing beyond the palace, the young prince was struck by the sights of old age, sickness, and death, and by the calm of a wandering ascetic. Resolving to seek the cause and cure of suffering, he abandoned his wife, his infant son, and his inheritance to live as a homeless seeker.

For years, the accounts say, he practised severe austerities, then rejected them as fruitless and adopted instead a “Middle Way” between indulgence and self-mortification. Seated in meditation beneath a fig tree at Bodh Gaya, he is said to have attained enlightenment—a direct insight into the nature of suffering and the way to its release.

At the Deer Park near Sarnath he preached his first sermon, “setting in motion the wheel of the law,” teaching the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path. He taught that craving binds beings to a cycle of rebirth and that its extinction brings nirvana, a release open to all regardless of caste. Around him grew the sangha, a community of monks and nuns.

For some forty-five years he wandered the cities and villages of the Ganges plain, gathering followers and royal patrons, until his death at Kushinagar at about the age of eighty. From these beginnings Buddhism spread across Asia, carried later by the emperor Ashoka. A contemporary teacher in the same region, revered by Jains as the founder of their faith, was Mahavira.
