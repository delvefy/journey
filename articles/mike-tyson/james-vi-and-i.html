James VI and I

King of Scotland and England (1566–1625)

James was the king who first united the crowns of Scotland and England, a learned and shrewd if ungainly ruler whose reign bridged the Tudor and Stuart ages. The son of Mary, Queen of Scots, and Lord Darnley, he became King James VI of Scotland as a baby when his mother was forced to abdicate, and was reared by regents and severe Presbyterian tutors. He grew into a canny politician who tamed his fractious Scottish nobles and asserted a lofty doctrine of kingship.

On the death of the childless Elizabeth I in 1603, James inherited the English throne as James I, the first of the Stuart line to rule both kingdoms. He styled himself King of Great Britain and dreamed of a fuller union. A scholar-king sometimes mocked as "the wisest fool in Christendom," he wrote on the divine right of kings and on the menace of witchcraft, and he sought peace abroad after the long Spanish wars.

Two great events marked his English reign. In 1605 a Catholic conspiracy to blow up the king and Parliament—the Gunpowder Plot—was discovered in the nick of time. And in 1611 there appeared the magnificent Authorized Version of the Bible he had commissioned, the "King James" Bible, one of the supreme monuments of the English language. As patron he also took Shakespeare's company under his wing as the King's Men.

His belief in absolute royal authority strained his dealings with Parliament and bequeathed a fatal conflict to his son and heir, Charles I. Among the brilliant Elizabethans who fell foul of the new reign—imprisoned for years in the Tower and finally executed—was the adventurer Walter Raleigh.
