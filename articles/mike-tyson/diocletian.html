Diocletian

Roman emperor (244–311)

Diocletian was the iron-willed soldier-emperor who hauled the Roman Empire out of the chaos of the Third Century and refounded it on new and more autocratic lines. Born of humble Dalmatian stock, he rose through the ranks of the army to be proclaimed emperor in 284, after fifty years in which emperors had risen and fallen in bewildering succession. He resolved to end the cycle for good.

His great innovation was the Tetrarchy, the "rule of four": two senior emperors styled Augusti and two junior Caesars, each governing a quarter of the empire, so that power and defense could be shared and the succession made orderly. He overhauled the provinces and bureaucracy, separated civil from military authority, reformed the army and the tax system, and tried—with little success—to halt runaway inflation by an edict fixing maximum prices. He cloaked the throne in oriental ceremony, demanding worship as a near-divine lord.

His reign carried a dark legacy for Christians: in 303 he unleashed the Great Persecution, the last and most savage assault the Roman state ever made upon the Church. Yet in 305 he did something no emperor had done before—he voluntarily abdicated and retired to his vast palace on the Dalmatian coast, reportedly preferring to tend his garden than to resume power.

The recovery he completed had been set in motion a few years earlier by another great soldier-emperor, the warrior who had reunited a fracturing empire by force of arms—Aurelian.
