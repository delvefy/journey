Jean Lafitte

Pirate and privateer of the Gulf of Mexico (c.1780–c.1823)

Jean Lafitte was the most famous pirate of the Gulf of Mexico, a French-speaking smuggler whose exact origins and end are lost in legend. By the 1810s he and his brother Pierre presided over a thriving outlaw kingdom at Barataria Bay, south of New Orleans, running contraband goods and smuggled slaves past American customs and commanding a fleet of privateers loyal to him.

His great moment came in the War of 1812. As a British force bore down on New Orleans, its officers tried to buy his help; instead Lafitte warned the Americans and offered his men, his ships, and his stores of cannon flints to the defending general. The Baratarians' gunnery contributed to the crushing American victory in January 1815, and in reward President James Madison granted the pirates a full pardon.

The reprieve did not reform him. He soon set up a new smuggling base on Galveston Island, from which he sold contraband and trafficked enslaved people—among his customers the young Jim Bowie—until the United States Navy forced him out.

What became of him afterward is uncertain; by tradition he died around 1823, somewhere in the Caribbean or Central America, and a thick growth of romantic legend has obscured the rest. His finest hour had been spent in the service of Andrew Jackson, and his pardon had come from the hand of James Madison.
