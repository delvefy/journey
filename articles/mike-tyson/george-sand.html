George Sand

French novelist of independence and rebellion (1804–1876)

George Sand was the pen name of Aurore Dupin, one of the most prolific, popular, and scandalous writers of nineteenth-century France. She scandalized respectable society by leaving her husband, wearing men's clothes in public, smoking, and taking a string of celebrated lovers—among them the poet Alfred de Musset and the composer Chopin—all while writing at a furious pace and earning her own living by her pen, a rare and defiant independence for a woman of her age.

Her early novels, such as Indiana and Lélia, are passionate protests on behalf of women's freedom and the rights of the heart against the constraints of marriage and convention. Later she turned to gentler, idealized tales of rural life—The Devil's Pool, Little Fadette—rooted in the countryside of her beloved estate at Nohant. Throughout she carried the influence of Rousseau, with his faith in nature, feeling, and human goodness.

A committed republican and idealist, she threw herself into the revolution of 1848. Her home and her correspondence made her a hub of the age's cultural life: she was the devoted friend of Flaubert, knew Balzac, Liszt, and Delacroix, and was admired across Europe.

By the time she died in 1876 she was a revered national figure. The towering man of French letters who mourned her as an immortal was Victor Hugo.
