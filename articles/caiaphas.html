Caiaphas

high priest of Israel, c. AD 18–36

Joseph ben Caiaphas, called simply Caiaphas in the New Testament, served as high priest of the Jerusalem Temple from about AD 18 until 36 — an eighteen-year tenure unmatched in length by any other holder of the office under direct Roman rule. The bare extremes of those dates already say something of the man: he outlasted four Roman prefects and presided over the Temple cult through nearly the entire decade of Pontius Pilate's governorship. He took office under Valerius Gratus, Pilate's predecessor, and was deposed by Lucius Vitellius shortly after Pilate himself was recalled to Rome. The two men, the Jewish high priest and the Roman governor, rose and fell almost together, and their long, working cooperation defined the religious and political climate of Judea in the years that the Christian movement was born.

He is named with unusual precision by Flavius Josephus, who twice gives his full title — Joseph, who was called Caiaphas — and recounts his appointment and removal. The New Testament adds the detail that he was the son-in-law of Annas, the high priest whom the Roman governor Quirinius had appointed in AD 6 and whom Valerius Gratus had deposed nine years later. Annas, deprived of the office himself, evidently retained the power to place his relatives in it: after a series of brief tenures by his sons-in-law and one son, Caiaphas held the post for the longest stretch and is repeatedly named together with Annas in the gospels, as though the two men shared a single authority. Luke calls them jointly the high priests in the year that John the Baptist began to preach.

The most remarkable confirmation of his existence came in 1990, when a construction crew working in the Peace Forest south of the Old City of Jerusalem broke through the roof of a first-century burial cave containing twelve ossuaries — limestone bone-boxes used in the brief Jewish practice of secondary burial. Two of them bore versions of the name Yehosef bar Qayafa, Joseph son of Caiaphas, and one of these, the most ornately carved of the group, contained the bones of an elderly man in his sixties. Most archaeologists accept the ossuary as belonging to the high priest of the gospels, making him one of the very few figures of the New Testament whose physical remains have been identified.

What is known of his administration is largely glimpsed through three sets of trials. The first is the trial of Jesus of Nazareth at Passover, perhaps in AD 30 or 33, when the council convened at night in the high priest's house and questioned the captive Galilean. According to the gospel narratives, Caiaphas presided. He asked Jesus directly whether he was the Christ, the Son of the Blessed; he interpreted the answer as blasphemy; he tore his robes and pronounced the prisoner worthy of death; and he handed him over to Pilate with the political charge of claiming kingship. The gospel of John attributes to him a striking earlier line, spoken to a council debating what to do about Jesus: it is better for you that one man should die for the people than that the whole nation should perish. It is among the most memorable sayings attributed to a Jewish high priest in any ancient source — calculating, lapidary, and, in the gospel writer's reading, unwittingly prophetic of the very crucifixion he was about to engineer.

The second set of trials, recounted in the early chapters of the book of Acts, took place perhaps a few months later, when Caiaphas and Annas summoned the disciples Peter and John to the Sanhedrin to answer for healing a crippled man at the Temple gate and proclaiming the resurrection of the same Jesus they had just had executed. The high priests forbade them to speak in that name. They returned a second time after the apostles defied the order, this time threatening more severely; only the intervention of the Pharisee Gamaliel, who counseled patience on the grounds that a movement of God could not be stopped and a movement of men would die of itself, prevented harsher action. Caiaphas's instinct, the narrative makes clear, was suppression. The third confrontation, the stoning of the deacon Stephen, was probably conducted under his presidency as well, though the text does not name him.

He governed in a small political space. The high priest of the Roman period was no longer the autonomous figure of Hasmonean Jerusalem but an official appointed and removable at the pleasure of the prefect or the legate of Syria, whose vestments were kept under Roman guard in the Antonia fortress and released only for the great festivals. To last eighteen years in this office required, above all, the confidence of the occupier. Caiaphas plainly had it: Pilate, otherwise willing to provoke the Jewish people over standards, aqueducts, and Samaritan prophets, never disturbed his Temple administration, and the two men evidently worked out a modus vivendi by which the priesthood kept order in the city and the prefect kept his hands free for the rest of the province. Their fall was nearly simultaneous: Vitellius, after recalling Pilate to Rome in AD 36, swept the high priesthood as well, replacing Caiaphas with Jonathan, son of Annas.

He disappears from view at that point. There is no record of his death, nothing of the years between his removal and the ossuary that may now bear his bones. His name survives in the gospels as the architect of the trial that handed Jesus to Pilate, in Josephus as a footnote in the chronology of Judean high priests, and on a finely incised stone box recovered, by accident, from a quiet hill above the Hinnom Valley nineteen centuries after he was laid in it.
