Drusus the Younger

c. 14 BC – AD 23 · only son of Tiberius, heir of the principate

Drusus Julius Caesar, called Drusus the Younger to distinguish him from his more famous uncle Drusus the Elder and the cousins and nephews of the same name, was the only natural son of the emperor Tiberius, born at Rome about 14 BC to Tiberius and his much-loved first wife Vipsania Agrippina, the daughter of Agrippa. He was about three when his mother was taken from his father by Augustus's command and given to another marriage; he was raised in his father's household. He served creditably in two important commands, held the consulship twice, was awarded the tribunician power in AD 22 as the formal designation of him as Tiberius's chosen successor, and was poisoned by his own wife Livilla and her lover Sejanus in the late summer of AD 23 in his thirty-seventh year. His death, which seemed at the time to be a slow wasting illness, was concealed for eight years before being exposed at the fall of Sejanus in AD 31.

He was born about 14 BC and was a small child when his father retired in 6 BC from political life to Rhodes for what became seven years of self-exile. His mother Vipsania, divorced by Tiberius under Augustus's compulsion in 11 BC and married again to Asinius Gallus, would have at least six other children, the boy's half-siblings, who remained close to him in adulthood. The first great quarrel of his public career was, in fact, with his stepfather Asinius Gallus, with whom he is reported to have come to blows in public on the Palatine in his early twenties; there is some indication that the bad blood between Asinius Gallus and Tiberius dated from this period and was a continuing source of poison in court politics for the next generation. Drusus the Younger was raised, after Tiberius's return to Rome in AD 2, increasingly within his father's household, and from AD 4, when Augustus adopted Tiberius and required him to adopt Germanicus, Drusus and Germanicus were cousins by blood and adoptive brothers in the same family.

About AD 4 Drusus the Younger married Livilla — the daughter of his uncle Drusus the Elder by Antonia the Younger, sister of Germanicus, granddaughter of Livia. She had previously been briefly married to Augustus's grandson Gaius Caesar, who had died of his wounds at Limyra earlier that year. The marriage between Drusus and Livilla, which was thus the marriage of a first cousin to a first cousin, produced three children: twin sons Tiberius Gemellus and Germanicus Gemellus, born about AD 19, of whom Germanicus Gemellus died at four; and a daughter, Julia Livia, born about AD 5, who would in time be married to Nero Julius Caesar, the eldest son of Germanicus and Agrippina the Elder, and after his exile and death would be married off again to Gaius Rubellius Blandus.

His first major command was in the late summer of AD 14, in his late twenties, when the legions in Pannonia mutinied on the news of Augustus's death and the accession of Tiberius. Tiberius sent Drusus, with a single cohort of the praetorian guard and his praetorian prefect Sejanus, to settle the camps. Drusus negotiated with the mutineers personally, used the convenient occurrence of a lunar eclipse — interpreted by the soldiers as a sign of divine displeasure with their action — and brought the camps back to discipline by a combination of negotiation, executions of the principal mutineers, and the dispatch of the surrendering legions back into the field. He had been blooded in command in his first major task. He returned to Rome in honor.

He served as consul in AD 15, jointly with his father, and as consul again in AD 21. Between the consulships he commanded in Illyricum from AD 17 to AD 20, where he conducted a careful diplomacy among the Marcomanni and the Quadi and the other German peoples north of the Danube, dividing them against one another and exploiting the quarrel between the Marcomannic king Maroboduus and the Cheruscan rival Arminius — Arminius, the destroyer of Varus's legions, who would be killed by his own kinsmen in AD 21 in part as a result of Drusus's diplomacy. Maroboduus, who had been expelled by Arminius's allies, fled into Roman territory and was kept by Drusus's arrangement at Ravenna for the rest of his life. The eastern frontier of the Rhine, which had collapsed in AD 9 and been violently restored by Germanicus from AD 14 to AD 16, was stabilized by his cousin's quieter and more political work over the next four years. Tacitus, no friend of the Tiberian house, gives him credit for the diplomacy.

He returned to Rome in AD 20 and was given the tribunician power in AD 22, the formal designation of him as Tiberius's chosen successor. He was about thirty-five when this honor was conferred. He was, on the testimony of every source, a less brilliant figure than his cousin Germanicus — who had died at Antioch three years earlier — but an able, careful, militarily competent man who would, had he lived, have been a serviceable and probably restrained second-generation emperor of the principate. He had quarreled openly with Sejanus, whom he disliked and whom he reportedly struck in a public argument; Sejanus, by Tacitus's reading, was the figure he most needed to remove from his father's court before he could rule.

He was, instead, poisoned. In the spring of AD 23 he fell into a slow wasting illness; he lingered for several months, attended by the imperial physician Eudemus, and died at Rome in the late summer. Tiberius, by Tacitus's report, attended the Senate on the day of the death with his old self-command, delivered the funeral oration in person, and bore the public ceremonies with the impassive dignity he was famous for; Tacitus notes the rumor at Rome that the emperor's grief was less than expected. Drusus the Younger was about thirty-six. His body was burned and his ashes interred in the mausoleum of Augustus.

The true cause of death emerged eight years later, when his own wife Livilla, exposed in the conspiracy with Sejanus and arrested in the purge of October AD 31, was identified by Sejanus's discarded first wife Apicata in a suicide note as the principal poisoner of her husband. Apicata's note revealed that Livilla had bribed Eudemus and Drusus's cup-bearer Lygdus to administer the poison gradually over weeks; that Sejanus had been her accomplice; and that the murder had cleared the succession in two directions — it removed the natural heir of Tiberius, and it left Livilla, the widow of an imperial prince, available as a marriage candidate for Sejanus's planned rise to the principate. Tiberius, by Suetonius's account, received the suicide note at Capri, retired into a darker isolation than before, and never again could bring himself to look directly on a portrait of Drusus the Younger.

The dynastic effect of his murder was very large. His sole surviving son Tiberius Gemellus was four at the death; with the deaths of Germanicus's elder sons Nero and Drusus Caesar in the purges of AD 29 to AD 33, Gemellus was, alongside his cousin Caligula, one of only two surviving male candidates for the succession in AD 37. Tiberius, on his deathbed, named the two of them as joint heirs; Caligula, within six months of his accession, had Gemellus killed on the charge of having a cough that resembled an antidote being taken in expectation of a poisoning — the absurdity of the pretext giving the measure of how far the dynasty had moved, in a generation, from the careful Augustan politics of designated heirs and quiet successions.

Had Drusus the Younger lived, the principate would have passed to him in AD 37 and, beyond him, perhaps to his son Gemellus. The reign that began with Caligula would either not have begun at all or would have been considerably postponed; the violent rule of Germanicus's house, which produced the events of the next thirty years, would have been an alternative tradition rather than the surviving one. He is the great might-have-been of the Tiberian house, as his cousin Germanicus was the might-have-been of the Augustan one. The two cousins both died of poison, four years apart, before either could rule.
