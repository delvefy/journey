Thomas Aquinas

1225 – 1274, the Angelic Doctor, Italian Dominican, master of the Summa and the Aristotelian theologian of the Latin Middle Ages

Thomas Aquinas was born about 1225 at the castle of Roccasecca in the Campagna of southern Italy, the youngest son of Landulf of Aquino, a count of Norman-Lombard descent in the service of the Emperor Frederick II, and Theodora of Caracciolo, of a family of the Neapolitan nobility. The position of the family was complicated: Landulf was a vassal of both the German emperor and the Neapolitan king of his line, and the castle of Roccasecca stood on a frontier of the long struggle between Frederick II and the papacy. In 1230, at the age of five, Thomas was offered as an oblate to the great Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino, a few miles away, where his uncle Sinibald was abbot and where the family expected him in time to succeed: it was a prestigious career for the youngest of seven sons.

The political wars of Frederick II disrupted the plan. By 1239 the emperor had occupied Monte Cassino and dispersed most of the monks; the boy Thomas was sent to the new University of Naples, which Frederick himself had founded in 1224 as a secular and imperial counterpart to Bologna. There he encountered for the first time the full text of Aristotle's natural philosophy and metaphysics, recently translated from Arabic and from Greek and not yet officially admitted to the Paris theology faculty; and there he met the Dominicans, the still-young order of preaching friars founded in 1216 by Saint Dominic and devoted to learning and to active urban ministry. In 1244, against the violent opposition of his family, who had not consigned him to Monte Cassino in order to see him become a mendicant friar, Thomas joined the Dominicans. His brothers, with his mother's connivance, seized him on the road north and held him captive at the family castle of San Giovanni for some fifteen months, hoping to break his resolution. He used the time to study the Bible and the Sentences. He emerged in 1245 unshaken, and was sent by the order to Cologne, to study under the great Dominican master Albertus Magnus.

The years with Albert were the formative ones. Albertus Magnus — the only medieval scholar called Great in his own lifetime — was at work on the immense project of producing Latin commentaries on the whole of the Aristotelian corpus, both the natural philosophy and the metaphysics and the new translations of the Greek commentators, and Thomas attached himself to the project. He listened to Albert's lectures at Cologne from 1248 to 1252, took the dictation of the master's commentaries, and absorbed both the substance and the temper of Albert's enterprise: the conviction that the philosophy of Aristotle, properly understood, was the natural philosophical complement to Catholic theology, and that to reject it on the grounds of its pagan source would be intellectual cowardice. His fellow students, struck by his silent stoutness, nicknamed him "the dumb ox of Sicily"; Albert is reported to have said that the dumb ox would one day bellow so loud as to be heard around the world.

From 1252 to 1259 Thomas was at Paris, first as bachelor of the Sentences — the standard examination exercise of advanced theology students — and from 1256 as master of theology, holding one of the two Dominican chairs in defiance of the secular masters who had tried to exclude the friars from the faculty. He produced in these years the Commentary on the Sentences, the disputed questions De Veritate, and the polemical Contra Impugnantes Dei Cultum, defending the mendicants against William of Saint-Amour. From 1259 to 1268 he was attached to the Roman court of the popes — first at Anagni, then at Orvieto, Rome, and Viterbo — teaching at the curia and at the studium of the Dominican order. The major work of this period was the Summa Contra Gentiles, completed about 1264, a four-book apologetic addressed to Christian missionaries among Muslim and Jewish intellectuals, and the beginning of the Summa Theologiae, the great unfinished masterpiece on which his reputation principally rests.

He returned to Paris in 1268 for a second regency, the most controversial of his career. The faculty of arts had embraced a strict reading of Aristotle, mediated through the great Arab commentator Averroes, which appeared to teach the eternity of the world, the unicity of the human intellect (and thus the absence of personal immortality), and the conflict of reason and revelation. The principal Latin Averroist was Siger of Brabant; the principal Augustinian critic of all use of Aristotle in theology was the new Franciscan minister general, Bonaventure, an old friend and rival of Thomas. Thomas held the middle position: Aristotle could and must be used by Christian theology, but on the points where pagan Aristotelianism conflicted with revelation, the pagan reading had to be corrected. His two short treatises De Aeternitate Mundi and De Unitate Intellectus contra Averroistas, written in 1270 in the heat of the controversy, are concentrated examples of his method. The condemnations of 1270 by Bishop Tempier of Paris, repeated and extended in 1277, included some Thomist positions among the rejected propositions, a verdict that was reversed only after his canonisation half a century later.

In 1272 he returned to Italy to organise a new Dominican studium at Naples, where he had been a student a generation earlier. He continued the Summa Theologiae into the third part, on Christ and the sacraments, but on 6 December 1273, at Mass in the chapel of St Nicholas at Naples, he had an experience — visionary, or a stroke, or both — which left him incapable of dictating further. To his secretary Reginald of Piperno's distressed appeals he answered only, "I cannot, because all that I have written seems like straw to me compared with what I have seen." The Summa remained unfinished. The supplement that fills out the third part in the standard editions is a posthumous compilation from his earlier writings.

Pope Gregory X summoned him to the Second Council of Lyon, called for 1274 to reconcile the Greek and Latin churches and to undertake a new Crusade. He set out from Naples in February, took a fall against an overhanging branch on the road, fell ill, and arrived at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova on the way north too weak to continue. He died there on 7 March 1274, in his forty-ninth year, after dictating a final brief commentary on the Song of Songs. He was canonised by John XXII at Avignon on 18 July 1323, declared Doctor of the Church by Pius V in 1567, and proclaimed by Leo XIII in Aeterni Patris (1879) the model of all Catholic philosophy and theology — the foundation of the Thomist revival of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The intellectual achievement of Thomas Aquinas has been compared to that of any thinker in the European tradition. The Summa Theologiae alone runs to some two million words across more than five hundred questions; the corpus as a whole — the disputed questions, the commentaries on twelve books of Aristotle, the biblical commentaries, the Eucharistic hymns including the Pange Lingua and the Lauda Sion, the treatise De Regno on political theory, the philosophical opuscules — is far larger. The system he constructed, in which Aristotelian philosophy is integrated with Christian revelation by way of distinctions between nature and grace, reason and faith, essence and existence, became, after centuries of contested partial acceptance, the most influential single body of thought in Catholic intellectual history. Dante put him in the heaven of the Sun in the Paradiso, surrounded by the great theologians; Joyce in A Portrait of the Artist took the Thomist aesthetics as the framework of his own theory of art. The Angelic Doctor outlived his enemies, and most of his admirers.
