Julia the Elder

39 BC – AD 14 · only daughter of Augustus, mother of the Julian line

Julia the Elder, born in late 39 BC at Rome and dead in exile at Rhegium in the autumn of AD 14, was the only natural child of Augustus and the central female figure of the long and unhappy Augustan succession. Three times married, three times widowed in the political sense — twice by death and once by her father's banishment of her — she was the mother of five children by her second husband Agrippa, three of whom her father adopted in his own name as candidates for the principate, and the grandmother through her daughter Agrippina the Elder of the emperor Caligula and the empress Agrippina the Younger and, through her, of the emperor Nero. The Julio-Claudian house from AD 37 onward was descended biologically from her, through Agrippina the Elder; her father's house ran on through her, but not through her father.

She was the daughter of Augustus by his second wife Scribonia, married for a year at the time of the Treaty of Misenum in 39 BC for political reasons connected with the elder Pompey's surviving family and divorced on the day Julia was born. Augustus took his daughter at once from her mother and gave her to be raised in his own household; he married Livia three days later. Julia was thus raised, from her infancy, in the increasingly imperial house on the Palatine, under the supervision of Livia and the education of Augustus himself, who was famously strict about the spinning and weaving and household economy of his daughter and granddaughters in a way that struck contemporaries as a touch reactionary even at the time. She was an able and well-educated young woman; Macrobius preserves a small file of her witticisms that are, even at second hand, the wit of a sharp and unsentimental Roman matron of the late Republic.

She was married three times. The first marriage, at fourteen, was to her first cousin Marcus Claudius Marcellus, the son of Octavia and the first designated heir of Augustus's principate. The marriage lasted two years; Marcellus died of fever at Baiae in 23 BC. The widow was then about sixteen.

Her second marriage was to Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, Augustus's oldest political partner and the most powerful subordinate of the regime, twenty-five years her senior and at the time of the marriage in 21 BC already married to her cousin Claudia Marcella the Elder, the daughter of Octavia by her first husband, who was sent away by imperial command so that Agrippa could marry Augustus's daughter and become, in effect, the bridge of the succession. The marriage lasted nine years and produced five children: Gaius Caesar, born 20 BC, and Lucius Caesar, born 17 BC, both adopted by Augustus as his sons in the year of Lucius's birth and groomed openly as joint heirs; Julia the Younger, born about 19 BC, who would be exiled to Trimerus for adultery in AD 8 and die there in AD 28; Agrippina the Elder, born about 14 BC, who would marry Germanicus, accompany her husband to the Rhine and to Antioch, attend his death, raise his children including Caligula, and die herself of forced starvation in exile on Pandateria in AD 33; and Postumus Agrippa, born in 12 BC after his father's death, exiled to Planasia and executed at the beginning of Tiberius's reign in AD 14. Julia accompanied Agrippa on his eastern command from 17 to 13 BC; she travelled with him through Asia and the East and held court at Mytilene and Ilium and Samos. Agrippa died at Campania in March of 12 BC. The widow was about twenty-seven.

Her third marriage was to Tiberius, the elder of her two stepbrothers, in 11 BC. The marriage was politically arranged by Augustus, who required Tiberius to divorce his much-loved wife Vipsania Agrippina, the daughter of Agrippa by an earlier marriage, in order to marry Julia. Tiberius did so under duress; the famous Roman anecdote of his subsequent encounter in the street with Vipsania, after which he reportedly burst into tears and was thereafter never permitted to see her again, dates from this period. The marriage with Julia was unhappy on both sides. They had one child, a son who died in infancy. In 6 BC Tiberius withdrew from political life and retired to Rhodes for what became seven years of self-imposed exile.

In the autumn of 2 BC, in a great public scandal whose details ancient sources record with relish and whose underlying political causes have been disputed since, Augustus issued an edict in the Senate exposing his daughter as an adulteress on a scale that, by the strict moral legislation Augustus himself had pushed through twenty years earlier, was a capital offense. A list of her lovers was named, including Iullus Antonius, the son of Mark Antony, her cousin by marriage, who had been raised in Octavia's house alongside her; several others of senatorial rank were named as well. Iullus Antonius was forced to take his own life, the others banished. Julia, by the lex Julia de adulteriis that her father had himself written, would have faced the loss of half her property and exile; Augustus had her banished to the small volcanic island of Pandateria, off the coast of Campania, where she was kept for five years under conditions of severe restriction — no wine, no male visitors, no companions but her mother Scribonia, who voluntarily went with her into exile. The cause of the rupture remains disputed: ancient sources attribute it to adultery, modern historians have variously argued for an attempted political conspiracy involving Iullus Antonius as a possible alternative regent, but on the evidence the case cannot be settled.

In AD 4 she was moved to a more comfortable residence at Rhegium on the toe of Italy. She remained there for the rest of her life. Augustus refused to see her or to allow her to be mentioned in his presence again; he is reported to have spoken of her, in private, only as a sickness in the house. Tiberius, who had divorced her formally on his return from Rhodes in AD 2, took no public position. Her son Postumus Agrippa was banished in his turn in AD 7 and killed in AD 14 at the beginning of Tiberius's reign; her daughter Julia the Younger was banished in AD 8. By the time of Augustus's own death in AD 14, of her five children only Agrippina the Elder remained in good standing, married to Germanicus and far away on the Rhine.

Augustus on his deathbed instructed Tiberius to make no provision for Julia from his estate; Tiberius, when he became emperor, reduced her allowance further. She died at Rhegium a few months after her father, in the autumn of AD 14, of malnutrition. She was about fifty-three. Her grandchildren by Agrippina the Elder — Caligula, Agrippina the Younger, Drusilla, Julia Livilla, Nero Julius Caesar, Drusus Caesar — would, in the next two generations, hold the principate and rule the Roman world. The daughter that Augustus disowned and starved was the only channel by which his blood line entered the imperial succession at all.

The case against Julia in the ancient sources is severe and consistent. The case for her, which a few moderns have made, rests on the political circumstances of 2 BC: she was a thirty-seven-year-old widow of Agrippa and an estranged wife of Tiberius, with two sons in their teens approaching the age at which they could carry the principate, and her father was sixty-one and visibly aging. The adulterer Iullus Antonius held the consulship the year before. If a conspiracy was being formed to substitute Iullus and Julia for Augustus and his heirs, with Julia's adult sons as the long-term cover for the new arrangement, the political explanation of the rupture would be coherent. But the surviving sources are unanimous in casting the case as one of sexual scandal alone, and the question is unlikely now to be settled. What survived in the public memory was the figure of the emperor's daughter raised to the height of moral instruction and brought down for the most public of moral failures — the punishment laid down by her father's own law, executed on his own child.

The road continues to Tiberius.
