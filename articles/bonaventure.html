Bonaventure

c. 1217 – 1274, the Seraphic Doctor, Italian Franciscan, minister general of the Friars Minor, biographer of Francis of Assisi, cardinal of Albano

Giovanni di Fidanza was born about 1217 at Bagnoregio in southern Tuscany, then a small town of the Papal States set on a tufa ridge between Orvieto and Viterbo. His father, also Giovanni di Fidanza, was a physician of modest means; his mother Maria di Ritella, according to the family tradition that he himself preserved, vowed her child to Saint Francis during a serious illness in his infancy, and from the cure dated his later name in religion: Bonaventura, "good fortune". Whether Francis himself ever blessed the boy is a pious accretion of the next century, but the family's devotion to the new Franciscan order, which had received papal confirmation only the year before his birth, is securely attested.

About 1235 he was sent north to the arts faculty at Paris, then the largest university in Latin Christendom, and there he heard the lectures of the secular master Alexander of Hales. When Alexander, well past sixty, took the Franciscan habit in 1236 and brought his chair with him, he founded what became the Franciscan studium at Paris; Bonaventure himself entered the order about 1243, by which date he had finished the arts course and was studying theology. Alexander, John of La Rochelle, Odo Rigaldus, and William of Middleton were his masters in succession. He read the Sentences as bachelor from 1248 to 1252 — the commentary survives and contains the substance of his theological system — and was licensed master in 1253. By that date the secular masters of the university, led by William of Saint-Amour, had begun the campaign to bar the mendicants from chairs in the faculty of theology, and Bonaventure was kept from his by a four-year dispute that was settled only by papal intervention in 1257.

On 2 February 1257, before he had occupied the chair he had at last been granted, Bonaventure was elected minister general of the Order of Friars Minor at the Roman chapter held at the Ara Coeli. He was thirty-nine, the seventh successor of Francis, and the office that came to him was in serious disarray. Francis had died in 1226; his original rule of evangelical poverty had been glossed and softened by successive popes; a movement of "Spiritual" Franciscans, drawing on the apocalyptic chronology of the Calabrian abbot Joachim of Fiore, demanded a literal return to the founder's poverty and proclaimed an imminent age of the Holy Spirit in which the Roman hierarchy would be superseded; an opposite faction, the so-called Conventuals, accepted property held in trust by the papacy for the order and accommodated to the requirements of urban preaching and the universities. Bonaventure's seventeen years as minister general were spent governing this divided body. He visited the provinces, regularised the constitutions at the chapter of Narbonne in 1260, deposed the Joachimite minister general John of Parma whom he succeeded, and pursued and silenced the more extreme Spirituals; at the same time he resisted any move to reduce the order to a clerical congregation indistinguishable from the older monastic orders. The compromise he struck — corporate poverty and personal austerity, but use of universities, settled houses, libraries, and the patronage of kings and popes — held the order together for two generations and was the foundation of the moderate Franciscanism that survived to the fifteenth century.

The chapter of Narbonne charged him also with the writing of a definitive life of Francis to replace the half-dozen lives already in circulation, several of them by close companions of the founder. The Legenda Maior, finished about 1263, was a polished hagiography that elided most of the conflicts of the early order and presented Francis as the saint of obedience to the Church and of mystical conformity to Christ; the chapter of Paris in 1266 ordered the destruction of all earlier lives so that this one alone should remain. The order was effectively unanimous in this for two centuries, with the result that the writings of Thomas of Celano and Leo of Assisi were largely lost until the nineteenth century. Bonaventure's portrait of Francis — the saint marked with the stigmata at La Verna, the lover of all created things who preached to the birds and the wolf of Gubbio, the new Christ of the western Church — became the canonical image.

His theological and philosophical work was written in the same crowded years. The Breviloquium of about 1257 is a compact systematic exposition of the faith for the schools of the order; the Itinerarium Mentis in Deum, written in 1259 during a retreat on Mount La Verna where Francis had received the stigmata, is a brief masterpiece in seven chapters that traces the ascent of the soul to God through the contemplation of creatures, then of the soul itself, then of the divine names of being and the good, and finally of the wordless ecstasy of mystical union. Where Thomas Aquinas, at the same desks of Paris, was integrating Aristotle with Christian doctrine in the long impersonal arc of the Summa, Bonaventure remained an Augustinian and a Platonist, refusing the eternal world of the philosophers, holding that all knowledge presupposes divine illumination, and treating natural reason as an opening into a contemplative ascent that culminates in love rather than in science. The two men respected each other; the Dominican Thomas and the Franciscan Bonaventure were licensed master on the same day in 1257 by the intervention of Pope Alexander IV, and Bonaventure later told the chronicler Salimbene that he would not exchange his own learning for that of "Brother Thomas".

His Collations on the Hexaemeron, delivered to the Paris students in the spring of 1273, were left unfinished at the twenty-third lecture: in May of that year Pope Gregory X named him cardinal bishop of Albano and summoned him to help prepare the general council that was to meet at Lyon in 1274 on the union of the Latin and Greek churches and a new crusade. The story that the legates from the pope, finding Bonaventure washing dishes in his convent garden when they arrived with the cardinal's hat, were told to hang it on a tree branch until he had finished, is preserved by the early Franciscan chroniclers and may be true. He resigned the office of minister general at the Pentecost chapter of 1274 and went on to Lyon. He preached the opening sermon of the council on 7 May, took the principal part in the negotiations that produced the brief and brittle reunion of the Greek and Latin churches, and died at Lyon on the night of 14–15 July 1274, of an illness which the bull of his canonisation a century later, and the more clinical chroniclers, do not name. He was buried in the Franciscan church at Lyon; the tomb was destroyed and the relics scattered by the Calvinists in 1562, except for the head, which survived until the French Revolution.

Sixtus IV, himself a Franciscan, canonised him in 1482. Sixtus V, also a Franciscan, declared him a Doctor of the Church in 1588, with the title Doctor Seraphicus, by which he had been called among his own order since his own century. Dante, in the Paradiso, set him in the heaven of the Sun beside Thomas Aquinas, the Dominican praising the Franciscan and the Franciscan praising the Dominican — the literary monument of the two great schools of the thirteenth century, and of the friendship that knit their founders together.
