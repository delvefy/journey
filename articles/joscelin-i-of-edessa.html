Joscelin I of Edessa

c. 1070 – September 1131, count of Edessa 1118–1131, founder of the Courtenay county

Joscelin was born about 1070 at the small castle of Courtenay in the Gâtinais, south of Paris, into the Frankish baronial family of the lords of Courtenay. He was a younger son and would in the ordinary course have served as a household knight in one of the great fiefs of central France, but the call of the First Crusade in 1095 turned over the lives of dozens of younger sons in his generation. Joscelin did not go on the First Crusade itself — he was a younger man than most of its leaders and lacked the means for the early outfit — but in the spring of 1101, in the second wave of the great pilgrim armies, he travelled east with his elder cousin Baldwin of Bourcq, whose grandmother had been a Courtenay. They reached Constantinople in the summer of 1101, crossed Anatolia in the great northern column under Stephen of Blois, were caught in the disasters of the Anatolian crossings in which most of that army was lost to the Seljuks of Rûm, and arrived in Antioch in the autumn of 1101 with a small surviving following.

His career was made by his cousin Baldwin. Baldwin had received the county of Edessa in 1100 on the elevation of his cousin and namesake, Baldwin I, to the kingship of Jerusalem; in 1101 he gave Joscelin the lordship of Tell Bashir, the second strongest fortress of the county, with the right to gather his own following of Latin and Armenian knights. The two lordships, Edessa held by Baldwin and Tell Bashir held by Joscelin, formed the political and military spine of the Latin Christian presence east of the Euphrates for the next thirty years. They campaigned together, against the Seljuks of Mosul and the Turcoman emirs of the Diyarbakir hills, in the early 1100s, with mixed results: in May 1104 the two of them, with Bohemond of Antioch and the prince Tancred, fought the great Battle of Harran against the army of Mosul and were comprehensively defeated. Baldwin and Joscelin, riding in the rearguard, were taken prisoner together by the atabeg Jikirmish, kept in the citadel of Mosul, and only released, by separate ransoms, between 1107 and 1108.

Joscelin returned to Tell Bashir to find that his cousin Tancred, regent of Antioch, had occupied his lordship in his absence and was reluctant to surrender it. He resorted, in the end, to a formal complaint to King Baldwin I of Jerusalem, who arrived in person, ordered the restitution, and afterwards summoned Joscelin south to Jerusalem on the more important business of the kingdom. From 1113 to 1118 Joscelin spent most of his time in Palestine; he was given the seigneurie of Tiberias as a fief of the kingdom, fought against Toghtekin of Damascus in the wars of those years, and was for a time considered the most capable lieutenant of the king. Baldwin I died of dysentery in April 1118 on the return from his Egyptian expedition; Baldwin of Bourcq, his cousin and Joscelin’s, was elected king of Jerusalem in his place; the new king at once restored Joscelin to the county of Edessa, vacated by his own elevation. Joscelin was about forty-eight when he received it. He held it for thirteen years.

The county he governed was poor, scattered, and exposed. The Christian population was overwhelmingly Armenian and Syriac; the Latin element was a thin layer of soldiers and clergy concentrated in Edessa and the river fortresses. Joscelin, who had spent thirty years on the frontier, knew the languages and the politics; he married an Armenian princess, Beatrice, the daughter of the lord Constantine of Vahka, and through her became the brother-in-law of half the Armenian baronage of the Cilician mountains. He kept Tell Bashir as his preferred residence even after his accession to Edessa — the city of Edessa lay too far east and was politically unreliable — and from Tell Bashir conducted, year after year, the small frontier wars against the Turcoman raiders and the emirs of Aleppo and Mardin.

The most famous episode of his life was his second captivity. In September 1122, on a raid into the Diyarbakir country, his column was ambushed by the Turkic prince Belek Ghazi of the Artuqid house, and he was taken prisoner along with his cousin Galeran, lord of al-Bira. They were held in the high mountain fortress of Kharput in eastern Anatolia. In April 1123 King Baldwin II, riding to release them, was himself ambushed and taken to the same prison; for ten months the king of Jerusalem and the count of Edessa shared a cell. In August 1123, by an intrigue Armenian historians attribute partly to chance and partly to the secret help of a guard the prisoners had bribed, fifty Armenians of Kharput took the citadel from the inside, killed the garrison, and freed the prisoners. The king and the count then quarrelled over the right course: Baldwin proposed to hold the citadel until a relieving force could arrive; Joscelin urged him to escape at once. The king agreed to remain; Joscelin slipped out by night, swam the Tigris with the help of two skin floats, made his way across the country, and arrived at Tell Bashir to organise a relief. Belek, however, returned to Kharput within days, mined the unprovisioned citadel, and took it before any relief could reach it; the freed prisoners, except Baldwin, were beheaded; the king was returned to a stricter confinement and not ransomed for another year. The episode was the celebrated story of the early Latin East: a king and a count, both prisoners, both freed, only one escaped.

Joscelin’s death in September 1131 was no less in the spirit of the chivalric anecdote. Besieging a small castle in the upper Euphrates country, he went out by night to inspect a sap his engineers were driving under the wall; the work collapsed and he was crushed under the spoil. His followers dug him out alive but with a broken back and chest. He was carried in a horse-litter back toward Aleppo; on the way he received word that Belek’s nephew, the emir Dawud of Hisn Kayfa, was approaching with a relieving column. Joscelin called his men together, refused to be moved to a safer location, had his litter carried to the head of his army, and waited. Dawud’s force, seeing the count alive at the head of his troops, withdrew without engagement. Joscelin lived a few hours longer, embraced his only son, the future Joscelin II, and died at the head of his army in the open field. He was about sixty-one. The county he had built, in the small hands of his son, would not survive him by twenty years.
