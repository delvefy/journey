Alexios III Angelos

c. 1153 – 1211, Byzantine emperor 1195–1203

Alexios was born about 1153, the eldest surviving son of the sebastos Andronikos Angelos Doukas and Euphrosyne Kastamonitissa. The Angeloi were a Philadelphian provincial family raised to the court by the marriage of his grandmother Theodora into the Komnenian house; he was therefore, like his younger brother Isaac, a great-grandson of Alexios I Komnenos on the female line. He served without distinction as a soldier in the late wars of Manuel I, was suspected of conspiracy under Andronikos I, and after his brother’s accession in 1185 lived for ten years on the periphery of the court, watched but unemployed. In the spring of 1195 he accompanied Isaac on a hunting expedition near Kypsella in Thrace; on 8 April, with the connivance of a faction of the imperial guard, he had himself acclaimed emperor while Isaac was at the hunt, the surgeons of his suite blinded his brother in the imperial tent, and Alexios returned to Constantinople as emperor.

He was forty-two and had nothing of the energy the moment required. The reign of eight years that followed was a continuous, undignified retreat. The treasury he inherited was empty; to fill it he debased the coinage, sold offices openly, and instituted a special tax, the Alamanikon, to pay off the Emperor Henry VI of Hohenstaufen, who in 1196 had demanded a vast tribute as the price for not invading the empire on behalf of his Norman wife. The death of Henry in September 1197 spared him the German invasion but not the humiliation of having raised the money for it. In the same years the second Bulgarian Empire under the brothers Asen and Peter, and after their successive assassinations under the Tsar Kaloyan, made itself permanent on the empire’s northern frontier. Campaigns against them in 1196 and 1199 were broken off without engagement. In Anatolia the Seljuk frontier crept westward; the Pontic city of Trebizond, where in 1204 the grandsons of Andronikos I would found an independent successor state, was already in semi-revolt before the end of the century.

His domestic position rested on the dynasty of his wife, the empress Euphrosyne Doukaina Kamatera, a woman of force whose four daughters by the emperor were the chief political assets of the regime. Eirene, the eldest, was married first to Roger III of Sicily and then, after Roger’s early death, to the German king Philip of Swabia, the brother of Henry VI and the leader of the Hohenstaufen interest in Europe; the marriage placed a daughter of Constantinople at the centre of the imperial succession in Germany. Anna, the second, was married to Theodore Laskaris, an officer of the court who would later, in exile after 1204, found the Empire of Nicaea and through her become the dynastic founder of the Greek state that recovered the capital in 1261. Eudokia, the third, was married first to Stefan Nemanjić of Raška, the future first king of Serbia, and then, after her repudiation, to the courtier Alexios Doukas, called Mourtzouphlos for his bushy eyebrows, who would in February 1204 strangle Alexios IV and reign for six weeks as Alexios V before the city fell. The four marriages were the principal political instrument of the reign and remain the principal reason its memory has survived.

The empress herself directed much of the administration. In 1197 a court intrigue accused her of an affair with the protostrator Vatatzes; the emperor had Vatatzes executed, sent his wife to a convent on the Princes’ Islands, and ruled alone for some months before public opinion and his own indecision restored her. From then on the chroniclers describe a reign in which the empress, her circle, and the emperor’s nephew-in-law and chief minister Alexios Palaiologos managed the day-to-day work of government while Alexios himself amused himself with the buildings and ceremonies of the palace.

The catastrophe of 1203 arose from his earlier choice not to molest his blind brother or his brother’s son. The boy Alexios, son of the deposed Isaac, had been kept in modest confinement at court since the coup; in the autumn of 1201 he escaped Constantinople, possibly with Pisan help, and made his way to the court of his sister Eirene and her husband Philip of Swabia. At Philip’s request the marquess Boniface of Montferrat, then negotiating with the Venetians for the leadership of the assembling Fourth Crusade, took up the boy’s cause; the crusader fleet under the Doge Enrico Dandolo arrived in the Bosphorus on 23 June 1203. Alexios III refused negotiation, refused battle, and refused to organise the defence of the city beyond a passive holding of the walls. After the crusaders forced the Golden Horn, broke the harbour boom, and on 17 July assaulted the Blachernae quarter, the emperor gathered the imperial treasury, his daughter Eirene the younger, and a small suite of officials, and on the night of 17–18 July rode out of the city by the Selymbria gate. He left his wife, his other daughters, his courtiers, and his blind brother behind.

The next years were a fugitive’s circuit. He travelled to Mosynopolis in Thrace, where in the autumn of 1204 he was joined by Mourtzouphlos, his son-in-law and the murderer of Alexios IV, then himself in flight from the Latin sack of Constantinople; Alexios III blinded him at dinner and turned him over to the Latins, who threw him from the column of Theodosius. He then moved to Thessalonica, where he was for a time the prisoner-guest of Boniface of Montferrat, by then the king of the new Latin Kingdom of Thessalonica; freed in 1205 on the payment of a ransom by his nephew Michael Doukas of Epirus, he travelled by way of Epirus to the court of the Seljuk Sultan Kaykhusraw I of Rum, whom he persuaded to take up his cause against his son-in-law Theodore Laskaris, now emperor at Nicaea. The Seljuk invasion of Bithynia ended at the Battle of Antioch on the Meander in the spring of 1211, where the Sultan was killed in personal combat by Theodore Laskaris and Alexios was taken prisoner. He was confined in the monastery of the Hyakinthos at Nicaea, where he died, almost sixty, in 1211 or early 1212.

His legacy lay in the marriages he had made for his daughters. The empire he had ruled was gone; the Greek successor states of Nicaea and Epirus, the Latin Empire of Constantinople, and the recovered empire of 1261 all carried, in different ways, the dynastic lines of those four marriages. Of his own rule no contemporary, friendly or hostile, had anything kind to say.
