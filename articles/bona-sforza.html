Bona Sforza

1494 – 1557, Milanese princess and Queen of Poland

Bona Sforza was born on 2 February 1494 at Vigevano in Lombardy, an event that her father Gian Galeazzo, the nominal Duke of Milan, would not live to celebrate; he died, almost certainly poisoned, in October of the same year, leaving the duchy to his usurping uncle Ludovico il Moro. Bona's mother Isabella of Aragon, daughter of King Alfonso II of Naples and one of the most cultivated and politically formidable women of her generation, retreated south to the duchy of Bari, which the Aragonese had carved out for her, and raised her surviving daughter there. Bona's education was the best Renaissance Italy could provide: Latin, Greek, music, law, dancing, the politics of dozens of small Italian courts seen from the wings. She grew into a striking and shrewd young woman with a portrait by Bartolomeo Veneto that captured both. She was also, by the early 1510s, one of the most eligible princesses south of the Alps.

She was married in 1518 to King Sigismund I of Poland — a widower, twenty-seven years older than she was — in a match arranged by the Emperor Maximilian I as part of his broader Habsburg negotiations after the Congress of Vienna of 1515. Bona crossed the Alps in a procession of unprecedented size with chests of cash, a fully Italian court, gardeners with seeds, cooks with recipes, secretaries, lawyers, a physician, and an explicit programme of cultural and political modernisation. She was crowned at Kraków in April 1518.

What she did over the next thirty years rearranged the Polish monarchy. As queen consort she built a personal patrimony that came to rival the king's: she bought up royal lands offered as security for debts, she ran her estates with Italian methods and Italian intendants, she lent money to magnates on the security of further estates, and by the 1540s she was probably the richest single landholder in the kingdom. The fortune was not for display. It financed her political programme: the elevation of her own son to the throne, the building of her own faction in the Sejm and the senate, the reduction of any magnate house that opposed her.

The first half of the programme — the dynastic insurance — she pushed through in 1530, when she had her son Sigismund II Augustus crowned junior king during his father's lifetime, the practice known as vivente rege. The Polish nobility tolerated it as a one-off and afterwards swore never to permit it again, but Bona had what she wanted: her son's accession would not depend on any subsequent election. She also fought, throughout the 1520s and 1530s, against the Habsburg orientation that her husband and his chancery preferred. The first marriage of her son, to Elizabeth of Habsburg in 1543, she opposed; the unhappiness of the bride and the early death she suffered in Vilnius confirmed Bona's reputation, fairly or not, as an enemy who acted by poison.

She brought to Poland a Renaissance court that her contemporaries found astonishing. She remodelled the Wawel Castle in Kraków with Italian masters; she introduced or popularised vegetables that to this day in Polish are called włoszczyzna, “Italian things” — leeks, carrots, cauliflower, celery, parsley; she sponsored Italian musicians and the building of gardens on the Italian model; she patronised humanists and brought architects from Bari and Milan into the kingdom. The cultural footprint of her reign survived the political enmity she generated and is still visible in Polish food, in the masonry of Kraków, and in the Italian vocabulary of Polish court life.

The political enmity, however, was profound. She accumulated enemies methodically — the great magnates, the Habsburg-leaning bishops, the king's chancellor Krzysztof Szydłowiecki for years, the Lithuanian Radziwiłłs at the end. When the king died in 1548 and her son became sole monarch, her power crumbled. Sigismund Augustus had married, in secret and against her wishes, the young Lithuanian Calvinist widow Barbara Radziwiłł. Bona detested the match, the family, and the religion. She fought publicly against the queen's coronation, then was reported — though never proved — to have poisoned Barbara during her final illness in 1551. From that point her son could no longer bear her at court. She withdrew to her own estates in Mazovia and waited.

In February 1556 she gathered her cash and her movable jewels and travelled south to Bari, the duchy that was now legally hers by inheritance from her mother. She had Polish escort, papal escort, and Habsburg safe-conduct. With her she took an enormous sum of ready money, much of which she lent the following year to her old Habsburg correspondents — Philip II of Spain, the new master of Naples and Milan — on the security of formal royal bonds. The loans came to be called the Neapolitan Sums (sumy neapolitańskie), and Philip's heirs never repaid them; for two centuries the Polish kings would press the claim and the Spanish crown would politely defer.

She did not live long in Italy. On 19 November 1557 she died at Bari, suddenly. Her own trusted secretary, Gian Lorenzo Pappacoda, was widely believed at the time and afterwards to have poisoned her on behalf of Philip II, who stood to escape repayment of the Neapolitan Sums if she died before her own will could be executed. She was sixty-three. She had named her four surviving daughters as her heirs, but in the confusion of the death-bed Pappacoda produced a will of his own in her name, naming Philip as the principal heir and himself as executor. Litigation continued for decades. Bona was buried in the Basilica of San Nicola at Bari, where her splendid Renaissance tomb still stands.

The verdict on her in Polish historiography fluctuated for centuries between the hostile — the foreign queen, the schemer, the Italian poisoner — and the appreciative — the moderniser, the patron, the political intelligence who built Sigismund Augustus's reign. The appreciative judgment has prevailed in modern scholarship. Whatever her enemies said of her, the country she left in 1556 was politically and culturally a different country than the one she had entered in 1518.
