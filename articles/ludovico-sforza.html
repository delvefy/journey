Ludovico Sforza

1452 – 1508, Duke of Milan, patron of Leonardo and Bramante, the prince whose invitation brought the French armies into Italy

Ludovico Maria Sforza, called il Moro from his dark complexion and the mulberry tree in his emblem, was born at Vigevano on 27 July 1452, the fourth son of Francesco Sforza, the condottiere who had seized Milan in 1450 from the failing Visconti, and of Bianca Maria Visconti, Filippo Maria Visconti's daughter. His father's career was the founding myth of the dynasty: a soldier of fortune who married into the old ducal house and made himself prince of one of the richest states in Europe. Ludovico grew up in the new humanist court at Pavia, was taught Latin, mathematics, and statecraft, and was given as appanage the duchy of Bari in southern Italy, an inheritance from his mother. He saw little of government in his youth; the duchy passed in 1466 to his elder brother Galeazzo Maria.

Galeazzo Maria was murdered in the cathedral of Milan on St Stephen's Day 1476 by three young men of his court, leaving an eight-year-old son, Gian Galeazzo. The regency went to the boy's mother, Bona of Savoy, with the secretary Cicco Simonetta as the real ruler of state. Ludovico, returning from a brief exile, manoeuvred with extraordinary patience against this arrangement. In 1480 he had Simonetta arrested, tried, and beheaded; in 1481 he established himself as the boy duke's guardian and effective sovereign of Milan. The young Gian Galeazzo was kept in pleasant seclusion at the castle of Pavia with his Aragonese wife Isabella of Naples; Ludovico, while formally only regent, exercised every actual power of the duke.

His ducal rule, from 1480 to 1499, was the high point of fifteenth-century Italian princely culture. The court at Milan and Pavia drew artists, engineers, scholars, and musicians from across Italy. In 1482, in response to a famous Latin letter of self-recommendation, Ludovico hired the young Florentine Leonardo da Vinci, who would spend almost two decades in his service, painting the Last Supper in the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie, designing the never-cast bronze horse for an equestrian monument to Francesco Sforza, decorating the Sala delle Asse with painted plane trees, and filling notebooks with hydraulic and military engineering for the duke. Donato Bramante was brought from Urbino and rebuilt the choir of Santa Maria delle Grazie and the chapter house of Sant'Ambrogio in the new classical manner he would later carry to Rome. The chancellor and humanist Bartolomeo Calco organised the administration. The court orator Bernardo Bellincioni wrote masques and verse; the architect Cristoforo Solari built tombs; Franchino Gaffurio was the chief musician.

In 1491 Ludovico married Beatrice d'Este, fifteen years old, the daughter of Ercole I d'Este of Ferrara and the sister of Isabella d'Este, marchioness of Mantua. The marriage was a triumph of Renaissance court culture. Beatrice, witty, energetic, almost theatrically self-conscious as a duchess, set the fashions of half of Italy: she gave Ludovico two sons, Ercole Massimiliano and Francesco; she organised the festivities and the gardens at the Sforzesco Castle in Milan and at the country palace of Vigevano; she was painted by Leonardo's circle and sculpted in her tomb effigy by Cristoforo Solari. She died in childbirth on 2 January 1497, at twenty-one, leaving Ludovico, by all reports, broken with grief.

His foreign policy was the disaster of his reign and of Italy. Threatened with the Neapolitan claims of Isabella of Naples, his nephew's wife, against him, and unable to count on the Medici of Florence after Lorenzo's death in 1492, Ludovico in 1493 began to encourage Charles VIII of France to revive the old Angevin claim to the kingdom of Naples. The hope was that a French expedition would frighten Naples into withdrawing its support for Gian Galeazzo. The expedition came. In September 1494 Charles VIII crossed the Alps with a modern army of eighteen thousand men and the artillery train that ended the equilibrium of fifteenth-century Italian warfare. He passed through Milan, where Gian Galeazzo Sforza died, conveniently for his uncle, in October; Ludovico assumed the ducal title at last on 22 October. The French marched south, took Naples without much fighting, and triggered the formation of the League of Venice in March 1495, which Ludovico himself now joined against the French he had invited.

The same year his diplomatic standing reached its high point. In March 1495 the new Emperor Maximilian I invested him with the duchy of Milan in his own right, ending the legal anomaly of his rule; the price was the betrothal of Ludovico's niece Bianca Maria Sforza to the emperor, with an enormous dowry, and a permanent Sforza-Habsburg alignment that would have many later echoes. But the French were not done with Italy. In 1498 Louis XII succeeded Charles VIII in France; Louis was the grandson of Valentina Visconti and so claimed Milan in his own person. In the summer of 1499 he invaded Lombardy with a larger army; Ludovico's Swiss mercenaries refused to fight, his subjects, taxed and tired, opened the gates of Milan to the French, and on 2 September he fled north over the Alps to the imperial court.

He raised an army of Swiss the following spring and returned, retaking Milan in February 1500. The reprieve was brief. In April his army met a French force at Novara, and his own Swiss soldiers, faced with the larger Swiss contingent in French pay, refused to fight their compatriots. He tried to slip out disguised as a common soldier and was recognised and handed over. Louis XII sent him to France as a prisoner. He was held first at the château of Pierre-Scize at Lyon, then at Lys-Saint-Georges, and from 1504 at the great fortress of Loches in Touraine. He spent eight years there, in a chamber painted with his own emblems and Latin mottoes that can still be seen on the walls. He died at Loches on 27 May 1508 and was buried at the priory of Saint-Ours nearby. His sons Ercole Massimiliano and Francesco, raised at the Habsburg court, would each in turn briefly recover Milan in the Italian Wars before the duchy passed permanently to the empire.

His reputation has been hard on him. Machiavelli, who hated the French invasion above all things in recent history, treated him in the Prince and the Discourses as the type of the irresponsible Italian prince who had ruined his country for short-term advantage. Guicciardini agreed. The verdict has stood. The other side of the ledger — the artistic flowering of Milan under his rule, the engineering improvements to the Lombard canals and the silk industry, the systematic patronage of every major figure of the high Italian Renaissance — survives in the works themselves and in the city Leonardo and Bramante helped to shape, but it has never quite balanced the political catastrophe he set in train.
