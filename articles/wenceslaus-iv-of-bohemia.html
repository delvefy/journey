Wenceslaus IV of Bohemia

1361 – 1419, King of Bohemia, deposed King of the Romans, king of the Hussite spring

Wenceslaus was born at Nuremberg on 26 February 1361, the eldest son of the Emperor Charles IV by his third wife Anna of Schweidnitz, and the heir on whom his father concentrated his dynastic ambitions for the Empire. He was crowned King of Bohemia at two, in his cradle, by way of associated rule with his father; King of the Romans at fifteen, on 10 June 1376, in an election Charles IV had spent the previous five years preparing; and succeeded his father in November 1378, seventeen years old, with the Empire and the Czech kingdom as his entire inheritance. The succession was the high point of Luxembourg dynastic engineering and of his own promise; nothing afterwards lived up to it.

His personal character was, by every contemporary account, the inverse of his father's: he was indolent, given to long bouts of drinking, easily distracted by hunting, prone to spectacular fits of temper alternating with passive withdrawal. His first wife Joanna of Bavaria, whom he had married at twenty in 1370, died young in 1386 of an attack supposedly inflicted upon her by his hunting dogs in a notorious incident at Karlštejn; his second wife Sophia of Bavaria, taken in 1389, lived with him until his death but never had children. He had no legitimate issue.

His central problem was the Western Schism, which had opened the year of his accession with the disputed election of the Roman Urban VI and the Avignon Clement VII. Charles IV in his final months had supported Urban as the legitimate pope; Wenceslaus continued that policy without enthusiasm, recognising Urban and his successors at Rome through the 1380s and 1390s but doing little to make the recognition effective. He was, in any case, increasingly absent from the imperial assemblies. The German princes complained that he held no diets, that he did not protect the Rhine roads from the robber knights, that he treated the Empire's business with contempt; the Italian factions, who could no longer find him in Aachen or Nuremberg when they wanted imperial recognition, drifted into their own arrangements. By 1394 his own Bohemian magnates were in open opposition; he was kidnapped by them in May 1394 at Beroun and held for four months at the castle of Wildberg in Upper Austria, released only on terms that made over much of his patronage to the noble council.

The Schism worsened. The Council of Pisa in 1409, summoned by an alliance of French and Italian cardinals on both sides, attempted to depose both rival popes and elect a third, Alexander V. Wenceslaus, persuaded by his confessor Jan of Jenstein and his Czech advisers, recognised Alexander. The German archbishops, all of whom remained loyal to the Roman pope Gregory XII, were furious. On 20 August 1400 the imperial electors, meeting at Lahnstein, had already declared Wenceslaus deposed for, among other things, dissipating the rights of the Empire and failing to suppress the Schism; they elected Rupert of the Palatinate in his place. Rupert was the king the Germans recognised for the next decade; Wenceslaus held only the title and his Bohemian kingdom. After Rupert's death in 1410 the electors chose Wenceslaus's younger half-brother Sigismund as King of the Romans, with Wenceslaus's own formal consent, in return for the recognition that he remained King of Bohemia.

His support of the Pisan pope had a consequence he had not anticipated: it brought into open conflict with him the German party at the University of Prague, which remained loyal to Rome. The dispute over national representation at the university came to a head in January 1409 with his Decree of Kutná Hora, which reversed the voting balance at the university from three German nations to one in favour of the Bohemian nation. The German masters left Prague in the summer of 1409, perhaps a thousand of them, principally for the new University of Leipzig founded for them by the Wettin princes; the empty Prague university was reorganised under Czech leadership, with Jan Hus elected rector in October. The decree gave the Bohemian church and Czech political life a clear national character at a moment when they were also developing a clear reform character, and the two strands — nationalist and religious — would fuse into the Hussite movement of the next decade.

Wenceslaus's protection of Hus through his decade of preaching at Bethlehem Chapel was real but inconstant. He resisted ecclesiastical pressure to silence Hus through 1410 and 1411; he turned against him in 1412 when Hus's sermons against the indulgence sale touched the royal treasury (Wenceslaus took a share of the receipts), but he never agreed to send him to be tried at Rome and allowed him to retire to the country. He attended none of the proceedings of the Council of Constance and made no public protest at Hus's burning in 1415; the protest of the Bohemian and Moravian nobles — the so-called Stížnost of 2 September 1415, with four hundred and fifty-two seals, the largest seal-cluster in any surviving medieval document — was made in his name but without his evident encouragement.

By 1419 the Hussite movement had become a popular insurrection. Open-air preaching, communion in both kinds, the spontaneous gathering of crowds on the hilltops the radicals called by Old Testament names — Tábor on Mount Hazmburk in southern Bohemia would shortly give the movement its hard wing's name — were running outside ecclesiastical control. On 30 July 1419 the radical preacher Jan Želivský led a procession through the New Town of Prague; the procession ended at the New Town Hall, the councillors of which were Royalists; in a confrontation the councillors were thrown from the upper windows of the hall onto the spears of the crowd below. This event, the First Defenestration of Prague, is conventionally taken as the beginning of the Hussite Wars.

Wenceslaus, hearing the news the next day at Nový Hrad outside Kunratice, suffered what contemporary accounts call a "stroke" or "apoplexy" of fury and died on 16 August 1419. He was buried at St Vitus Cathedral, in the tomb his father had prepared. His widow Sophia held the regency on behalf of his half-brother Sigismund for the few months until Sigismund's troops arrived. The interregnum that followed lasted fifteen years.

Wenceslaus IV is, in Czech historical memory, a small and not entirely unsympathetic figure beside the looming statue of his father Charles IV. He inherited at seventeen the most ambitious and best-administered kingdom in central Europe and lost the half of it in his first twenty years; he kept the rest, lost it intermittently to his own magnates, and presided unconsciously over the opening of one of the most original religious and political movements in late medieval Europe. His private life had been a long alcoholic decline; his public life ended in a stroke at the news of a riot. The Bohemian crown he had received from his father passed to his brother Sigismund only nominally and was not effectively occupied again by a king until the 1430s.
