Pope John XXII

c. 1244 – 1334, Jacques Duèze of Cahors, the Avignon pope of the poverty controversy and of the long quarrel with Ludwig the Bavarian

Jacques Duèze was born about 1244 at Cahors in Quercy, the son of a wealthy bourgeois family of cloth merchants and money-lenders. He studied the arts at Cahors and Orléans, the civil and canon law at Montpellier, and theology at Paris, taking the degree of doctor of laws and remaining an exceptionally learned jurist for the rest of his life. He entered service in the household of Charles II of Anjou, king of Naples, and rose through the Angevin administration as a financial expert: he was bishop of Fréjus in 1300, chancellor of Charles II by 1308, bishop of Avignon by 1310, and was made cardinal-bishop of Porto by Clement V in December 1312. He was thus one of the principal Gascon-Provencal-Angevin clergy that had filled the College of Cardinals under Clement; small in stature, lean, with the dry intelligence and inexhaustible patience of an experienced administrator.

The conclave that elected him pope was the longest of the medieval church: it sat for twenty-eight months from the death of Clement V in April 1314 until the cardinals, divided beyond endurance between an Italian party that wished to return to Rome and a French party led by the king's brother that wished to stay in France, finally agreed at Lyon in August 1316 on Duèze as a compromise, on the explicit understanding (which he immediately repudiated) that he would conduct them to Italy. He was crowned at Lyon on 5 September 1316, returned to Avignon at the end of the month, and never left it again. His seventeen-year pontificate fixed Avignon as the residence of the papacy — the apostolic palace was begun under him, the curial bureaucracy was rebuilt around the apostolic camera, the system of papal reservations of benefices (provisions) was extended to most of Europe, and the centralisation of papal taxation became, in his hands, an instrument of financial administration of unprecedented power.

Two great controversies dominated his pontificate: the poverty controversy and the quarrel with Ludwig the Bavarian. The first was theological in form and political in substance. The Spiritual Franciscans, the radical wing of the order, held that Christ and the apostles had owned nothing, neither severally nor in common, and that this absolute poverty was a doctrine of the Gospel. The general chapter of the Franciscans at Perugia in 1322 endorsed this view. John XXII responded in 1323 with the bull Cum Inter Nonnullos, which declared the Spiritual position heretical, and over the following years extended the condemnation to the entire Spiritual party and to its protectors. He further dismantled the legal fiction by which the papacy had since the thirteenth century held nominal ownership of all Franciscan property, in order that the friars might continue to use it without owning it; by the bull Ad Conditorem Canonum of 1322 he restored ownership to the order, thereby converting them from honest poor men into ordinary religious proprietors at a stroke. The minister general of the Franciscans, Michael of Cesena, summoned to Avignon to answer for his order's defence of the Spiritual position, escaped from house arrest in the spring of 1328 and fled north to Ludwig the Bavarian's court in Italy, taking with him William of Ockham and Bonagratia of Bergamo. From the safety of Munich Cesena and Ockham produced over the next dozen years a stream of treatises that argued John XXII was a heretic for his teachings on poverty and on lesser doctrinal points, and called for his deposition.

The political quarrel was with the German king. Ludwig of Bavaria had been elected by four of the seven electors in 1314 against the Habsburg Frederick the Fair; John XXII, exploiting the divided election, claimed that the imperial vicariate over Italy reverted to the papacy in any case of disputed succession, and ordered Ludwig to suspend the exercise of imperial powers until a papal investigation could determine the rightful king. Ludwig, having defeated his rival in battle at Mühldorf in 1322, refused. John excommunicated him in March 1324, deposed him in July 1324, and continued the bulls of deposition through every year of the reign. Ludwig answered with the Sachsenhausen Appellation, declaring John a heretic, and in 1327–1328 descended into Italy, was crowned emperor at Rome by Sciarra Colonna on 17 January 1328, and set up an antipope, the Spiritual Franciscan Nicholas V. The antipope lasted under three years; Nicholas surrendered at Avignon in 1330 and was kept under house arrest in the papal palace for the rest of his life, an object of curiosity. But the constitutional defeat of John's claim was permanent: the Declaration of Rhense of 1338, under his successor Benedict XII, made it imperial law that no king of the Romans required papal confirmation.

His own private theological misadventure was the Beatific Vision controversy. In a series of Advent sermons at Avignon in 1331–1332 he advanced the personal opinion that the souls of the just do not see the divine essence at death but only the humanity of Christ, postponing the full beatific vision to the general resurrection. The proposition was traditional in some patristic sources but contradicted the working theology of his time, was sharply attacked by the Paris theologians and by King Philip VI, and provided the Franciscans with rich new ammunition. He retracted on his deathbed in December 1334 in a written declaration which his successor Benedict XII duly confirmed by the constitution Benedictus Deus in 1336. The episode is among the few in medieval church history in which a sitting pope was effectively obliged to abandon a theological position under doctrinal pressure.

He pursued an active foreign policy through patient diplomacy and unceasing money. He financed the Guelf cities of Tuscany and the Angevin king Robert of Naples against the Ghibelline lords of northern Italy; he supported the kings of France in the early phase of what would become the Hundred Years' War; he financed the Crusade of the Smyrniote League against the Turks in the Aegean; he canonised Thomas Aquinas in 1323 (forty-nine years after his death), the act which closed the older controversies between Augustinians and Aristotelians at Paris and inaugurated the Thomist revival of the later Middle Ages; he created the dioceses of Toulouse, Pamiers, Lombez, and twelve others in southern France; and at his death in 1334 he left the papal treasury, by the most reliable contemporary estimate, with about three quarters of a million gold florins on hand, an unprecedented surplus, the foundation of the financial wealth of the Avignon papacy.

He died on 4 December 1334 at Avignon, aged about ninety, having outlived almost every cardinal who had elected him. His pontificate left two large legacies. The administrative state of the Avignon papacy, with its enormous reach into the benefice system of every Christian kingdom, was his work and outlasted Avignon itself; it carried over to the Renaissance papacy at Rome and remained the basic shape of papal government to the Reformation. The theological-political opposition that he had provoked — the Marsilian and Ockhamist arguments against papal sovereignty, the Franciscan defence of evangelical poverty, the imperial doctrine that the empire stood without papal mediation — was also his work, in the sense that no other pope of the fourteenth century provoked so concentrated a body of reasoned hostility, and that the writings produced in opposition to him — Defensor Pacis, Ockham's Dialogus, the polemical sermons of the dissident friars — carried, in time, into the Reformation and beyond.
