Clare of Assisi

1194 – 1253, founder of the Poor Ladies, abbess of San Damiano, defender of absolute poverty

Chiara di Favarone di Offreduccio was born in Assisi on 16 July 1194, the eldest daughter of a household of the upper nobility of the town. Her father Favarone was a knight of the Offreduccio family, with estates and a tower in the city and lands at Sasso Rosso on the mountain above; her mother Ortolana, of the Fiumi family, was a woman of unusual independent devotion who had made the pilgrimages to Rome, to the Holy Land, and to Santiago de Compostela before her marriage, and brought up her daughters in habits of prayer and almsgiving uncommon even for the period. Clare had at least two younger sisters, Catherine and Beatrice, who would in time follow her. The Offreduccio belonged to the maiores of Assisi, the noble faction that in 1198 was driven from the town by a rising of the commercial commoners; Clare spent some years of her childhood in exile at Perugia, and returned to a city in which the old nobility could no longer count on its prerogatives.

She was eighteen years old in the spring of 1212 when she first heard Francis of Assisi preach the Lenten sermons in the cathedral of San Rufino, two or three hundred yards from her father's house. Francis was about thirty, already five years into the life of voluntary poverty, with a small band of brothers and a verbal papal approval. The two had probably already met in private through the agency of cousins and confessors: a marriage had been proposed for Clare with a young man of her own station, and her resolution to reject it and follow the form of life Francis was preaching was settled before Palm Sunday. On the night of Palm Sunday, 18 March 1212, Clare left her parents' house through a small back door, walked the mile and a half down to the chapel of the Portiuncula in the plain below the town, and was received there by Francis and the brothers. They cut her hair, took her wealthy clothes, and dressed her in a rough penitential tunic. The brothers escorted her to the Benedictine convent of San Paolo delle Abbadesse, and from there a few days later to the smaller house of Sant'Angelo di Panzo, where her sister Catherine, then aged fourteen, ran away to join her. Their uncle Monaldo and the male relatives of the family rode after them with a band of armed retainers and tried to drag Catherine home; she clung to the altar and, the early lives report, became suddenly so heavy that they could not lift her. They left without her. She took the name Agnes.

Francis settled the two sisters, and the small group of women who quickly gathered around them, in the rough buildings beside the country chapel of San Damiano which he had himself rebuilt seven years before. Clare remained there for forty-one years, until her death. She was made abbess by Francis in 1215 against her own reluctance, and never afterwards left the small enclosure of the convent. Her mother Ortolana, after the death of Favarone, came in old age to join her daughters there; the youngest sister Beatrice came at last as well. The community grew slowly — perhaps fifty women by Clare's death — but it was the model on which the female second order of the Franciscan movement, the Damianitae and later the Poor Ladies of San Damiano, took shape across Italy, southern France, Spain, the Empire, and Bohemia.

The principle on which Clare insisted, against the steady resistance of the curia, was absolute corporate poverty: not only personal renunciation by individual sisters but the refusal of the convent itself to own any property, even the buildings in which the sisters lived or the gardens that fed them. This was the rule Francis had given the friars in 1209 and that had already been compromised, with his reluctant assent, in the official rule of 1223. For the female order it was inconvenient on practical grounds — an enclosed contemplative community of women could not, like the friars, beg from door to door — and was uncongenial in principle to the canonists. The Cardinal Hugolino dei Conti di Segni, cardinal-protector of the Franciscan family from 1220, wrote a rule for the Poor Ladies in 1219 based on the Benedictine model, which permitted communal property; Clare accepted it for her own house only on the express stipulation that San Damiano should hold to its older custom. When Hugolino was elected Pope Gregory IX in 1227 he offered her a formal dispensation from any vow of poverty she felt bound by; her reply, preserved in his own bull Sicut Manifestum Est of 17 September 1228, was that she did not wish to be dispensed from following Christ. Gregory granted her in that bull the Privilegium Paupertatis, the unique formal right not to own property in common — a privilege that was, by its nature, never sought again because no one else was willing to live by it.

The Franciscan order, after the death of Francis in 1226, divided rapidly between those who wished to maintain the founder's rule in its severity and those who accepted the modifications the papacy successively imposed. Clare's role in this controversy — conducted from her enclosure, by letters and by visiting brothers — was constant and consistent. She regarded herself as the keeper of Francis's original rule, refused all attempts by Innocent IV in 1247 to substitute a milder code, and in the last year of her life wrote her own rule, the Forma Vitae, the first monastic rule written by a woman that was ever approved by the Roman Church. Innocent IV had ridden up from Perugia to visit her on her deathbed, where the document was approved by his bull of 9 August 1253. She died two days later, on 11 August, holding the parchment in her hands.

Her correspondence with Agnes of Bohemia, the youngest daughter of King Ottokar I, is the principal personal document of her interior life. Agnes had refused royal marriages with two emperors and a king, founded a Poor Ladies' convent at Prague in 1234, and entered it as a sister; over the next twenty years she wrote to Clare and received four surviving letters, in which Clare addressed her as "queen and spouse of the immortal king of glory", and developed an austere mysticism of poverty as the imitation of the poor and crucified Christ. Two further episodes belong to the public record of her abbacy. In September 1240, Saracen mercenaries in the army of Frederick II, then at war with Gregory IX, raided the territory of Assisi and broke into the enclosure of San Damiano. The lives report that Clare, seriously ill, had herself carried to the door of the refectory with a silver ciborium containing the Blessed Sacrament; the raiders, finding her there, withdrew. The next year a similar incursion by an imperial captain named Vitalis of Aversa was driven off after she and the sisters had prayed barefoot through a day and a night.

Her body was carried up to Assisi for burial on 12 August 1253 and laid first in San Giorgio, the church where Francis had been buried before the great basilica was finished. The canonisation process, initiated almost at once, was concluded by Pope Alexander IV with the bull Clara Claris Praeclara of 15 August 1255, less than two years after her death. The basilica of Santa Chiara, built on the site of San Giorgio, was consecrated in 1265 to receive her relics; the wooden crucifix from San Damiano that had spoken to Francis in 1206 hangs above her tomb. Her order, which had taken her name in popular usage in her own lifetime, became formally the Order of Saint Clare — the Poor Clares — in 1263, the year of the rule that the Conventual Franciscan minister general Bonaventure caused to be substituted in most houses for the rule she had written. San Damiano alone kept her own.

The road continues to Pope Gregory IX.
