Marcus Claudius Marcellus

42 BC – 23 BC · nephew and first designated heir of Augustus

Marcus Claudius Marcellus, born at Rome in 42 BC and dead before his twentieth birthday in the autumn of 23 BC, was the first child of the new Augustan dynasty and the first designated heir to the principate of his uncle Augustus. He held a single junior magistracy, married his cousin Julia the Elder, was buried with extraordinary public ceremony in the unfinished mausoleum his uncle was building on the Field of Mars, and gave his name to the great theatre that Augustus completed beside the Tiber and dedicated in his memory. Had he lived, the line of succession at Rome might have descended through him and Julia rather than through the eventually-imposed adoption of Tiberius; instead, his death of an illness at Baiae in his nineteenth year was the first of the dynastic catastrophes that, in two generations, would force the principate from one branch of the Augustan household to another.

He was the son of Gaius Claudius Marcellus Minor, consul in 50 BC and one of the moderate optimate opponents of Julius Caesar, and of Octavia the Younger, the elder sister of the man who was then Octavian and would, fifteen years later, become Augustus. The Claudii Marcelli were one of the oldest and most distinguished houses of the Roman nobility; an ancestor four generations back, Marcus Claudius Marcellus the consul of the Hannibalic war, had killed a Gallic chieftain in single combat and dedicated the rare spolia opima at Rome and was one of the most famous Roman commanders of the middle Republic. The infant Marcellus, born within months of his father's death, inherited the name and the dignity of that house through his father and the political access to the new dispensation through his mother.

He was raised first in his mother Octavia's household and, from 40 BC, when she married Mark Antony at the Treaty of Brundisium, in the unusual extended household at Athens and at Rome that included his two elder half-sisters by his father's earlier marriage, his half-sisters Antonia the Elder and Antonia the Younger by his mother's new marriage to Antony, and eventually the orphans of Antony and Cleopatra who came home with his mother after Alexandria. From his teens he was treated as a member of the imperial family, and from about 30 BC, after Octavia's withdrawal from Antony's house, he was largely raised under his uncle's supervision.

In 25 BC, when he was about seventeen, he was sent to Spain to serve on his uncle's staff during the long Cantabrian war in the north of the peninsula. He served creditably and was recalled in the autumn with Augustus's other military protege, Livia's son Tiberius, then sixteen, who had served on the same campaign. The two were given the unusual joint honor of an advance in the cursus honorum: the right to stand for the consulship ten years before the legal age, the praetorship five years before, and immediate enrolment in the Senate. Marcellus was the more visibly favored of the two; Tiberius, his cousin by Augustus's wife Livia, was, in the gossip of the period, understood to be the alternative dynastic candidate held in reserve.

In late 25 BC or early 24 BC, on Augustus's return from Spain, Marcellus was married to Augustus's only child, Julia the Elder, then about fourteen. Augustus himself was still in the field and absent from the wedding; his place was taken in the ceremony by Agrippa, whose later jealousy of the young man's advancement was said by ancient sources to begin with that day. The marriage was the public announcement of the new dynastic arrangement: Marcellus, if Augustus died, would be expected to step into the principate. He was awarded the augurate; he gave the games of the city as curule aedile in 23 BC, on a scale that the Roman public remembered for years; he was visibly being moved forward in the political life of the city.

In the same summer Augustus fell desperately ill, a sickness so severe that he was widely believed to be dying. He recovered, but Marcellus, who had been with him through the illness, fell ill in turn that autumn at Baiae, where Augustus had retired to convalesce. The young man's illness did not yield to the cold-water regime of the imperial physician Antonius Musa, which had saved Augustus, and he died there in the late summer of 23 BC, aged nineteen. The popular grief at Rome, by the consistent account of Velleius Paterculus, Dio, and Virgil, was enormous.

Augustus delivered the funeral oration himself. The body was burned in the Field of Mars and the ashes interred in the mausoleum Augustus had been building beside the Tiber, of which Marcellus became the first occupant. Octavia, the boy's mother, never quite recovered; she lived for another twelve years in formal mourning and seclusion. Virgil, returning to a passage in the sixth book of the Aeneid that he had been composing, inserted the famous lines in which Anchises shows Aeneas a young shade walking with the line of future Romans — "if only you could break the harsh decrees of fate, you would be Marcellus" — and read the passage in Octavia's house; she is said to have fainted when she heard her son's name. The lines were paid for, by Donatus's later account, with a gift of ten thousand sesterces a verse from the imperial household.

The political consequence of his death was immediate and very large. Augustus, who had still no son of his own, was forced to reopen the question of the succession on the floor of the Senate in a way that he had taken some pains to avoid. Agrippa — the most powerful subordinate of the regime, now ostentatiously sent to the East as governor of the eastern provinces in what looked like a removal — was recalled within a year. In 21 BC, Julia, the eighteen-year-old widow of Marcellus, was married to Agrippa; she would give him five children — Gaius, Lucius, Julia the Younger, Agrippina the Elder, and Postumus Agrippa — who would carry the dynasty into the second generation. Marcellus left no children. The theatre that Augustus completed at the foot of the Capitoline near the Tiber, begun by Julius Caesar and finished in 13 or 12 BC, was dedicated in Marcellus's name and stands still in the city under that name today.

He had been, by all the surviving evidence, an able and engaging young man, popular at Rome, a fluent speaker, openly admired by his uncle. He was the first of three young heirs that Augustus would lose in succession — Marcellus in 23 BC, Lucius Caesar in AD 2, Gaius Caesar in AD 4 — and the first sign that the new dynasty's hope of orderly hereditary succession was going to be very much harder to secure than the political settlement itself. The principate did not pass through him. It passed, two heirs and a quarter of a century later, through his widow Julia and her sons by another marriage; eventually, by accident, it passed through his Claudian cousins, the line of Tiberius and Drusus the Elder, who had stood as the alternative all along.

The road continues to Julia the Elder.
