Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach

1490 – 1568, last Grand Master of the Teutonic Order and first Duke of Prussia

Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach was born on 17 May 1490 at Ansbach in Franconia, the third son of Frederick I, margrave of Brandenburg-Ansbach, and Sophia Jagiellon, daughter of King Casimir IV of Poland. The double inheritance was the defining fact of his life: through his father he was a Hohenzollern of the Franconian branch and a prince of the Holy Roman Empire, through his mother he was a nephew of the king of Poland and stood within the dynastic orbit of Kraków. As a younger son he was directed toward an ecclesiastical career, and in 1510 the Teutonic Order, in desperate need of a candidate who might extract it from its long subordination to the Polish crown, elected him Grand Master at the age of twenty.

The Order he inherited was a shadow of the crusading state it had been. Since the Second Peace of Toruń in 1466 it had held only the truncated eastern half of its former territory, with its capital at Königsberg, and had ruled even that as a vassal of the Polish king to whom the Grand Master was required to render homage. The Polish demand for homage was the immediate occasion of Albert's election: the Polish king Sigismund I was his maternal uncle, and the chapter calculated that a nephew with imperial credentials might play diplomatic games that an ordinary German knight could not. Albert obliged them. He refused the homage, looked to emperor and pope to back him, and in 1519 began the last war of the Teutonic Order against Poland.

The Polish-Teutonic War of 1519–1521 was militarily inconclusive. Albert's mercenary armies, paid by imperial subsidies that never quite arrived in full, took some towns and lost others; Polish armies plundered Prussian Order territory but could not reduce Königsberg. A four-year truce concluded in 1521 left everything exactly where it had been. During the truce Albert travelled west to seek further support, and in late 1523 he made the journey that changed his life and his Order: he met Martin Luther in Wittenberg.

The conversation, conducted over several days, is one of the better-attested episodes of early Reformation diplomacy. Luther, who saw the Teutonic Order as an anachronism encrusted with vows that no one any longer believed, told Albert plainly to discard the rule, marry, and convert the Ordensstaat into a hereditary secular duchy under Lutheran reformation. The advice was self-interested — Luther wanted a Protestant principality on the Baltic — but Albert took it. He returned to Königsberg, communicated his intentions in confidence to his uncle Sigismund, and over the next eighteen months negotiated the terms.

On 8 April 1525, in the Treaty of Kraków, the Teutonic Order in Prussia was dissolved. On 10 April 1525, in the market square of the Polish capital, Albert knelt before his uncle Sigismund I and rendered as Duke of Prussia the homage he had refused for fifteen years as Grand Master. The duchy he now held was the first openly Protestant state in Christian history, fully two years before the Reformation was institutionalised anywhere else, and it was held as a hereditary fief of the Polish Crown. The Teutonic Order survived elsewhere — its branch in Livonia would last for another quarter-century, its German bailiwicks would last for centuries — but the heart of the Order, the state in Prussia, was gone.

Albert's secularisation was condemned by the emperor, by the pope, and by the remaining knights of the Order assembled in Mergentheim, who elected a counter-master and excommunicated him. None of it could be enforced. He had Poland and he had the bulk of the Prussian estates. He married, in 1526, Dorothea of Denmark, by whom he had several children of whom only Anna Sophia survived to adulthood; after Dorothea's death in 1547 he married Anna Maria of Brunswick-Lüneburg in 1550, by whom he had his son and heir Albert Frederick.

The reform of the duchy he carried through methodically. The Lutheran church order was promulgated; monasteries were dissolved and their endowments diverted to schools, hospitals, and the support of evangelical clergy; the printing press at Königsberg produced Lutheran works in German, in Old Prussian, in Lithuanian — including the first book ever printed in Lithuanian, the Catechism of Martynas Mažvydas in 1547. In 1544 he founded the Albertina, the University of Königsberg, intended both as a Lutheran intellectual centre on the Baltic and as a school for the new ducal administration. The Albertina would last almost exactly four hundred years, producing Immanuel Kant among many others, until it was destroyed in the Second World War.

The latter half of his reign was darkened by the Osiandrist controversy. Andreas Osiander, the Lutheran theologian whom Albert had brought to Königsberg as a refugee in 1549, proposed a doctrine of justification — sanctification by the indwelling of Christ — that the mainline Lutheran orthodoxy at Wittenberg, Philip Melanchthon especially, rejected as Catholicising. Albert, who valued Osiander personally and theologically, defended him; the duchy's clergy split; Lutheran princes elsewhere accused Königsberg of heresy. The quarrel poisoned the last fifteen years of Albert's life and weakened the ducal authority. He was forced in 1566 to accept a settlement imposed by Polish royal commissioners that ended Osiandrism, executed two of his closest advisors for treason, and limited his autonomy considerably. His Polish suzerain by then was no longer Sigismund I, dead since 1548, but his cousin Sigismund II Augustus.

Albert outlived almost everyone he had begun his reign with. He died on 20 March 1568 at his castle of Tapiau at the age of seventy-seven, only a few hours before his wife Anna Maria, whose death he had not been told of. He was buried in the cathedral at Königsberg. His son Albert Frederick succeeded him but was mentally unstable from the 1570s; the duchy passed eventually under the regency of the Hohenzollern senior line in Berlin, and so by the seventeenth century into the hands of the Brandenburg electors who, a hundred years after that, would crown themselves kings in Prussia. The state Albert had improvised out of a defunct Crusader Order had become the kernel of the Prussian and German future. Few rulers of the Reformation generation made a more durable choice with thinner cards.
