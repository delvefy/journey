Edward the Black Prince

1330 – 1376, Prince of Wales and Aquitaine, hero of Crécy and Poitiers, the heir who died before his father

Edward was born at Woodstock Palace on 15 June 1330, the first child of Edward III of England and Philippa of Hainault. He was four months old when his father carried out the coup at Nottingham that broke the regency of Isabella and Roger Mortimer, and his childhood was the beginning of his father's long personal reign. He was created Earl of Chester at three, Duke of Cornwall — the first English duchy — at seven, and Prince of Wales at thirteen. The epithet "the Black Prince" by which English history has known him was first recorded in print in the sixteenth century; whether it derives from the colour of his armour, from his temper, or from his reputation in France — where the Aquitaine campaigns left a long and bitter memory — cannot now be settled.

His upbringing was that of a fourteenth-century prince in the years of his father's transformation of the English crown into a great European power: an education in arms by the soldiers of the king's household, in literature by clerks of the chapel, in horsemanship and falconry on the king's progresses between Windsor, Westminster, Eltham, and the hunting lodges of the south. He was knighted by his father on the beach at La Hougue in Normandy in July 1346, sixteen years old, on the morning of the campaign that ended at Crécy.

At Crécy on 26 August 1346 he commanded, in nominal independence, the right wing of the English army — the post of greatest danger, against the French chivalric main — under the close supervision of the experienced earls of Warwick and Oxford. The battle is reported by Froissart as the occasion of one of the great anecdotes of the king's reign: when in the press his son was hard-pressed and a messenger reached his father with an urgent request for reinforcements, Edward III is said to have replied "let the boy win his spurs", and refused them. The prince's wing held; the French chivalry, charging uphill into the English longbow shafts, was destroyed; among the dead, marked by his crest of three ostrich feathers and the motto Ich Dien, was John, the blind King of Bohemia, whose badge the prince thereafter adopted as his own. It is borne by every Prince of Wales since.

His decade of fame ran from the renewal of the war in 1355. As his father's lieutenant in Aquitaine, then as the independent commander of a major chevauchée out of Bordeaux, he ravaged the Languedoc in the autumn of 1355 from Carcassonne to the Mediterranean; he marched north through Périgord and Poitou the following summer; and at Maupertuis near Poitiers on 19 September 1356 he met and destroyed the army of King John II of France, three times the size of his own, in an action that combined the longbow shaft-rain of Crécy with a daring outflanking charge by his Gascon cavalry. King John was taken prisoner on the field; the prince supped that night with his royal captive in his own tent, the highest-ranking English military prize since the capture of King David II at Neville's Cross ten years earlier.

King John was brought back to England and lived for four years in luxurious captivity, in the negotiations that culminated in the Treaty of Brétigny in 1360. By the treaty the king of France ceded to Edward III an enlarged Aquitaine in full sovereignty — from the Loire to the Pyrenees, including Poitou, Saintonge, the Limousin, Périgord, the Agenais, Quercy, and the Bigorre — in return for Edward's renunciation of the French throne and a ransom of three million crowns. The prince was created Prince of Aquitaine in 1362 and went out to govern the new principality from Bordeaux.

His seven years at Bordeaux were the most ambitious continental enterprise mounted by an English prince of the late Middle Ages, and the most costly. He kept a court of extraordinary magnificence; he attempted to govern a duchy three times the size of England with a small staff and the resentful cooperation of the Gascon lords; he intervened, in 1367, in the Castilian civil war on behalf of the deposed Pedro the Cruel against his half-brother Henry of Trastámara, and at the battle of Nájera on 3 April 1367 defeated the Trastamaran army in a textbook longbow action, restoring Pedro briefly to his throne. The campaign was financially ruinous and physically destructive: the prince contracted, by the most plausible modern diagnosis, an amoebic dysentery that he never wholly shook off, and Pedro, the supposed beneficiary, defaulted on the agreed payments and was assassinated by his half-brother two years later.

The taxes the prince imposed on the Gascon lords to recover the costs of Nájera drove them to appeal over his head to Charles V of France, who from 1369 began the methodical reconquest of the territories ceded at Brétigny. The English position in south-western France collapsed in the early 1370s. The prince, in failing health and in his anger, sacked Limoges in September 1370 after its bishop had defected to the French, in an action remembered by French chroniclers as one of the worst atrocities of the war. He returned home from Aquitaine in January 1371, twenty thousand pounds in debt and a sick man, never to leave England again.

He had married in 1361, against his father's evident reservations, his first cousin once removed Joan, called the Fair Maid of Kent, the only daughter of Edmund of Woodstock, Earl of Kent — a brilliant, scandalous, twice-widowed beauty whose first marriage had been annulled and whose elopement with the prince had to be regularised by papal dispensation. Their younger son, Richard of Bordeaux, born in 1367, was the prince's only surviving child after the death in infancy of his elder son Edward.

The last five years of his life were spent at Berkhamsted and Kennington, in increasing illness. He played, from his sickbed, a leading part in the political crisis of the "Good Parliament" of 1376 — ranging himself against his brother John of Gaunt and the court financiers — in what was the last public action of his life. He died at Westminster on 8 June 1376, eight days short of his forty-sixth birthday, his father by then aged and visibly declining. He was buried at Canterbury Cathedral, where his bronze gilt effigy, the most famous English military effigy of the Middle Ages, lies above his tomb. His ten-year-old son Richard succeeded their grandfather as Richard II thirteen months later.

His reputation in the centuries after his death was a strange composite. Froissart's chronicle made him the model of the chivalric prince; the French tradition long remembered him as a destroyer; the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries took him up as the type of English military virtue, with his ostrich feathers stamped on every English regimental colour. The historical Edward of Woodstock had been an extraordinary field commander, an ineffective administrator, and an unlucky governor of conquered territory whose death twelve months before his father's brought to the English crown a child king and, eventually, the long political crisis that opened the deposition of Richard II and the Lancastrian usurpation.
