Henry Percy, 1st Earl of Northumberland

1341 – 1408, kingmaker of Henry IV, three-times rebel against him, killed at Bramham Moor

Henry Percy was born on 10 November 1341, the eldest son of Henry Percy, third Baron Percy of Alnwick, and Mary, daughter of Henry, Earl of Lancaster. The Percys had been northern barons since the early thirteenth century, but it was Henry's father and grandfather who built the family into the dominant magnate house of the eastern March. His own marriage in 1358 to Margaret Neville, sister of John, Lord Neville of Raby and aunt of the Ralph Neville who would become first Earl of Westmorland, brought the Percy and Neville interests into the close alliance that, in the next generation, would turn into a long rivalry for control of the north.

He succeeded as fourth Baron Percy in 1368 and at once entered the political life of Edward III's old age. He fought in France in 1369 and was warden of the East March from 1372. The coronation of Richard II in July 1377 was the occasion of his elevation: on the morning of the coronation the boy king created him Earl of Northumberland, the first English earldom granted in the new reign and the most senior new creation between then and 1399. The earldom was both a recognition of Percy power along the Border and a deliberate counterweight to John of Gaunt, who had quarrelled with the Percys over precedence and would for the next twenty years.

His career through the 1380s and 1390s was that of a great northern magnate alternating between Border campaigns, missions to Scotland on the king's behalf, and increasingly uneasy attendance at court. He fought a slow war along the Tweed against the earls of Douglas and Dunbar; he negotiated the truces of Leulinghem and Pleshey; he was twice warden of both Marches. His relations with Richard II declined as the king grew more autocratic in the 1390s, but he kept clear, with the older man's caution, of the open quarrels in which his eldest son Hotspur was constantly involved. In June 1399, when Henry Bolingbroke landed at Ravenspur in Yorkshire from his French exile, the Percys were the first great northern magnates to bring out their retainers for him. The earl himself joined Bolingbroke at Doncaster on 5 July with eight hundred Percy men; his son Hotspur was already in arms; their adherence carried the rest of the north and made the revolution possible.

For the first two years of Henry IV's reign he was the most rewarded subject in the kingdom. He was made Constable of England for life, warden of both Marches, lord of the Isle of Man in his son's name, holder of vast new grants in north Wales, in Cumberland, and in Lothian. The Welsh grants in particular set the family at cross purposes with the rising of Owain Glyndŵr from September 1400, the suppression of which was made into a Percy responsibility. The grievances of the Percys against the new regime accumulated rapidly. The Welsh war was unfunded; the Scottish wardenships were paid late or not at all; the king refused to ransom the earl's son-in-law Edmund Mortimer, captured by Glyndŵr at Pilleth in June 1402, on the grounds that Mortimer had married a daughter of Glyndŵr and become a traitor.

The first rebellion was the rebellion of Hotspur in July 1403. The earl was supposed to bring an army south to meet his son's small force outside Shrewsbury; he was either too slow to march or too cautious to commit. Hotspur was killed at Berwick Field on 21 July before the earl had crossed the Trent. The earl himself, sixty-one years old, surrendered without a fight to the king at York and was lodged at Coventry for the autumn. Parliament in 1404 restored him to most of his estates but stripped him of the constableship and the wardenships of the Marches. He retired to Warkworth and brooded.

The second rebellion came in May 1405. The plan, the so-called Tripartite Indenture conspiracy, was the most ambitious revolt of the reign: the earl, allied with Edmund Mortimer at Harlech, with Glyndŵr in Wales, and with Archbishop Richard Scrope of York and the earl Marshal Thomas Mowbray, was to divide England and Wales among the three signatories — Glyndŵr in Wales, the earl in the north, Mortimer in the south — in displacement of Henry IV. The northern rising opened with Archbishop Scrope's proclamation at York in late May; the earl was meant to march down from Warkworth in support. The royal forces under Ralph Neville, the earl's brother-in-law and bitter rival, manoeuvred Scrope and Mowbray into surrender at Shipton Moor on 29 May before the earl had moved; both were executed at Bishopthorpe outside York — Scrope in his vestments, the first English archbishop executed by royal warrant since Becket — on 8 June. The earl, again outpaced, fled to Scotland with his grandson the boy Henry Percy and his ally Lord Bardolf.

He spent his last three years in exile, moving between the Scottish court, Wales, and the Welsh exile court of Glyndŵr. In February 1408 he crossed the Border with a small force, hoping to raise the north again on his own authority. He met the Yorkshire sheriff Sir Thomas Rokeby at Bramham Moor outside Tadcaster on 19 February 1408. He had perhaps two thousand men; Rokeby had a comparable force of Yorkshire levies. The fighting was brief; the earl was killed in the front of his line, sixty-six years old. His head was set on a pole on London Bridge; his body was buried at York Minster. The Percy lands were forfeited to the crown.

His grandson Henry Percy, son of Hotspur, was restored by Henry V in 1416 as second Earl of Northumberland and recovered most of the family property; the Percys then resumed their northern dominance for another century, on the Lancastrian side of the Wars of the Roses. The second earl was killed at the first battle of St Albans in 1455; the third was killed at Towton in 1461; the fourth was murdered at Topcliffe in 1489; the family's run of violent deaths on the Border in service of the crown they had themselves nearly toppled is unique in English peerage history.

The first earl himself, in Shakespeare's Henry IV plays, is the calculating father who hangs back from his son's rebellion at Shrewsbury and pays the price for the hesitation; the historical record is less neat. He was a great northern lord of the older type: patient, prickly, slow, immensely tenacious, with a long memory and a careful eye for his family's land — the type whose decision in 1399 had made a king, and whose three later attempts to unmake the king had cost his line a son, his estates, and finally his head. In the long aftermath the great northern families — Percy, Neville, Stanley — were left to compete for the political vacuum he had opened; the Wars of the Roses, fifty years later, were among other things the second phase of that competition.
