Octavia the Younger

c. 69 BC – 11 BC · elder sister of Augustus, fourth wife of Mark Antony

Octavia the Younger, born about 69 BC and dead at Rome in 11 BC, was the elder full sister of Augustus and one of the great female figures of the late Roman Republic and the Augustan settlement. She was remembered in antiquity for her patience under domestic disaster, her decency, and the strange diplomatic role she briefly played as the wife of Mark Antony and the human guarantor of his last reconciliation with her brother. She married twice; she was widowed twice; she raised by herself, in addition to her own three living children, the three children that her husband Antony left behind by Cleopatra after the suicides at Alexandria, and a stepson by Antony's earlier marriage to Fulvia. Through her elder son Marcellus she was the unsuccessful mother of Augustus's first designated heir; through her younger daughter Antonia the Younger she was the grandmother of Germanicus and Claudius and the ancestor of every Julio-Claudian emperor from Caligula onward.

She was the daughter of Gaius Octavius, a senator of equestrian-rank background from Velitrae in the Alban hills, and of Atia Balba Caesonia, the niece of Julius Caesar. She had an elder half-sister, also called Octavia, by her father's first marriage; her full brother, four years younger, was the future Augustus. Her father died when she was about ten; her mother remarried Lucius Marcius Philippus, the consul of 56 BC, in whose house she and her brother were raised, on the edge of Caesar's political circle and at the close margin of the senatorial nobility.

Around 54 BC she was married to Gaius Claudius Marcellus Minor, of the great Claudian house of the Marcelli — a moderate optimate who was consul in 50 BC and an open opponent of Julius Caesar but who, after Pharsalus, was spared by Caesar and lived quietly until his death in 40 BC. By him she had three children: Marcus Claudius Marcellus, born in 42 BC, who would in time be designated Augustus's heir; and two daughters, Claudia Marcella the Elder and Claudia Marcella the Younger, both of whom would marry high into the new dynastic system, the elder in turn to Agrippa and to Iullus Antonius.

Marcellus the consul died in 40 BC, the year of the second great triumviral crisis between her brother and her future second husband. Mark Antony's wife Fulvia, who had led a war against Octavian in Italy on her absent husband's behalf, had died the same summer in Greece. The Treaty of Brundisium, by which the triumvirs avoided open civil war and divided the empire between them, was sealed by a dynastic marriage: Octavian gave his sister Octavia, the widow of three months, to Antony as a public pledge of his commitment to the partnership. She was about twenty-nine, with three small children; Antony was about forty-three. The Senate, which would otherwise have required Octavia to observe the customary ten-month period of mourning, formally lifted the rule for her in honor of the public interest in the match. Octavia, by every contemporary report, played the part of the unifying matron with remarkable dignity, and lived with Antony in Athens for the better part of three years between 39 and 37 BC. They had two daughters: Antonia the Elder, born in 39 BC, and Antonia the Younger, born in 36 BC.

The marriage failed when Antony returned in 36 BC to Cleopatra in Alexandria, with whom he had earlier conducted a relationship at the time of his Parthian expedition. Octavia returned to Rome with her two infant daughters and joined her brother's household on the Palatine. Over the next four years she made repeated public efforts to recover her husband — most famously the journey of 35 BC, when she sailed to Athens with troops and supplies that Octavian had grudgingly given her for the Parthian campaign, only to find that Antony refused to meet her and sent word that she should hand the supplies to his officers and return to Italy. She did. Through the long open declaration of war that followed, she remained in her brother's house, refusing to be divorced; Antony divorced her formally only in 32 BC, when the political confrontation was inescapable.

After Actium and the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra at Alexandria in 30 BC, Octavia performed what was, by ancient standards, the most extraordinary act of her life. She received into her own household, and raised as her own alongside her own surviving children, the three orphaned children of Antony and Cleopatra — Cleopatra Selene, Alexander Helios, and Ptolemy Philadelphus — together with Antony's surviving son by Fulvia, Iullus Antonius. Cleopatra Selene was eventually married out to the client king Juba II of Mauretania; Alexander Helios and Ptolemy Philadelphus disappear from the sources, perhaps having died young; Iullus Antonius grew up in her house and was eventually married to her own daughter Claudia Marcella the Elder. To raise the children of the woman who had displaced her, and of the husband who had divorced her, in the same nursery as her own daughters, was a piece of public theater whose intended message was unmistakable; whether the warmth was real, the gesture was magnificent.

Her own great private grief was the death in 23 BC of her son Marcellus. The young man, then nineteen, had been adopted in expectation as Augustus's son and heir, had been married to Augustus's daughter Julia, and had been given an early consulship and the dignities that signalled Augustus intended the principate to pass to him. He died of a fever at Baiae in the autumn of 23 BC. Octavia withdrew almost entirely from public life. She continued to receive visitors and conduct her household, but the sources report that she was never the same; Virgil, in the sixth book of the Aeneid, included the famous passage in which Anchises shows Aeneas in the underworld the shade of "the young Marcellus" whom the fates would only show to Rome and not let live, and Octavia, hearing the lines read by the poet in her house, is said to have fainted.

She died in 11 BC, in her late fifties. Augustus delivered the funeral oration himself, and the Senate decreed her honors of a kind not previously offered to a private Roman woman. The portico of Octavia at the Field of Mars, which her brother had completed and rededicated to her on her son Marcellus's funeral, would stand under her name in the city for centuries afterward. Her grandson by Antonia the Younger, Germanicus, was a boy of four at her death; her grandson Claudius was still in his cradle, born the year before. The line that came through her surviving daughter Antonia the Younger would, within fifty years, hold the principate.

Her older daughter Antonia the Elder married Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus and was, through their son Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus, the grandmother of the emperor Nero by his marriage to Agrippina the Younger. Her younger daughter Antonia the Younger married Drusus the Elder and produced the line described above. Her elder son was lost; her younger daughters thus carried the line in two channels into the imperial succession of the next century. Octavia herself, of whom no political action of consequence is recorded in the sources after the death of Marcellus, became, in the public memory of the Augustan age, the image against which the wreckage of Julia the Elder was contrasted — the dignified Roman matron whose decency had been wasted and whose patience had survived.
