Nur ad-Din

Nur ad-Din Mahmud ibn Zengi, February 1118 – 15 May 1174, atabeg of Aleppo and Damascus, lord of Syria

Mahmud, the second son of the great Turkic atabeg Imad ad-Din Zengi, was born in February 1118 at Aleppo or in its dependent district, the second of four sons by an unnamed concubine. He grew up at his father’s itinerant court, accompanied him on the campaigns that consolidated Zengid power across the Jazira in the 1130s, and was at his side at the long siege of Edessa in 1144 when his father took the first Crusader capital to fall to a Muslim army. He was twenty-six when his father was murdered by a slave in a tent before Qalat Jabar on the Euphrates on the night of 14 September 1146. The Zengid lands fell apart at once: the eastern half, with Mosul, passed to his elder brother Saif ad-Din; the western half, with Aleppo and the long Syrian frontier with the Latins, fell to Mahmud, by that time already known by the honorific Nur ad-Din, ‘the light of religion’.

His first act was to recover Edessa. In the first weeks after his father’s death the Frankish count Joscelin II re-entered the city by the secret invitation of its Armenian Christian population; Nur ad-Din arrived with the Aleppine army on 3 November 1146, stormed the breach within forty-eight hours, and this time refused to allow the city’s mixed Christian and Muslim community to recover. The walls were thrown down, the Christian quarter expelled, and the city left as a half-empty ruin that never recovered its standing in the medieval east. The catastrophe became the immediate justification, in Europe, for the preaching of the Second Crusade by Bernard of Clairvaux. When the great armies of King Louis VII of France and the Emperor Conrad III of Germany arrived in Syria in 1148, however, they made the wholly unaccountable decision to attack not Edessa or Aleppo but Damascus, the only Muslim city of the region that was still independent of the Zengids and, indeed, was for the moment allied with the Latin kingdom. The siege of Damascus collapsed within four days; the Damascene regime, frightened by the demonstration of Christian intentions, looked from then on to Aleppo for protection. In April 1154 Nur ad-Din entered Damascus by the gate of the Faradis, on the invitation of a faction within its garrison, and made the city his capital. Muslim Syria was for the first time in seventy years united in a single hand.

For the next twenty years his policy was the slow, steady reduction of the Crusader states. He won the Battle of Inab in June 1149, in which Raymond of Antioch was killed and his head sent to the caliph at Baghdad. He captured the Latin count Joscelin II in 1150 by the connivance of a Turcoman scouting party; the count died in his prison at Aleppo nine years later. He fought four set-piece engagements with the army of the kingdom of Jerusalem under Baldwin III and his successor Amalric, was beaten twice by Baldwin at Buqaia and the Battle of Krak in 1163, beat Amalric at Harim in August 1164, and steadily pressed back the limits of the Latin counties of Edessa and Antioch until the eastern frontier of Christian Syria was reduced to a narrow strip along the coast. In the 1160s he repeatedly negotiated with the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Komnenos, who was the protecting overlord of the principality of Antioch; the two men never met but exchanged ambassadors in nearly every year of the decade. Nur ad-Din regarded the Byzantine emperor as a serious threat and worked, with mixed success, to detach the Latin states from his protection.

The Egyptian campaigns of the late 1160s were the crowning project of his career. The Fatimid caliphate, ruling at Cairo over a great Shi’ite state grown weak through factional civil war, was the obvious next prize for any power that aspired to encircle the Latin kingdom. From 1163 King Amalric of Jerusalem and Nur ad-Din competed for influence in Cairo by alternately supporting the rival viziers. Between 1163 and 1168 Nur ad-Din sent three expeditionary forces under his Kurdish general Shirkuh, on the third of which Shirkuh’s nephew, the young Yusuf ibn Ayyub — the future Saladin — served as second in command. The third expedition entered Cairo in January 1169; Shirkuh died two months later and Yusuf succeeded him as vizier. In September 1171 he abolished the Fatimid caliphate altogether and ordered the Friday prayer at Cairo read in the name of the Abbasid caliph and of Nur ad-Din. Egypt and Syria were now in theory united under the same political and religious obedience.

In practice the union was already coming apart. The young Saladin, secure in Egypt, began to act independently; he refused twice to bring his army north to join Nur ad-Din against the kingdom of Jerusalem, on the alternating pretexts of internal revolt and Frankish threat to his own frontier. Nur ad-Din was preparing, in the spring of 1174, the army that would have marched into Egypt to bring his lieutenant to obedience when, on 6 May, he developed an acute attack of angina at Damascus; he died of it nine days later, on 15 May 1174, at the age of fifty-six. His only son, the eleven-year-old as-Salih Ismail, was passed over by his amirs in the succession; Saladin marched on Damascus that autumn and took the city without resistance.

The reputation of the man, in the contemporary and the later Arabic tradition, was less that of a conqueror than of a saint. The biographers, especially Ibn al-Athir and Abu Shama, dwell on his personal austerity, his refusal to wear silk, his habit of dispensing justice in person from a small open chamber adjoining the citadel of Damascus, his founding of madrasas, hospitals, and the great Bimaristan Nuri in Damascus, which still stands and which functioned as a working teaching hospital into the early twentieth century. He took, at the height of his power, no title higher than al-malik al-adil, ‘the just king’, and refused, in deliberate contrast to his pupil Saladin, the title of sultan. The political work he had done — the unification of Muslim Syria, the encirclement of the Latin states, the absorption of Egypt — was the indispensable foundation of what Saladin would shortly accomplish.
