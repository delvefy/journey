Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor

1194 – 1250, Stupor Mundi, last great Hohenstaufen, King of Sicily, King of the Romans, King of Jerusalem, antagonist of two popes

Frederick was born in a tent set up in the market square of Jesi, a small town in the Marches of Ancona, on 26 December 1194, while his mother Constance was travelling south to rejoin her husband. He was the only surviving child of two of the most unlikely consorts in medieval Europe: the Emperor Henry VI, son of Frederick Barbarossa and heir of the Hohenstaufen claim to the German kingship, and Constance of Hauteville, posthumous daughter of King Roger II of Sicily and heiress of the Norman kingdom of southern Italy and Sicily. The marriage had been arranged to settle a forty-year-old quarrel between the two powers, and the boy united the two crowns and the two political traditions: the German feudal kingship of his Staufen ancestors, and the Greek and Arab and Norman bureaucratic state in Palermo to which his mother had returned. He was crowned king of Sicily at Palermo at the age of four. Within months his father was dead of dysentery at Messina; within fourteen months his mother had died too, having committed the boy to the wardship of Pope Innocent III, who had been elected at Rome the same day she died.

The papal wardship was nominal. The Sicilian kingdom, in the absence of any effective central authority, fell into the hands of contending captains, German and Norman by turns, who alternately held the young king as a hostage of their own faction; the surviving chronicles describe the boy, between the ages of five and twelve, living in the corridors and the streets of Palermo with the freedom and the rations of a hanger-on, fed by the goodwill of the Saracen, Greek, and Italian inhabitants of the city. He acquired in those years the half-dozen languages he later spoke fluently — Latin, Greek, Sicilian, German, Provençal, and Arabic — and the omnivorous interest in the sciences and the natural world that the next generation in Italy was to call stupor mundi, the wonder of the world. He came of age at fourteen in December 1208, and the next year, on Innocent's arrangement, married Constance of Aragon, the widow of King Imre of Hungary, ten years his senior.

The German side of his inheritance had passed at his father's death to a brief contested election and then to Otto IV of Brunswick, of the rival Welf family. When in 1211 Otto, breaking with Innocent and threatening Sicily, was excommunicated, a German faction of Hohenstaufen partisans — led by Archbishop Siegfried of Mainz and the Bohemian king Ottokar I — elected the seventeen-year-old Frederick king of the Romans in absentia. He left Palermo in March 1212 with a handful of attendants, eluded Welf patrols in the Alps, and reached the Rhineland by way of Genoa and Pavia. By 1214 the great victory of his ally Philip II of France over Otto and the English at Bouvines had broken the Welf cause; in July 1215 Frederick was crowned a second time at Aachen, where he took the cross. The crusading vow proved a mortgage on the next thirteen years of his life.

On 22 November 1220, Pope Honorius III crowned him emperor in St Peter's. The years between Aachen and Rome had been spent consolidating his German position; the years between Rome and his crusade were spent on the Sicilian kingdom, which he found in disorder and reorganised on the most centralised lines in Latin Europe. He suppressed the Saracen rebels of the western highlands of Sicily and resettled them — some thirty or forty thousand — as a loyal military colony at Lucera in Apulia, where they served him as a personal corps independent of feudal levies. In 1224 he founded the University of Naples, the first state university in Europe, designed to train a literate civil service for his kingdom without recourse to the church schools or to Bologna. In 1231, after long preparation, he promulgated at Melfi the Constitutions of Melfi — the Liber Augustalis — a comprehensive legal code that combined Roman law, Norman custom, and contemporary canonical jurisprudence into a single instrument of royal sovereignty, the most ambitious legislative work of the thirteenth century.

The crusade he had repeatedly postponed at last had to be undertaken. In 1225, after his first wife's death, he had married Isabella of Brienne, the eleven-year-old heiress of the kingdom of Jerusalem, and assumed her title; she died giving birth to their son Conrad in 1228, by which time the political pressure for his departure was unbearable. He sailed at last in September 1227, was excommunicated within weeks by the new pope Gregory IX for delaying once more (the excuse was an outbreak of plague that turned the ship back), and sailed again in June 1228 still under sentence of excommunication. The crusade he then conducted was without battles. He negotiated directly with the Ayyubid sultan al-Kamil, with whom he had been in correspondence since his Sicilian years and whose Arabic he could read, and obtained by a treaty of February 1229 the return of Jerusalem, Bethlehem, and Nazareth to Christian rule for a term of ten years. On 18 March 1229 he set the imperial crown on his own head at the high altar of the Holy Sepulchre, since no patriarch under the excommunication would crown him. He was back in Italy by June. The treaty of San Germano in July 1230 reconciled him to Gregory; the peace lasted nine years.

The years from 1230 to 1239 were the height of his reign. He held a great diet at Mainz in August 1235 which promulgated the Mainzer Landfrieden, a public peace for the empire that survived in many of its provisions into the early modern period; he married a third time, Isabella, the sister of Henry III of England, in a ceremony of celebrated splendour at Worms; he rebuilt and refortified the south Italian and Sicilian castles, of which the octagonal Castel del Monte in Apulia is the most famous; he composed, or had his court compile, the treatise De Arte Venandi cum Avibus — the Art of Hunting with Birds — a work on falconry and ornithology drawn from direct observation that has been taken since the nineteenth century as one of the founding documents of modern zoology. The court at Foggia, Palermo, and Lucera was filled with Saracen astronomers, Jewish physicians and translators, Greek mathematicians, Provençal troubadours, and the first poets of Italian vernacular literature, the Sicilian School from whose Italianised Provençal forms Dante would draw the sonnet. The relations with the Italian communes were not so easily managed: the second Lombard League, the heir of his grandfather's old enemies, took form in 1226 and was repeatedly at war with him. At Cortenuova in November 1237 he won a great battle against them, captured the carroccio of Milan, and seemed within reach of subduing the north.

It was at this point that his relations with Gregory IX, papered over since 1230, broke down again. In March 1239 the pope excommunicated him a second time, on a longer indictment that included the charge of heresy. The war that followed ran from 1239 until Frederick's death and engulfed his successors: it was fought by pamphlet and sermon, by alliance and assassination, and by intermittent armies in northern and central Italy. Gregory died in 1241; the brief Celestine IV followed; the long interregnum ended in 1243 with the election of Sinibaldo Fieschi as Innocent IV. In June 1245 Innocent, having fled to Lyon out of Frederick's reach, opened the First Council of Lyon and on 17 July formally deposed Frederick as emperor and Sicilian king. The deposition was not enforceable by arms, but it transformed the political position of the Hohenstaufen in Germany, where alternative kings (William of Holland from 1247) now had papal backing, and in Italy, where the Guelf cities took the opportunity to renew their resistance.

The last five years were a long and intermittent decline. The defeat of his army before Parma in February 1248, when the besieged townsmen sallied out of the half-encircled city and overran his fortified camp of Vittoria with his treasure and his crown, was a shock from which he never quite recovered. The treason and suicide of his chancellor and intimate Pietro della Vigna in 1249 darkened his court. His son Enzio, captured by the Bolognese in May 1249, would die a prisoner in their city twenty-three years later. He died at Castel Fiorentino near Lucera on 13 December 1250, aged fifty-five, of dysentery after a hunting trip, attended by his sons Manfred and Conrad and dressed at his own request in the habit of a Cistercian monk. His body was carried in slow procession to Palermo and laid in the cathedral, in a porphyry sarcophagus beside his father and his Norman grandfather, where it has lain ever since.
