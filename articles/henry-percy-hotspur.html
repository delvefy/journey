Henry Percy (Hotspur)

1364 – 1403, heir of the Percy earls of Northumberland, the soldier whose nickname has outlived the family, killed at Shrewsbury

Henry Percy, called Hotspur from his youth by the Scots whom he chased and was chased by along the eastern March, was born on 20 May 1364 at Alnwick Castle in Northumberland, the eldest son of Henry Percy, fourth Lord Percy and first Earl of Northumberland, and his first wife Margaret Neville. The Percy family had been the leading magnates of the English north since they had bought the great lordship of Alnwick from the Bishop of Durham in 1309; they had multiplied their property, their following, and their importance in every generation since, and by Hotspur's youth they shared with the Nevilles the effective political control of the Border. Hotspur grew up in arms; he was knighted at ten by Edward III; he was at sea against the French at fifteen, on the Scottish March under his father at sixteen, and was given the wardenship of the East March in his own name at twenty.

The nickname is recorded in Scottish chronicles from 1385. He was, by every contemporary account, the most active English soldier on the Border in his generation: in the saddle by dawn, out by night with a small force, raiding into Lothian and Teviotdale by week-long expeditions, leading from the front. The pattern that would define his career is in place by his early twenties: a man of explosive temper, conspicuous physical courage, somewhat slighter strategic patience than his father, and an unusual willingness to gamble.

He fought at the battle of Otterburn on the night of 5 to 6 August 1388 against a Scottish raiding force under James Douglas, the second Earl of Douglas, and was defeated and captured, though the Scots lost Douglas to a sudden arrow in the throat in the same action. The encounter, fought by moonlight, became the subject of one of the great Border ballads — "The Battle of Otterburn" — and of the related "Chevy Chase", which fixed Hotspur and Douglas in folk memory as the type of the Border knight. The ransom was eventually paid; Hotspur was back in command on the Border within two years.

In 1399, when his old comrade Bolingbroke landed at Ravenspur, the Percys — father and son — were the first great northern magnates to declare for him. The earl of Northumberland brought eight hundred men out of Alnwick; Hotspur brought more. They escorted Bolingbroke through Yorkshire and the Midlands, took Richard II's surrender at Flint, attended the coronation. Henry IV rewarded them with the wardenship of both Marches and a grant of the lordships of the Isle of Man — in Hotspur's name — and of much of north Wales. The young Welsh revolt of Glyndŵr from September 1400 was made, in part, into their problem, by appointment as well as by geography.

The high point of the alliance was the battle of Homildon Hill on 14 September 1402. Archibald Douglas, fourth Earl of Douglas, the son of Hotspur's old captor, had raided into Northumberland; Hotspur intercepted his retreat on the open moor of Homildon and destroyed the Scottish army by archery before they could close. Douglas and several other Scots earls were captured. The dispute that followed was over what should be done with them. Henry IV demanded their ransoms be paid into the royal exchequer; Hotspur refused. The king refused, in turn, to use any of the immense Percy ransoms of the previous decade to pay for the Welsh war or to ransom Hotspur's brother-in-law Edmund Mortimer, captured at Pilleth by Glyndŵr in June. The breach that followed was made worse by the fact that the king was already in debt to the Percys for the wages of the Scottish garrisons.

The rebellion was decided in July 1403. The Percys' grievance had hardened over the spring into a programme: the deposition of Henry IV in favour of Edmund Mortimer's nephew, the boy Earl of March, the prisoner Mortimer's claim to the throne under Yorkist genealogy. The conspiracy was, in retrospect, the first articulation of what would become the Yorkist position of the 1450s. Hotspur slipped south from Berwick on 9 July, raised his banner at Chester on 11 July, and marched on Shrewsbury with the intention of meeting his uncle Thomas Percy, Earl of Worcester, and either joining with Glyndŵr's Welsh army out of the west or breaking the king's son Prince Henry, who held Shrewsbury for the regime.

Henry IV moved faster than Hotspur expected. The two armies met at Berwick Field outside Shrewsbury on the afternoon of 21 July 1403 in what was the largest pitched battle yet fought between Englishmen in living memory. The forces were of comparable size, perhaps fifteen thousand a side. The royal army included the prince of Wales, sixteen years old, in nominal command of the left wing; the rebel army included Worcester and the captive Scot Archibald Douglas, who had reversed allegiance under ransom. The battle opened with a long-range archery duel in which the king's standard-bearer was killed and Prince Henry was struck in the face by an arrow that left a wound he kept for life. In the close-quarter fighting that followed, Hotspur and Douglas charged at the king's banner with a hand-picked party of household knights, hoping to kill or take Henry IV in person. The king had ridden, by some accounts, in another's surcoat; Hotspur killed a man he took for the king and looked up to see, on a near hill, the king himself, still in command. Within moments Hotspur was killed, by an arrow that struck him as he opened his visor, and the rebel army broke.

His body was at first buried in haste at Whitchurch by his cousin the Baron Furnival. Within days the king ordered it disinterred — rumour was already running through the country that Hotspur was not dead — and exhibited at Shrewsbury between two millstones in the marketplace. He was then quartered and his head exhibited at Micklegate Bar in York. His widow, Elizabeth Mortimer, sister of the captive Mortimer and granddaughter of Lionel of Antwerp, eventually recovered the dismembered remains and reburied them. He was thirty-nine.

His father survived him by five years. The earl of Northumberland rebelled again in 1405 with Archbishop Scrope of York and was crushed at Shipton Moor; he rebelled a third time in 1408 and was killed at Bramham Moor. The Percy earldom passed in 1416, with the regrant of the lands by Henry V, to Hotspur's son Henry, the second Earl of Northumberland of the second creation, who died fighting for Henry VI at St Albans in 1455 in the first battle of the Wars of the Roses.

Hotspur's afterlife as a literary figure is largely Shakespeare's work. In Henry IV, Part One, written about 1597, the dramatist made him the foil to Prince Hal — almost the same age in the play, though in fact more than twenty years older — in one of the central character oppositions of the histories: the doomed soldier of an older code beside the politic prince who would become Henry V. The historical Hotspur had been thirty-nine when he died; Shakespeare's Hotspur is in his early twenties. The plain north-country knight who killed and was killed along the Tweed has carried into the language of public life, as type and as common noun, the nickname his enemies gave him.
