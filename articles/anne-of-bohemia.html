Anne of Bohemia

1366 – 1394, Queen of England, eldest surviving daughter of Charles IV, conduit of Wycliffite ideas to Prague

Anne was born at Prague on 11 May 1366, the eldest daughter and second surviving child of the Emperor Charles IV by his fourth wife Elizabeth of Pomerania, and an elder half-sister of the future Sigismund of Luxembourg. She was raised in the Hradčany castle complex, in the multilingual court of Prague that her father had built into the most culturally ambitious capital of the late fourteenth-century Empire; she spoke German, Czech, and Latin from childhood and acquired French and English later. She was a particular favourite of her father; portraits of his court of the 1370s show her with him repeatedly in religious foundations.

Her marriage was the diplomatic offer of the regency council of England in 1380, after the failure of an earlier proposal to marry Richard II to Caterina, the daughter of Bernabò Visconti of Milan. The Bohemian alliance, brokered by John of Gaunt and signed at Westminster in May 1381, was politically motivated: it sought to detach the Empire from the French side in the Schism (Charles IV had recognised the Roman Urban VI) and to provide England with a continental ally against the dauphinist supporters of Avignon. The bride brought no dowry — on the contrary, Richard's regents paid twenty thousand florins to the Luxembourg court for the privilege — and so the marriage was unpopular in England, where the political class had expected a more lucrative alliance.

She crossed from the Low Countries with a small Czech retinue in December 1381 and was met at Dover by her future husband, who was fifteen, as she was. They were married at Westminster on 20 January 1382 and she was crowned two days later. The marriage proved unusually happy. The two of them were of the same age and of similar temperament — cultivated, devout, fond of court ceremonial, indifferent to military affairs — and lived in close personal companionship for the twelve years of the marriage. They had no children; whether by infertility on one side or the other, contemporaries did not know, and modern historians have not resolved.

Her public role was to a large degree a ceremonial one. She intervened, by the custom of medieval queens, in clemency cases, persuading her husband to spare the citizens of London in 1392 during their quarrel over loans and the rebels of the Peasants' Revolt of June 1381, two of which she had attended Richard at Smithfield on horseback at the age of fifteen. Her household kept its own Bohemian character: her chaplains, her chamber women, her almoner, were largely Czechs brought over with her in 1381 and 1382. A continuing exchange of students and clerics ran between her household and Prague throughout her marriage, with the result that copies of the works of John Wycliffe — who had been at the height of his Oxford controversy at the time of her arrival and was politically protected by her brother-in-law John of Gaunt — began to travel back to Bohemia in the chests of Czech scholars returning to her father's university. By the late 1380s a small but persistent circulation of Wycliffite manuscripts had reached Prague through this channel; by 1402 Jerome of Prague had returned from Oxford with the central Wycliffite corpus, and Jan Hus took it up the following year.

That Anne knew or directed any of this is uncertain. The Lollard chronicler John Capgrave in the next century credited her with reading the Gospels in three languages — in Latin, in German, and in English — and remembered her as a patroness of vernacular biblical reading at the English court. Whether or not she personally read English Wycliffite material, her household was the connective tissue of a transmission that the church authorities at both ends would later regret. The pattern was not unique to her — English students travelled to Prague independently — but her household made it routine in a way that would not otherwise have happened.

Her own piety was conventionally Bohemian: she was devoted to the cult of St Wenceslaus of her father's foundations, brought a relic of him to England, and was the patroness of the Carmelites at Oxford. She funded an extension of the Westminster Abbey library and is the probable patroness of the Wilton Diptych, the small folding altarpiece in which Richard II is depicted being presented by his patron saints to the Virgin and Child. She supported Chaucer, who served as her clerk of the king's works, and is the dedicatee of his Legend of Good Women; Chaucer's prologue to that poem is one of the most explicit references to her in English literature of her lifetime.

She died of plague at Sheen Palace on 7 June 1394, twenty-eight years old. Richard II was personally destroyed by the death — he ordered the manor of Sheen razed to the ground, did not return to it for years, and his subsequent autocratic phase, in which he turned against the older nobility and stripped his cousin Bolingbroke of the Lancastrian inheritance, dates from the months after her funeral. She was given an elaborate funeral at Westminster; the king walked with her bier from Sheen to Westminster Abbey, where she was buried in the chapel of Edward the Confessor. Her tomb effigy by Henry Yevele, still in place beside that of Richard II, was carved before the king's death and was the only royal effigy of the medieval English crown to be made before the queen's husband would also be entombed in it; Richard's body, when it came at last in 1413 after his murder at Pontefract and a long delay, was at last laid beside hers by Henry V.

Her second marriage — the dynastic alliance her childlessness had failed to produce — was to be Richard II's marriage in November 1396 to the seven-year-old Isabella of Valois, daughter of Charles VI of France. The arrangement was a French peace, not an Imperial alliance, and the Bohemian connection at the English court lapsed; the Lollard movement she had helped to disseminate carried on without her. The "Good Queen Anne", as the English popular tradition remembered her into the seventeenth century, lay in Westminster Abbey throughout the Reformation, through the Restoration, through the long Hanoverian century; her bones, like her husband's, were disturbed only by repair work in the nineteenth century, and a lock of her hair removed at that time is preserved in the abbey library.

Her historical importance is in two parts. As Queen of England, she was a popular and personally beloved consort whose death broke her husband's political balance; as conduit of Wycliffite ideas to her father's kingdom, she stands at the head of a transmission that, by way of Jerome of Prague and Hus, produced the Hussite movement and, through Luther's encounter with Hus a century later, fed into the Reformation. The biographer's wish for a Wycliffite influence she had personally directed has to be set aside; the transmission was the work of her household, of which she was the patroness rather than the author.
