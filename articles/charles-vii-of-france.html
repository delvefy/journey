Charles VII of France

1403 – 1461, King of France, called the Victorious, who lost his kingdom in childhood and won it back over thirty years

Charles was born in Paris on 22 February 1403, the eleventh and fifth surviving child of King Charles VI and Isabeau of Bavaria. His father had been intermittently mad since 1392, and the kingdom was the field of a savage faction-fight between the king's brother Louis of Orléans and his cousin John the Fearless of Burgundy, neither of whom would yield control of the royal council. The murder of Louis of Orléans in 1407 by the Burgundian's agents in a Paris street opened a civil war between Orléanists, soon called Armagnacs after the count who led them, and Burgundians, that ran for the rest of Charles's lifetime. Charles, the youngest of three living brothers and the least politically important, was given the county of Ponthieu as a child, and the title of Dauphin only in 1417 when both his elder brothers had died in quick succession.

He came of age in the catastrophe of his life. In 1415 the English king Henry V invaded and at Agincourt destroyed the French chivalry; in 1418 the Burgundians seized Paris by night with the help of the butchers' guild and massacred their Armagnac opponents; the fourteen-year-old Dauphin escaped over a wall and rode to the safety of the Loire. In 1419 he met John the Fearless on the bridge of Montereau to attempt a reconciliation; in the middle of the negotiation, members of the Dauphin's escort drew their weapons and killed the duke. Whether Charles ordered it or simply failed to prevent it has never been settled. The Burgundian response was to ally with Henry V; the Treaty of Troyes of May 1420 declared Charles disinherited as illegitimate, married Henry to Catherine of Valois, his sister, and named Henry's children heirs of France. When Henry V and Charles VI died within weeks of each other in 1422, the infant Henry VI of England was proclaimed king of France in Paris and Charles, on his own authority, in Bourges. He was nineteen and ruled, in fact, only the territory south of the Loire.

The years 1422 to 1429 are sometimes called the reign of the King of Bourges. The English under the Duke of Bedford held Paris, Normandy, and most of the lands north of the Loire; the Burgundians held Picardy and Champagne; the English-Burgundian alliance was steadily reducing the Loire fortresses. At Verneuil in 1424 a French army with Scottish allies was destroyed. By October 1428 Orléans, the last bridge town of the middle Loire, was under siege. Charles was on the point of fleeing to Dauphiné or Scotland. The court at Chinon was poor, demoralised, divided into intriguing factions around the king's father-in-law Yolande of Aragon and the Constable Richemont.

In February 1429 a peasant girl of seventeen from the village of Domrémy on the Lorraine frontier arrived at Chinon claiming to be sent by God to raise the siege of Orléans and to lead the Dauphin to be crowned at Reims. Charles examined her, had her examined by theologians at Poitiers, and at last gave her the relief expedition for Orléans in April 1429. The relief succeeded; the English siege collapsed in May; a French campaign through the Loire took Jargeau, Meung, and Beaugency in June and destroyed an English field army at Patay on 18 June. Joan led the king's army across hostile territory to Reims, the traditional coronation place of French kings, where on 17 July 1429 Charles was anointed in the cathedral by the archbishop while the Maid stood beside the altar holding her white standard. The combination of legitimate coronation and the propaganda of supernatural mission shifted the war decisively.

Joan was captured at Compiègne in May 1430 by the Burgundians and sold to the English; she was tried at Rouen for heresy and witchcraft by an ecclesiastical court under the Bishop Pierre Cauchon and burned in the marketplace on 30 May 1431. Charles, who was occupied with the campaigns of the Loire and the north, made no recorded effort to ransom her. He did, twenty-five years later, in 1456, sponsor the nullification trial that overturned her sentence; she was canonised in 1920.

The slow recovery of the rest of the kingdom occupied the second half of his reign. The decisive diplomatic stroke was the Treaty of Arras of September 1435, by which Philip the Good of Burgundy switched sides; the English were thus left isolated. Paris fell in 1436. From 1444 Charles negotiated the Truce of Tours with England, sealed by the marriage of his niece Margaret of Anjou to Henry VI. In 1445 he reorganised the French army, replacing the old feudal levies with twenty compagnies d'ordonnance of permanent paid cavalry, the first standing army of medieval Europe; he supplemented them in 1448 with companies of mounted archers, called francs-archers, drawn from each parish. The brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau, his master-gunners, modernised the artillery park, which now used corned powder and reduced-weight bronze cannons; at Formigny in 1450 the French gunners broke an English column and Normandy was retaken; at Castillon on 17 July 1453 they destroyed the army of John Talbot, the last English field force in France. Bordeaux fell in October. With it ended the Hundred Years War.

His later reign was unhappy. His mistress Agnès Sorel, who had been his official maîtresse en titre — the first to bear that public status in French history — died suddenly in 1450, possibly of a mercury overdose, possibly poisoned. His finance minister Jacques Cœur, who had funded the closing campaigns of the war and rebuilt royal credit, was arraigned on charges of poisoning Sorel and embezzlement, stripped of his fortune in 1453, and driven into exile. The king's eldest son the Dauphin Louis, with whom he had been on bad terms since the failed Praguerie revolt of 1440, governed Dauphiné like an independent prince and from 1456 lived in open exile at the Burgundian court. Charles became increasingly suspicious of poisoning, refused for periods to eat, and is said to have died on 22 July 1461 at Mehun-sur-Yèvre of an infected jaw made worse by starvation.

His reign had begun in the worst political crisis of medieval France and had ended with France the strongest territorial state in Western Europe. The standing army he had created and the centralised financial administration he had built were the foundations of the Bourbon monarchy. The Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges of 1438, asserting Gallican rights over the French church, would still be cited in disputes with Rome three centuries later. He had been thought, in 1422, scarcely a real king at all; the surname le Victorieux given to him by later chroniclers measures the distance between his accession and his death.
