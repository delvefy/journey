Ptolemy II Philadelphus

King of Ptolemaic Egypt, 309–246 BCE

Ptolemy II, called Philadelphus, was the son of Ptolemy I Soter and succeeded him as king of Egypt around 283 BCE. Under his long and prosperous reign the Ptolemaic kingdom reached its height of wealth and cultural brilliance, with Alexandria the most splendid city of the Greek world.

He was the supreme royal patron of learning. He vastly enlarged the Library and Museum of Alexandria, gathering scrolls from across the world and supporting a glittering circle of scholars and poets—among them Callimachus, Apollonius of Rhodes, and the pastoral poet Theocritus, who sang the king's praises. He completed the great Pharos lighthouse, one of the wonders of the ancient world, and by tradition commissioned the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures made for the Jews of Alexandria.

His court mingled Egyptian with Greek custom: he married his own full sister, Arsinoe II, a formidable and politically gifted queen who was deified alongside him, and from this union came his surname Philadelphus, “sibling-loving.”

Abroad he kept Egypt a great power, waging the Syrian Wars against the rival Seleucids and projecting Ptolemaic influence across the eastern Mediterranean. He exchanged embassies with distant courts—with Rome, then a rising republic—and his name appears among the Hellenistic kings to whom the Indian emperor Ashoka claimed to have sent missionaries. He died in 246 BCE, leaving Egypt rich, powerful, and the unrivalled centre of Greek science and letters.
