Diophantus

Greek mathematician of Alexandria, fl. c. 250

Diophantus of Alexandria is one of the most influential mathematicians of antiquity and one of the most shadowy. Almost nothing is known of his life; he is usually placed in the third century, and the only personal trace is a riddle preserved in the Greek Anthology that encodes his age at death as eighty-four — a puzzle that may itself be a later invention. What survives is his work, and that was enough to reshape the subject.

His Arithmetica, planned in thirteen books of which several Greek and Arabic ones come down to us, is a collection of some hundred and thirty problems solved by ingenious algebraic reasoning. Rather than the geometry that dominated Greek mathematics, Diophantus sought numbers — usually positive rationals — satisfying stated conditions, and he introduced abbreviations for the unknown and its powers that pointed the way toward true symbolic algebra. For this he is often called, with al-Khwarizmi, a father of algebra.

The problems he posed, demanding whole-number or rational solutions, gave rise to the whole branch of study now called Diophantine analysis. His text was preserved and studied in late antiquity — Hypatia of Alexandria wrote a commentary on it — and translated and printed in the Renaissance. Most famously, it was in the margin of a Latin edition of the Arithmetica that Pierre de Fermat jotted the tantalizing claim, that he had a proof too large for the page, which became Fermat’s Last Theorem and haunted mathematics for three and a half centuries.
