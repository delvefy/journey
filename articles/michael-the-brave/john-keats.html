John Keats

1795–1821, English Romantic poet

John Keats was born in Moorgate, London, the eldest son of a Finsbury livery-stable manager who died in a fall from a horse when the boy was eight. His mother died of tuberculosis six years later, and he was placed in the care of a guardian who apprenticed him to a surgeon. He completed his medical training at Guy's Hospital and earned an apothecary's licence in 1816, but turned at twenty-one to poetry.

His sonnet “On First Looking into Chapman's Homer,” written in October 1816 after a night of reading the Elizabethan translation with his friend Charles Cowden Clarke, declared his vocation in print. Through Leigh Hunt, who published it, he was drawn into the Hampstead literary circle that included William Hazlitt and, briefly, Percy Bysshe Shelley. He met William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge on walks across Hampstead Heath. He read Milton, Shakespeare, and Boccaccio with equal passion; the Decameron supplied the plot of his 1818 narrative poem Isabella, or the Pot of Basil.

The Tory reviewers savaged his first volume and his epic Endymion (1818). Undeterred, he completed in a single twelve-month burst the work on which his fame rests: the unfinished Hyperion, the great odes — To a Nightingale, On a Grecian Urn, To Autumn, On Melancholy, To Psyche — The Eve of St Agnes, La Belle Dame sans Merci, and Lamia. By the end of 1819 he was engaged to his neighbour Fanny Brawne and showing the signs of the disease that had taken his mother and brother.

In autumn 1820 his friend Joseph Severn accompanied him to Rome on doctor's orders. He died there of tuberculosis in February 1821, just twenty-five, asking that his stone be inscribed “Here lies One whose Name was writ in Water.” Shelley elegized him as “Adonais”; Lord Byron, who had mocked him in life, contributed the cruel epitaph that Keats had been “snuffed out by an article.”

The road continues to William Wordsworth.
