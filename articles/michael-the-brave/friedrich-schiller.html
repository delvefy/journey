Friedrich Schiller

German poet, playwright, and historian, 1759–1805

Schiller was educated, against his will, at a harsh military academy in the service of the Duke of Württemberg, and his first play, The Robbers, a fiery drama of rebellion and freedom, caused a sensation that forced him to flee the duke’s territory. Its passionate idealism made him a leading voice of his generation and announced the great theme of his life’s work: the struggle for liberty and moral dignity against tyranny and circumstance.

For a time he turned to history, writing on the revolt of the Netherlands and the Thirty Years’ War and becoming a professor at Jena, and he immersed himself in the philosophy of Kant, producing influential essays on beauty, freedom, and the aesthetic education of humanity. His move to Weimar began the famous friendship and creative partnership with Goethe that defined the classical age of German letters.

In his mature dramas he brought historical figures to vivid life, among them Mary, Queen of Scots, in Maria Stuart and Joan of Arc in The Maid of Orleans, along with the towering Wallenstein trilogy and the freedom-drama William Tell. His poem the “Ode to Joy,” a hymn to human brotherhood, would later be immortalised by Beethoven in his Ninth Symphony. Ennobled and celebrated, Schiller died of tuberculosis at only forty-five, mourned with Goethe as one of the twin glories of German literature.
