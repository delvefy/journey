James II of England

Last Catholic king of England, 1633–1701

James II of England, James VII of Scotland, was the last Catholic monarch to rule the British kingdoms and the king whose stubborn faith provoked the Glorious Revolution. The second surviving son of Charles I and younger brother of Charles II, he was born in 1633 and spent his youth in exile during the Civil War and the Commonwealth, serving with distinction in French and Spanish armies.

Restored with his brother in 1660, he became Lord High Admiral and an able commander of the fleet in the Dutch wars; the colony of New York was named in his honour. But his open conversion to Roman Catholicism set him at odds with the nation, and through the Exclusion Crisis Parliament tried in vain to bar him from the throne.

He succeeded in 1685 and at once crushed the rebellion of his nephew the Duke of Monmouth, whose followers were savagely punished. Then he pressed too far: suspending penal laws against Catholics, packing offices with co-religionists, and prosecuting bishops who resisted. When his Catholic queen bore a son in 1688, raising the spectre of a Catholic dynasty, leading men invited his Protestant son-in-law William of Orange to intervene.

William landed, James's nerve and his army failed him, and he fled to France. An attempt to recover his crown through Catholic Ireland ended in defeat at the Boyne in 1690. He passed his last years in dignified exile at Saint-Germain under the protection of Louis XIV, increasingly given to devotion, and died there in 1701. He was succeeded in time by his own daughters, the first of them Mary and then the second, Queen Anne.
