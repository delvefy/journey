Cornelius Vanderbilt

1794–1877, American shipping and railroad magnate

Cornelius Vanderbilt was born on Staten Island into a family of modest Dutch farmers and ferrymen. He left school at eleven, and at sixteen borrowed one hundred dollars from his mother to buy a small sailing periauger with which he ran passengers between Staten Island and Manhattan. From that single boat he built, over six decades, one of the largest private fortunes in American history.

During the War of 1812 he won government contracts to supply forts in New York harbor, and afterward worked under the steamboat operator Thomas Gibbons, learning the new technology while helping to break the Fulton–Livingston steam monopoly affirmed by the Supreme Court in Gibbons v. Ogden. By the 1830s Vanderbilt commanded his own steamboat lines on the Hudson and along the New England coast, undercutting rivals so ruthlessly that competitors paid him to withdraw. The press began to call him “the Commodore,” a title he kept for life.

The California gold rush turned him toward oceanic trade. He opened a Nicaragua transit route across the isthmus that briefly rivaled the Panama crossing, and ran transatlantic steamers to Europe. Only in his late sixties did he pivot decisively to railroads, buying control of the New York and Harlem, then the Hudson River, and finally the New York Central, which he consolidated into a single trunk line from New York City to Chicago. He rebuilt Grand Central Depot in Manhattan as its terminus.

His railroad battles with Daniel Drew, Jay Gould and Jim Fisk over the Erie Railway in 1868 became a defining episode of Gilded Age finance. Vanderbilt died in 1877 leaving roughly one hundred million dollars, almost all of it to his son William Henry. A late gift founded Vanderbilt University in Nashville, his only major philanthropy and the chief monument to a man who otherwise built almost exclusively for himself.
