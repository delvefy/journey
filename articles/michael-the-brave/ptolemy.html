Ptolemy

Greco-Roman astronomer and geographer, c. AD 100–170

Claudius Ptolemy lived and worked in Roman Alexandria, and through his writings he governed the way the world understood the heavens and the earth for more than a thousand years. Drawing on the observations of earlier Greeks, above all Hipparchus, he gathered the astronomy of antiquity into a single great treatise the Arabs would call the Almagest, “the greatest.” In it he set out a detailed mathematical model of an Earth-centred cosmos, in which the sun, moon, and planets move on combinations of circles—deferents and epicycles—arranged so cleverly that they could predict the positions of the heavenly bodies with real accuracy.

This geocentric system, with the motionless Earth at the centre of nested celestial spheres, became the accepted picture of the universe throughout the medieval Islamic world and Christian Europe. Its very success made it hard to dislodge, and it stood as orthodoxy until the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Ptolemy’s reach extended beyond the stars. His Geography compiled the place-names of the known world with coordinates of latitude and longitude and instructions for drawing maps, shaping cartography down to the age of Columbus, even as its errors in the size of the globe misled later explorers. He also wrote on optics, music, and—in the Tetrabiblos—astrology. Few individuals have so thoroughly defined the science of their age, and his name marked the established order of the cosmos until a Polish astronomer dared to move the Earth.

The road continues to Nicolaus Copernicus.
