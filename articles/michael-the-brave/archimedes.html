Archimedes

Greek mathematician and inventor of Syracuse, c. 287–212 BC

Archimedes of Syracuse is widely regarded as the greatest mathematician of antiquity and one of the greatest of all time. Working in the Sicilian city under the patronage of King Hiero II, and in contact with the scholars of Alexandria such as Eratosthenes, he pushed Greek mathematics to heights not equalled for nearly two thousand years. He calculated a remarkably accurate value for pi, found the areas and volumes of curved figures by a method of exhaustion that anticipated the integral calculus, and took special pride in proving the relationship between a sphere and its enclosing cylinder.

He was also a brilliant physicist and engineer. He established the law of the lever—“give me a place to stand and I will move the earth”—and the principle of buoyancy, which legend says struck him in his bath, sending him running through the streets crying “Eureka!” The water-raising screw named for him and a host of ingenious machines are credited to his hand.

When Rome besieged Syracuse during the Second Punic War, Archimedes turned his genius to its defense, devising catapults, cranes, and other engines that held the legions at bay for two years. When the city finally fell in 212 BC, he was killed by a Roman soldier—according to tradition, while absorbed in a geometrical diagram, telling his killer not to disturb his circles. The Roman commander Marcellus, who had ordered him spared, mourned his death, and posterity has counted it among the great losses of the ancient world.
