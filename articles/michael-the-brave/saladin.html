Saladin

Sultan of Egypt and Syria, 1137–1193

Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, known to the West as Saladin, was born in 1137 at Tikrit into a family of Kurdish soldiers in the service of the Zengid rulers of Syria. He rose under the great Syrian prince Nur ad-Din, and was sent as a young officer to Egypt, where his talents carried him to the office of vizier. In 1171 he ended the long Shia Fatimid caliphate, returned Egypt to Sunni allegiance, and laid the foundation of his own Ayyubid dynasty.

After Nur ad-Din’s death he spent a decade welding Egypt, Syria and the Jazira into a single power encircling the Crusader states. The reckoning came in 1187. At the Horns of Hattin, in the parched hills above Galilee, he annihilated the army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem, captured its king and the relic of the True Cross, and within months took Jerusalem itself, eighty-eight years after the crusaders had stormed it. Where the First Crusade had ended in massacre, Saladin’s entry was marked by restraint, ransom and mercy—a contrast that fixed his fame in Christian as well as Muslim memory.

The fall of Jerusalem summoned the Third Crusade, and with it Saladin’s long duel against Richard the Lionheart. He could not prevent the loss of Acre or hold the coast against Richard’s generalship, but neither could the crusaders prise Jerusalem from him; the war ended in 1192 with a truce that left the holy city in his hands and the pilgrim roads open. He died at Damascus the following year, so free with his charity that, it was said, he left scarcely enough money for his own funeral. Generous, pious and chivalrous, he became the rare enemy whom his foes revered.
