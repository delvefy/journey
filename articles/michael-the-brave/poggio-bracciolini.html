Poggio Bracciolini

Italian humanist and book-hunter, 1380–1459

Gian Francesco Poggio Bracciolini was the greatest recoverer of lost classical texts in the early Renaissance. Born in 1380 near Arezzo, he learned the art of the scribe in Florence under the humanist chancellor Coluccio Salutati, and his beautiful, clear handwriting—the model for the "Roman" letterforms later cast in type—won him a place as a secretary in the papal Curia, which he served for some half a century.

The defining episodes of his life came during the long Council of Constance, when the idleness of the papal court let him ride out to the great monastic libraries of Switzerland and Germany. In their neglected cupboards he found treasure after treasure: the complete text of Quintilian, lost speeches of Cicero, the architecture of Vitruvius, the history of Ammianus Marcellinus, and, most momentous of all, the sole surviving copy of Lucretius's poem On the Nature of Things, whose Epicurean vision he returned to the world in 1417.

Poggio was also a combative man of letters. He carried on savage Latin feuds with rivals such as Lorenzo Valla and Filelfo, and wrote moral dialogues on avarice, nobility, and the miseries of princes, a history of Florence, and the Facetiae, a notorious collection of bawdy jokes and anecdotes.

Patronised by Cosimo de' Medici and the heirs of Petrarch's humanism, he crowned his career by serving as Chancellor of his adopted Florence in his last years. He died in 1459, having done more than almost any man to restore the literature of antiquity.
