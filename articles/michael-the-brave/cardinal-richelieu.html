Cardinal Richelieu

Armand Jean du Plessis, chief minister of France, 1585–1642

Armand Jean du Plessis was born in Paris in 1585, the youngest son of an impoverished Poitou nobleman. When his elder brother renounced the family see of Luçon for a monastic life, the boy was redirected from a soldier’s career to the Church, and in 1607, on the nomination of Henri IV, was consecrated bishop of the marshy Atlantic diocese at the unusual age of twenty-two. He proved a tireless administrator and a careful chronicler of his own ambitions.

Politics reached him through the regency of Marie de’ Medici, whose secretary of state he briefly was in 1616. After her fall he followed her into exile, helped reconcile her with her son Louis XIII, and was made a cardinal in 1622. Two years later he entered the royal council; by 1629 he was effectively master of France. His programme is famous for its three blunt fronts: break the political power of the Huguenots, humble the great nobles, and oppose the Habsburgs abroad. The siege of La Rochelle in 1627–28, ended by his enormous sea wall, finished Protestant France as a state within a state. The Day of Dupes in November 1630, when Louis XIII chose him over his own mother, finished his court enemies.

Abroad he subsidised the Protestant powers—the Dutch Republic, Sweden under Gustavus Adolphus—against the Catholic Habsburgs, and in 1635 brought France openly into the Thirty Years’ War. The treaties that would reward this policy at Westphalia came after his death. At home he founded the Académie française in 1635, rebuilt the Sorbonne, and groomed the young Italian Giulio Mazzarini, the future Cardinal Mazarin, as his successor. Worn out by overwork and chronic illness, he died at the Palais-Cardinal in December 1642, five months before the king he had ruled.
