Georges Clemenceau

1841–1929, "the Tiger," Prime Minister of France

Georges Benjamin Clemenceau was born in Mouilleron-en-Pareds in the Vendée, the son of an anticlerical country doctor who had been imprisoned for republican agitation under Napoleon III. He took his own medical degree in Paris in 1865, spent four years teaching and writing in the United States, where he married Mary Plummer, and returned to be elected mayor of Montmartre on the eve of the siege of Paris in 1870. In the Chamber from 1876 he made his name as a duellist and orator of the radical left, bringing down so many cabinets that he was nicknamed the "wrecker of ministries."

In 1898 he turned his paper L'Aurore over to Émile Zola's open letter to the President of the Republic, gave it the famous headline "J'Accuse...!", and made the rehabilitation of Captain Dreyfus his cause. As prime minister from 1906 to 1909 he broke a wave of strikes, separated church and state in practice, and built the entente with Britain. In November 1917, with the front broken and morale collapsing, President Poincaré recalled him at seventy-six and entrusted him with the conduct of the war.

He gave the country the simple slogan "I make war" and toured the trenches in his rumpled grey overcoat. He pressed for the unified command that put Ferdinand Foch over all Allied armies in March 1918, held the Chamber and the press to their duty during the spring crisis, and on 11 November 1918 announced the armistice from the tribune to a standing ovation. At the Paris Peace Conference of 1919 he sat between Woodrow Wilson and David Lloyd George and pushed for the harshest possible terms on Germany; the resulting Versailles Treaty satisfied no one and he was voted out of the presidency the following January. He retired to write his memoirs, travelled to India and the United States, and died in Paris in November 1929.

The road continues to David Lloyd George.
