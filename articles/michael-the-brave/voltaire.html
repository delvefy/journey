Voltaire

French Enlightenment writer and philosophe, 1694–1778

Born François-Marie Arouet, Voltaire adopted his famous pen name early and used it to become the most celebrated and feared writer of his age. His sharp tongue twice landed him in the Bastille and once drove him into exile in England, where he admired the relative liberty, the empiricism of Locke, and above all the science of Newton, whose ideas he later helped introduce to France with the aid of his learned companion Émilie du Châtelet.

Astonishingly prolific, he wrote plays, poetry, history, essays, and thousands of letters, but he is best remembered for his philosophical tales, especially Candide, whose mockery of the doctrine that this is the best of all possible worlds skewered the optimism of Leibniz. Through works such as the Philosophical Letters and the Treatise on Tolerance he became the great champion of reason, freedom of thought, and religious toleration, signing his campaigns against fanaticism with the cry “crush the infamous thing.”

For a time he was the guest and intellectual ornament of Frederick the Great at Potsdam, until their friendship soured into a celebrated quarrel. He intervened publicly in cases of judicial injustice, most famously defending the memory of the executed Protestant Jean Calas, and he corresponded as an equal with monarchs across Europe. Returning to Paris in triumph shortly before his death, he was lionised by the city. Denied a church burial, he was later honoured by the Revolution and interred in the Panthéon, remembered as the embodiment of the Enlightenment itself.
