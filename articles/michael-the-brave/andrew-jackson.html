Andrew Jackson

7th President of the United States, 1767–1845

Born in the Carolina backcountry and orphaned during the Revolution—scarred for life when a British officer slashed him for refusing to clean his boots—Jackson became a frontier lawyer, planter, and militia general in Tennessee. He won national fame crushing the Creeks at Horseshoe Bend and, in January 1815, annihilating a British army at the Battle of New Orleans, becoming the hero “Old Hickory.”

Denied the presidency in 1824 despite winning the most votes—the result of what his supporters called a “corrupt bargain” between John Quincy Adams and Henry Clay—he swept to victory in 1828 as the champion of the common man. His presidency launched an era of mass democracy and the modern Democratic Party. He waged war on the Second Bank of the United States, faced down South Carolina and his own vice-president Calhoun in the Nullification Crisis, and survived the first assassination attempt against a president.

His record carries a deep stain: the Indian Removal Act drove the Cherokee and other nations from their homelands along the deadly “Trail of Tears.” Forceful, vindictive, and beloved by his followers, he reshaped the presidency into a popular tribune’s office and still gazes from the twenty-dollar bill. He died at his Hermitage estate in 1845. His most persistent and formidable political antagonist across three decades was Henry Clay.
