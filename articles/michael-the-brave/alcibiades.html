Alcibiades

Athenian general and statesman, c. 450–404 BCE

Alcibiades was the most brilliant and the most dangerous Athenian of his generation—a general, orator, and political adventurer whose dazzling gifts were matched only by his lack of scruple. Born around 450 BCE into a noble family and raised in the household of his guardian Pericles, he became a devoted companion of the philosopher Socrates, who once saved his life in battle, and whom he in turn shielded.

Handsome, rich, and persuasive, he rose swiftly in the democracy and in 415 BCE was the chief promoter of the great expedition against Sicily. But on the eve of its departure he was implicated in a scandal—the mutilation of the sacred Herms and the mocking of the Mysteries—and recalled to stand trial. Rather than face it he fled to Sparta, where he advised Athens' enemies on how best to wage the war against his own city.

Distrusted by the Spartans in turn, he passed to the Persian satrap Tissaphernes, intriguing on every side, before engineering his recall to Athens. He returned in triumph and won notable victories, above all at Cyzicus in 410, but a single naval reverse at Notium revived his enemies, and he withdrew once more into exile.

He was assassinated in Phrygia around 404 BCE, the very year Athens finally fell, his death linked by some accounts to Spartan and Persian designs. To Thucydides and later writers he came to embody both the genius and the moral instability of Athens in its decline.
