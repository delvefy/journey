Paolo Uccello

Florentine painter, master of perspective, 1397–1475

Paolo di Dono, nicknamed Uccello for his fondness for painting birds and beasts, was born in Florence in 1397. As a boy he entered the busy workshop of Lorenzo Ghiberti, then at work on the bronze doors of the Baptistery, and so came up among the artists who were inventing the new Florentine manner. He spent some early years in Venice working on mosaics for San Marco before returning home to make his career.

Uccello belonged to the first generation gripped by the new science of linear perspective, and he pursued it with a single-mindedness that became legendary; Vasari tells of him sitting up through the night over his vanishing points, murmuring what a sweet thing perspective was while his wife called him to bed. The fascination shows in everything he made: the foreshortened lances and tumbling horses of his three panels of the Battle of San Romano, painted around 1438 and later prized by the Medici; the drowned world of his Flood in the cloister of Santa Maria Novella; and the eerie geometry of his Hunt in the Forest.

His most public work was a commemoration of another man on this road. In 1436 the city of Florence had him paint, on the wall of its cathedral, a great monochrome equestrian image of the mercenary John Hawkwood, a painted monument standing in for a sculpted one. By his own later account Uccello ended his long life old, poor and little regarded, his obsessive art having passed out of fashion. He died in Florence in 1475.
