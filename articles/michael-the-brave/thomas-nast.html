Thomas Nast

1840–1902, German-American political cartoonist

Thomas Nast was born in Landau, in the Bavarian Palatinate, and brought to New York City at the age of six. He trained briefly with the genre painter Theodore Kaufmann and at the Academy of Design, and from fifteen worked as a staff illustrator for Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper. In 1859 he travelled to Sicily to cover Garibaldi's expedition of the Thousand for the London Illustrated News, a commission that made his reputation as a war artist.

From 1862 he was a staff artist at Harper's Weekly, and through the Civil War his battlefield drawings and patriotic allegories did more than any other images to shape Northern public feeling. Lincoln is reported to have called him “our best recruiting sergeant.” After Appomattox he became a partisan of Reconstruction and a champion of Ulysses S. Grant, whose two presidential campaigns he helped to win.

His greatest single campaign was against the Tweed Ring in New York. From 1869 onward, week after week in Harper's Weekly, he savaged William M. Tweed and his confederates as a bloated tiger, as the vulture “Twas Him,” and as fugitives in stripes. The cartoons made Tweed's face universally known; when Tweed fled to Spain in 1875 he was identified there from one of Nast's drawings. In 1872 Nast helped destroy Horace Greeley's third-party challenge to Grant, and in 1884 his bolt to Grover Cleveland helped tip the Mugwump vote that elected the first Democrat since the war.

Nast invented or fixed several of the visual symbols of American politics: the Republican elephant, the Democratic donkey, the rotund and bearded modern Santa Claus, the figure of Columbia, and the standard image of Uncle Sam. He lost his fortune in the 1884 collapse of Grant and Ward, and accepted from Theodore Roosevelt in 1902 a consulship at Guayaquil in Ecuador, where he died of yellow fever within months.
