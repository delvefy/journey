Joseph Haydn

Austrian composer, 1732–1809

Haydn rose from humble beginnings as a choirboy in Vienna to become the most respected composer of his generation and a decisive figure in shaping the classical style. For some thirty years he served the wealthy Esterházy princes at their remote Hungarian palace, where, as he put it, his isolation forced him to become original. In that long employment he turned out a vast body of work and refined the forms that would define an era.

He did so much to develop the symphony and the string quartet that he is often called the father of both. His more than a hundred symphonies, culminating in the brilliant set written for his triumphant visits to London, and his great series of quartets established models that others would build upon for a century. Genial, witty, and generous, he was known affectionately as Papa Haydn.

His friendships and pupils linked him to the other titans of his age: he formed a warm mutual admiration with the younger Mozart, who dedicated quartets to him, and he taught the young Beethoven. Hearing the oratorios of Handel performed on a grand scale in London inspired him, late in life, to compose his own masterpieces in the form, The Creation and The Seasons. Honoured throughout Europe, he died in Vienna in 1809 as French armies under Napoleon occupied the city, the acknowledged elder statesman of music.
