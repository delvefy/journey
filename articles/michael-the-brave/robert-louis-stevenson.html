Robert Louis Stevenson

1850–1894, Scottish novelist, essayist, and poet

Robert Louis Balfour Stevenson was born in Edinburgh into the family that for three generations had built Scotland's lighthouses. The chronic respiratory illness that would shadow his life kept him in bed through long stretches of childhood, tended by the nurse Alison Cunningham, "Cummy," to whom he later dedicated A Child's Garden of Verses. He was meant to follow his father into engineering, switched to the law and was called to the Scottish bar in 1875, but had already turned to literature: essays in the Cornhill Magazine, travel sketches like An Inland Voyage and Travels with a Donkey in the Cévennes.

In 1876 at an artists' colony at Grez-sur-Loing he fell for Fanny Osbourne, an American ten years his senior with two children, and in 1879 made the journey across the Atlantic and overland to California that nearly killed him; The Amateur Emigrant and Across the Plains grew out of it. They married in 1880 and spent the next years in shifting climates — Davos, the Adirondacks, Bournemouth, Hyères — that he hoped might preserve his lungs. From this travelling sickbed he produced the books that made him a household name: Treasure Island (serialised in 1881), The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886), Kidnapped (1886) and the unfinished masterpiece Weir of Hermiston.

In 1888 he chartered the yacht Casco and sailed with Fanny and his mother for the South Seas, drifting through the Marquesas, the Tuamotus, Tahiti, Hawaii and the Gilberts before settling in 1890 on a four-hundred-acre estate, Vailima, above Apia in Samoa. The Samoans called him Tusitala, the teller of tales; he intervened in their factional politics and protested the colonial carve-up of the islands in letters to The Times. On the evening of 3 December 1894 he was opening a bottle of wine on the veranda when he collapsed of a cerebral haemorrhage and died within hours, aged forty-four. He was buried at the summit of Mount Vaea.
