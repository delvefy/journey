Skanderbeg

Gjergj Kastrioti, Lord of Albania, 1405–1468

Gjergj Kastrioti was born in 1405, fourth son of John Kastrioti, lord of a clutch of mountain estates in the Mat and Dibra districts of upper Albania. About 1423, in a routine extraction of hostages from a vassal house, he was sent to the court of Murad II, converted to Islam, given the name İskender — Alexander — and the rank of bey, and trained in the palace school at Edirne. For two decades he served the Ottoman field army on the Anatolian and Balkan frontiers, rising to the command of a sancak. The Hungarian victories of John Hunyadi gave him his opening: at the battle of Niš on 3 November 1443 he broke off from the Ottoman wing with three hundred Albanian horsemen, rode to Krujë, presented a forged firman ordering its surrender, and on 28 November raised the red-and-black flag of his father’s house over the citadel and renounced Islam.

In March 1444 he convened the lords of central and northern Albania at Lezhë under Venetian eyes and was elected their captain-general. For the next twenty-four years he conducted a defensive war in the mountains against Murad II and his son Mehmed II, repulsing thirteen major Ottoman expeditions. He defeated Ali Pasha at Torvioll in June 1444, broke a punitive army under Mustafa Pasha at Otonetë in 1448, and held the third great siege of Krujë in 1450 against Murad himself, who marched away in October without taking the town. The condottiere lessons he had absorbed from Hunyadi and from his Venetian advisers were turned to ambush, scorched earth, and the use of the limestone passes as natural defensive works.

His diplomacy was as resourceful as his soldiering. Pope Nicholas V granted him the title Athleta Christi; Alfonso the Magnanimous of Aragon-Naples bankrolled his commissaries; he held the second siege of Krujë in 1466 against Mehmed II in person, and the fourth, in 1467, also failed. Twice he crossed the Adriatic to fight in Italy, once for Alfonso, once for Ferrante of Naples against the Angevin invasion of 1461. He died of a fever at Lezhë on 17 January 1468. Krujë held out for another ten years; when at last it fell in 1478, the Ottomans, the story went, dug up his bones and wore fragments of them as amulets in the belief that they conferred his courage.

The road continues to Mehmed II.
