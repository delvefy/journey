Jay Gould

1836–1892, American railroad magnate and speculator

Jay Gould was born in Roxbury, in the Catskills of New York State, the son of a struggling farmer. He left home young, learned surveying, and used the maps he drew of Delaware and Ulster counties to enter the leather tanning trade. By his late twenties he had moved to Wall Street and was buying railroad bonds, especially in small lines whose value he understood better than the speculators who held them.

His first great campaign came in 1868, when together with Daniel Drew and Jim Fisk he controlled the Erie Railway and fought Cornelius Vanderbilt for it. The contest, known as the Erie War, was waged by issuing fraudulent stock, bribing the New York legislature, and conducting parallel proceedings in friendly courts. Gould emerged in control of the Erie, though he was eventually forced out, and the affair set the tone of his public reputation.

In 1869, with Fisk and the connivance of Abel Corbin, brother-in-law to President Grant, he tried to corner the New York gold market. When Grant ordered the Treasury to sell gold on 24 September, prices collapsed, ruining many speculators in what became known as Black Friday. Gould had quietly sold ahead of the break and walked away rich.

Through the 1870s and 1880s he assembled an extraordinary portfolio: the Union Pacific, the Missouri Pacific, the Texas and Pacific, and several smaller western lines, at one point controlling about one-sixth of the nation's railroad mileage. He took over Western Union and broke a major telegraph strike led by the Knights of Labor in 1883, and he held the Manhattan Elevated that carried much of New York City's commuter traffic.

He died of tuberculosis in 1892 at fifty-six, leaving an estate of roughly seventy-two million dollars to his children. In popular memory he became the archetype of the Gilded Age robber baron, and his name a byword for predatory finance.
