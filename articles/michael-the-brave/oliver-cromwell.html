Oliver Cromwell

Lord Protector of England, 1599–1658

A minor East Anglian gentleman and member of Parliament, Cromwell was an unremarkable figure until middle age, when civil war broke out between King Charles I and the Parliament. A man of fierce Puritan conviction, he proved a soldier of genius. The disciplined cavalry he raised, the “Ironsides,” were decisive at Marston Moor, and his model was extended to the New Model Army that shattered the royalists at Naseby in 1645.

When negotiations with the captured king collapsed, Cromwell concluded that Charles could never be trusted. He pressed for the trial that, in 1649, sent the king to the scaffold and abolished the monarchy. As leader of the Commonwealth he campaigned with brutal thoroughness in Ireland, where the massacres at Drogheda and Wexford left a bitter memory, and crushed the Scots who had proclaimed the dead king’s son, the future Charles II, defeating him at Worcester.

In 1653 he dissolved the remnant of Parliament and accepted the title of Lord Protector, ruling as a king in all but name, refusing the crown itself when it was offered. His foreign policy made England feared once more, allied with Mazarin’s France against Spain. At home he sought a godly reformation while struggling to find a stable settlement. He died of fever in 1658; within two years the monarchy was restored, his body disinterred and posthumously hanged—yet he remains one of the most formidable and divisive figures in British history.
