Philippe Pétain

1856–1951, victor of Verdun and chief of the French State

Henri Philippe Bénoni Omer Joseph Pétain was born in Cauchy-à-la-Tour, a peasant village of the Pas-de-Calais, into a smallholding family. He went to Saint-Cyr without distinction in 1876 and spent thirty-eight years inching to the rank of colonel, an obscure infantry lecturer who taught that the defensive use of firepower would dominate the next war and was therefore passed over by a general staff committed to l'attaque à outrance. The catastrophe of 1914 vindicated him. By the spring of 1916 he was a corps commander, and when the Germans launched their offensive at Verdun on 21 February he was given the salient and told to hold it.

For ten months he rotated three quarters of the French army through the slaughter and organised the Voie sacrée, the single road on which trucks rolled day and night carrying men and shells, and the slogan attributed to him, "Ils ne passeront pas," became the rallying cry of the Republic. He took over as commander-in-chief in May 1917, restored order after the army mutinies with a combination of executions and improved leave, food and rotation, and emerged at the armistice as the most popular soldier in France, made a Marshal that November.

Between the wars he was ambassador to Franco's Spain and the embodiment of military caution. The débâcle of May 1940 brought him back as deputy and then prime minister at the age of eighty-four; on 22 June he signed the armistice with Hitler at Compiègne, and on 10 July the National Assembly at Vichy voted him full constitutional powers as head of the French State. His regime collaborated with the German occupation, deported Jews to the camps, and pursued a reactionary "National Revolution." Tried for treason in 1945 in a court presided over by his old protégé Charles de Gaulle, he was condemned to death; de Gaulle commuted the sentence to life imprisonment, and he died on the Île d'Yeu in July 1951.

The road continues to Georges Clemenceau.
