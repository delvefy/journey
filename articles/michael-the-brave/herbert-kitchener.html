Herbert Kitchener

1850–1916, Earl Kitchener of Khartoum, Secretary of State for War

Horatio Herbert Kitchener was born in Ballylongford, County Kerry, the second son of a retired colonel of irregular habit. He commissioned into the Royal Engineers in 1871, taught himself Arabic, and spent the next fifteen years on survey and intelligence work in Palestine, Cyprus and the Sudan. He was at the failed attempt to relieve General Gordon at Khartoum in January 1885, and when in 1896 the British government finally set about reconquering the Sudan, Kitchener as sirdar of the Egyptian army organised the slow advance up the Nile with a railway built behind him as he went. On 2 September 1898 at Omdurman his Maxim guns broke a charge of forty thousand Dervishes for the loss of fewer than fifty British dead — among the cavalry on the field was the young Winston Churchill — and the Mahdist state collapsed.

Sent to South Africa in 1900 as chief of staff and then as commander-in-chief, he beat the Boer commandos by gridding the veld with blockhouses and barbed wire and concentrating the rural civilian population in camps where disease killed more than twenty thousand. He went on to be commander-in-chief in India, where he broke with Lord Curzon, and then proconsul in Egypt. In August 1914, on the outbreak of war, Asquith made him secretary of state for war.

His face, glaring beneath a peaked cap on the recruiting poster captioned "Your Country Needs You," became the icon of British wartime mobilisation; the volunteer "Kitchener armies" of more than two million men were essentially his creation. On 5 June 1916, en route to Russia for negotiations with the Tsar, his cruiser HMS Hampshire struck a German mine off the Orkneys and sank with almost all hands, including the field-marshal himself. He was sixty-five and unmarried.
