Amenhotep III

Pharaoh of Egypt, r. c. 1386–1349 BC

Amenhotep III, sometimes called the Magnificent, ruled Egypt for nearly four decades at the very summit of its imperial power and splendour. The great-grandson of the conqueror Thutmose III, he inherited an empire already vast and secure, and rather than expand it by war he maintained it by wealth and diplomacy. The cuneiform letters preserved from his court show him corresponding as an equal with the kings of Babylon, Mitanni and the Hittites, cementing alliances with gold and with marriages to foreign princesses.

His Great Royal Wife was Tiye, a woman of non-royal birth but extraordinary influence, who bore him the heir who would become Akhenaten. His reign was an age of unrivalled artistic achievement and colossal building: he raised the elegant Luxor Temple, adorned Karnak, and erected a mortuary temple so vast that, though it has largely vanished, the two seated giants known as the Colossi of Memnon still guard its site — statues that a later age believed sang at dawn.

Egypt under Amenhotep III glittered as never before or after. Yet the calm concealed the coming storm, for his son would overturn the gods and the order of the realm, and his grandson Tutankhamun would be left to restore them. When Amenhotep died about 1349 BC, he left the throne to that son, and with him the golden age of the Eighteenth Dynasty passed into its strangest chapter.
