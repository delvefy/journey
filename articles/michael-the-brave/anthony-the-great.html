Anthony the Great

Egyptian hermit and father of monasticism, c. 251–356 AD

Anthony was born around 251 to a prosperous Christian family in middle Egypt. About the age of twenty, hearing in church the Gospel words to sell all one has and give to the poor, he gave away his inheritance, placed his sister in the care of a community of virgins, and devoted himself to a life of prayer, fasting, and manual labour under the guidance of older ascetics.

Seeking ever deeper solitude, he withdrew first to the tombs, then to a ruined fort at Pispir on the desert's edge, and finally to a remote mountain near the Red Sea. His long struggles there against temptation and demonic assault, vividly described by his biographer, became one of the most famous themes in Christian art. Though he sought to be alone, his holiness drew crowds of would-be disciples, and the deserts around him filled with hermits who looked to him as a father.

He did not invent the monastic life, but his example, more than any other, established and spread it, and he is rightly called the father of Christian monasticism. Twice he left his solitude for Alexandria: once during persecution to hearten the martyrs, and again in old age to denounce the teaching of Arius and lend his immense prestige to the Nicene cause of his friend Athanasius.

Emperors and bishops sought his counsel by letter, yet he remained an unlettered Coptic ascetic to the end. He died on his mountain around 356, reputedly aged one hundred and five, and through Athanasius's account his name became known throughout the Christian world.
