Gallienus

Roman emperor, r. 253–268

Gallienus was raised to the purple in 253 as co-emperor with his father Valerian, taking the western half of an empire convulsed by the great crisis of the third century. When Valerian was captured by the Persians in 260, Gallienus was left to rule alone over a realm that seemed to be coming apart: the Gallic provinces broke away under their own emperor in the West, the East fell under the sway of Palmyra, and a swarm of usurpers — the so-called Thirty Tyrants — rose on every frontier.

Against this tide he proved abler than his troubles suggest. He reformed the army, creating a mobile cavalry force and removing senators from military command in favour of professional officers, and he leaned on the Palmyrene prince Odaenathus to hold back Persia in the East. He also ended the persecution of the Christians, granting them toleration.

A cultivated ruler, Gallienus presided over a brief flowering of art and letters and was, with his wife, a devoted patron of philosophy. He drew to his court the great Neoplatonist Plotinus, and is said to have favoured the philosopher’s dream of founding a city to be governed by Plato’s laws. In 268, while besieging a usurper at Milan, Gallienus was murdered by his own officers, and the soldier Claudius Gothicus took his place — one in the grim succession of emperors made and unmade by the army.
