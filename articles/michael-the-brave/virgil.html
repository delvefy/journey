Virgil

Roman poet, author of the Aeneid, 70–19 BC

Publius Vergilius Maro was born near Mantua in northern Italy and rose, through the patronage of Maecenas and ultimately of the emperor Augustus himself, to become the supreme poet of Rome. His career unfolded against the violent end of the Republic and the founding of the imperial peace, and his work both reflected and celebrated that new order. He began with the Eclogues, pastoral poems of shepherds and song, and went on to the Georgics, a luminous didactic poem on farming and the Italian land.

His masterwork, the Aeneid, occupied his last eleven years. Consciously rivalling the Greek epics of Homer, it follows the Trojan prince Aeneas as he flees the sack of Troy, loves and abandons Queen Dido of Carthage, descends to the underworld, and at last wins a foothold in Italy from which Rome and the line of Augustus would spring. Grand, melancholy, and humane, it became at once the national epic of Rome and the central poem of the Latin tradition.

Virgil fell ill returning from Greece and died at Brundisium in 19 BC, leaving the Aeneid unfinished; he asked that it be burned, but Augustus ordered it preserved. Revered through antiquity and the Middle Ages as a sage and even a prophet, he was chosen by Dante as his guide through Hell and Purgatory, the very emblem of human wisdom.
