Pope Innocent III

Pope, c. 1160–1216

Lotario dei Conti di Segni was born about 1160 and elected pope in 1198, still in his thirties, the most commanding figure ever to hold the medieval papacy. Where his predecessors had claimed authority, he exercised it, declaring the pope set between God and man, below the one and above the other, and treating the crowns of Europe as instruments of his will. He humbled kings with the terrible weapons of interdict and excommunication.

England felt this most sharply. When King John refused to accept Stephen Langton as Archbishop of Canterbury, Innocent laid the whole kingdom under interdict and excommunicated the king, releasing his subjects from obedience until John capitulated in 1213 and received England back as a fief of the Holy See. France too was placed under interdict over Philip II Augustus’s tangled marriage, and Innocent arbitrated the disputed contest for the empire, ultimately throwing his weight behind his young ward, the future Frederick II.

His reach extended to faith and war alike. He proclaimed the Fourth Crusade, which to his dismay was diverted to storm Christian Constantinople in 1204; he loosed the Albigensian Crusade upon the Cathars of the south of France; and he gathered the Church at the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215, whose decrees on doctrine and discipline shaped Catholic life for centuries. Yet he also had the discernment to recognise something new and humble stirring in the Church, giving his blessing to the little band gathered around a poor man of Assisi rather than crushing it. Innocent died at Perugia in 1216, leaving the papacy at the zenith of its temporal power.
