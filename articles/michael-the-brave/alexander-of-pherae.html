Alexander of Pherae

Tyrant of Pherae in Thessaly, r. c. 369–358 BCE

Alexander of Pherae was tyrant of the Thessalian city of Pherae from about 369 to 358 BCE, a ruler whose name became a byword for cruelty. He seized power in the turbulent aftermath of the earlier Pheraean tyrants and sought to dominate all Thessaly, driving the cities of the region to appeal for outside help.

That appeal drew in Thebes, then at the height of its power. The Theban statesman Pelopidas came to Thessaly to check him and, on one embassy, was seized and held prisoner by the tyrant until a Theban army secured his release. The two became bitter enemies. In 364 BCE Pelopidas led a force against Alexander at Cynoscephalae; the Thebans won the battle, but Pelopidas was killed in the press, and the victors imposed harsh terms on the tyrant, stripping him of his wider power.

Ancient writers dwell on Alexander's savagery—accounts tell of victims buried alive or thrown to dogs—yet they also preserve the strange story that he once wept helplessly at a tragic play and had to leave the theater, ashamed to be seen pitying actors when he pitied no living man.

He was murdered in 358 BCE in a palace plot led by his own wife, Thebe, and her brothers—a fitting end, the moralists said, for a reign built on fear.
