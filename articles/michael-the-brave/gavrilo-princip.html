Gavrilo Princip

Assassin of Sarajevo, 1894–1918

Gavrilo Princip was the young Bosnian Serb whose pistol, fired on a Sarajevo street, set in motion the First World War. Born in 1894 to a poor peasant family in a Bosnia ruled by Austria-Hungary, he grew into a frail, bookish, and intense youth, consumed by the dream of freeing the South Slavs from Habsburg rule and uniting them in a single nation.

In Belgrade he was drawn into the conspiratorial movement known as Young Bosnia, which took the secret societies and tyrannicides of earlier European revolutionaries as its model. Armed and helped across the border by agents of the Serbian secret society called the Black Hand, Princip and a handful of companions resolved to strike at the visiting heir to the throne.

On the twenty-eighth of June 1914—Vidovdan, a day heavy with Serbian memory—an early bomb thrown by a fellow plotter failed. But later that morning the archduke's open car, having taken a wrong turn, stopped by chance directly before Princip. He stepped forward and fired twice, killing Franz Ferdinand and his wife.

Too young under Austrian law to hang, he was sentenced to twenty years and died of tuberculosis in the fortress of Terezín in 1918, before the war he had ignited had run its course. The deed was wildly out of proportion to the sickly student who did it. The revolutionary creed that had formed him drew on the nationalist and anarchist prophets of the previous century, among them Mikhail Bakunin.
