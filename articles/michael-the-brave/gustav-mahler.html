Gustav Mahler

Austrian composer and conductor, 1860–1911.

Born to a German-speaking Jewish family in the Bohemian village of Kalischt, Mahler was sent at fifteen to the Vienna Conservatory, where he studied composition and absorbed the rival worlds of Brahmsian classicism and Wagnerian music drama. His professional life was a steady climb up the German operatic ladder — Bad Hall, Laibach, Olmütz, Kassel, Prague, Leipzig, Budapest, Hamburg — and culminated in 1897 when, after a pro forma conversion to Catholicism to clear the antisemitic bar, he was appointed director of the Vienna Court Opera.

The Vienna decade is remembered as one of the great reigns in operatic history. With the stage designer Alfred Roller he produced visually integrated stagings of Mozart, Wagner, and Beethoven that displaced a generation of routine; as a conductor he was a tyrant for textual fidelity who reformed the orchestra's playing while alienating its players, the press, and the antisemitic Viennese public who eventually drove him out. He composed only in the summers, at lakeside huts on the Attersee, the Wörthersee, and at Toblach, producing the nine completed symphonies, an unfinished Tenth, and the song cycles — the Kindertotenlieder, the Rückert-Lieder, and the symphony-cycle hybrid Das Lied von der Erde — that he himself thought his most personal utterance.

In 1907 a daughter died, a heart condition was diagnosed, and he resigned Vienna for the Metropolitan Opera and the New York Philharmonic. His marriage to Alma Schindler, two decades his junior, ran into open crisis when she took the architect Walter Gropius as a lover; Mahler sought out Sigmund Freud, who in a single famous walk in Leiden diagnosed a mother fixation. He returned to Vienna fatally ill in 1911. His symphonies, dismissed in his lifetime as bloated and Jewish, became after 1960 a central pillar of the modern repertory.
