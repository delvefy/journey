Tertullian

Latin Church Father, c.155–220

Tertullian was born about 155 at Carthage, in Roman North Africa, and trained in rhetoric and perhaps law before his conversion to Christianity in middle life. He became the first great Christian writer to compose in Latin, and is justly called the father of Western theology: it was he who forged much of the vocabulary that the Latin Church would use ever after, coining or fixing such terms as Trinity, person and substance.

A polemicist of ferocious wit, he turned his pen against every enemy of the faith. In his Apology he defended the persecuted Christians and declared that the blood of the martyrs was the seed of the Church; in his vast treatise Against Marcion and in works against Valentinus and others he assailed the heretics; and against the philosophers he famously demanded, “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” — rejecting the easy marriage of Greek wisdom and the Gospel that the Alexandrians embraced.

Rigorous to the point of severity, he was drawn in his later years to the austere prophetic movement of the Montanists and broke with the mainstream Church, which is why, for all his genius, he was never numbered among the saints. Yet his influence was immense: the bishop Cyprian called him simply “the master,” Jerome recorded his life among the illustrious, and the whole Latin tradition that culminated in Augustine drew on his thought and his language. He died about 220, the founder of Christian literature in the West.
