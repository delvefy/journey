John Dryden

English poet, dramatist, and critic, 1631–1700

Dryden dominated the literature of Restoration England so completely that the age is often called the Age of Dryden. Coming to maturity as the monarchy returned, he greeted Charles II with the panegyric Astraea Redux and rose to be Poet Laureate and Historiographer Royal. In supple, vigorous couplets he made verse a weapon of public debate, above all in Absalom and Achitophel, a brilliant satire on the politics of the succession crisis, and in Mac Flecknoe, which buried a rival poet under mountains of ridicule.

For the reopened theatres he wrote a stream of heroic tragedies and comedies, among them All for Love, his reworking of the Antony and Cleopatra story, and adapted Milton’s Paradise Lost for the operatic stage. As a critic, his Essay of Dramatic Poesy and prefaces founded modern English literary criticism, written in a clear, conversational prose that set a new standard.

When the Catholic James II fell in 1688, Dryden—himself a recent convert—lost his offices, and in his last years turned to translation. His complete English Virgil, crowned by the Aeneid, was a national event, and his Fables Ancient and Modern recast tales from Chaucer, Boccaccio, and Ovid. He died in 1700 and was buried in Poets’ Corner beside Chaucer, the acknowledged master of his generation.
