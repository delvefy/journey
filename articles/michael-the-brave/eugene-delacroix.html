Eugène Delacroix

Leader of French Romantic painting, 1798–1863

Eugène Delacroix was born in 1798 at Charenton-Saint-Maurice near Paris, in years of revolution and Napoleonic war. His mother had connections to the foreign minister Talleyrand, whom rumour also gave him as a natural father. He trained under the academic Pierre-Narcisse Guérin and at the École des Beaux-Arts, but his real teacher was the studio of his older friend Théodore Géricault, whose Raft of the Medusa announced the violent new manner.

Delacroix entered the Salon with The Barque of Dante in 1822, followed by The Massacre at Chios in 1824, an enormous canvas of the Greek war for independence that drew on Lord Byron's verse and on news of fresh Ottoman atrocities. The Death of Sardanapalus of 1827, a riot of red and gold, set the agenda for Romantic excess. His Liberty Leading the People of 1830 turned the July Revolution into a permanent emblem of republican France.

A six-month journey to Morocco and Algeria in 1832 fed him for the rest of his life with the light, the costumes and the Arab horsemen that he painted again and again. He covered the libraries of the Palais Bourbon and the Senate with vast allegorical cycles, and a chapel in the Church of Saint-Sulpice with his last great commission, Jacob Wrestling with the Angel.

He kept a journal famous for its acute thinking on colour, on art, and on himself; he was painted in his Paris circle alongside Frédéric Chopin and George Sand, and championed in print by Charles Baudelaire, whose Salon reviews canonised him. After his death in 1863 his palette of complementaries fed directly into Manet, Van Gogh and Cézanne. But the older painter on whose model he formed himself, who died too young to know his fame, was Théodore Géricault.
