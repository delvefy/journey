John Calvin

French Protestant reformer, 1509–1564

Calvin was born at Noyon in Picardy in 1509 and trained as a humanist scholar and lawyer in Paris and Orléans. Won to the cause of reform, he fled the persecution of Protestants in France, and in 1536, still in his twenties, published the first edition of the Institutes of the Christian Religion, a work he would expand throughout his life into the most systematic and influential statement of Reformed theology, building upon the breach that Luther had opened.

Passing through Geneva, he was pressed by the reformer Farel to stay and help organise its church. After an interval of exile he returned in 1541 to make the city the capital of the Reformed faith, imposing a rigorous moral discipline through its Consistory and turning Geneva into both a model Christian commonwealth and a haven for Protestant refugees. The burning of the anti-Trinitarian Michael Servetus in 1553 left a lasting shadow over his rule.

Calvin’s theology stressed the absolute sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, and the doctrine of predestination, and he gave his followers a disciplined model of church government. From the academy he founded in Geneva his ideas spread across Europe, shaping the French Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, the Puritans of England, and, through his pupil John Knox, the Presbyterians of Scotland. Few thinkers have left a deeper mark on the religious and social life of the modern West.
