Robert Hooke

English natural philosopher and polymath, 1635–1703

One of the most inventive minds of the scientific revolution, Hooke began as assistant to Robert Boyle, for whom he built the air pump that made possible Boyle’s famous experiments on gases. As Curator of Experiments to the new Royal Society he was charged with devising fresh demonstrations at every meeting, a task that drew on his seemingly inexhaustible ingenuity in mechanics, optics, and instrument-making.

In 1665 he published Micrographia, a book of breathtaking engravings of fleas, flies, and the structure of cork, in which he coined the biological term “cell.” It made microscopy a sensation and inspired observers across Europe. He formulated the law of elasticity that bears his name, worked on the balance spring for watches, grasped the inverse-square nature of gravitation, and contributed to astronomy, palaeontology, and a dozen other fields.

After the Great Fire devastated London in 1666, Hooke served as a city surveyor and worked hand in hand with his close friend Christopher Wren to replan and rebuild the capital, designing buildings and the towering Monument to the fire. His later years were soured by a bitter priority dispute with Isaac Newton over gravitation and the nature of light. Brilliant but quarrelsome, and long overshadowed by his great rival, Hooke is now recognised as a founder of experimental science.
