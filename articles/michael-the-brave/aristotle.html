Aristotle

Greek philosopher, 384–322 BC

Aristotle was born in 384 BC at Stagira in northern Greece, the son of a physician attached to the Macedonian court. At seventeen he travelled to Athens and entered the Academy of Plato, where he remained for some twenty years, the most brilliant of the pupils, until his master’s death. Departing Athens, he spent years on the Aegean coast and the island of Lesbos pursuing the patient observation of animals and plants that makes him the founder of biology as well as of so much else.

About 343 BC King Philip II of Macedon summoned him to tutor his son, the boy who would become Alexander the Great. The pairing of the supreme philosopher and the supreme conqueror has fascinated every later age. Around 335 BC Aristotle returned to Athens and founded his own school, the Lyceum, whose members were called Peripatetics from his habit of teaching as he walked.

His achievement is almost without parallel in its range. He created formal logic with the theory of the syllogism; he wrote foundational works on metaphysics, the soul, physics, rhetoric and poetics; and in the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics he framed questions about virtue, happiness and the state that are still argued today. Where Plato looked to eternal Forms, Aristotle turned to the evidence of the senses and the analysis of things as they are. After Alexander’s death in 323 BC, anti-Macedonian feeling turned against him, and rather than let Athens, as he put it, sin twice against philosophy, he withdrew to Chalcis, where he died the following year. To the medieval world he was simply the Philosopher, and his authority shaped Western and Islamic thought for the better part of two thousand years.

The road continues to Alexander the Great.
