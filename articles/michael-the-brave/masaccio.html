Masaccio

Florentine painter, 1401–1428

Tommaso di Ser Giovanni di Mone Cassai was born on the feast of Saint Thomas, 21 December 1401, at Castel San Giovanni di Valdarno, the son of a notary who died young. The family nickname Masaccio — clumsy Tom — stuck to him as a working name. He matriculated in the Florentine guild of physicians and apothecaries, the painters’ arm, in January 1422, by which time he was already living in Florence. He worked alongside the older Masolino da Panicale, fifteen years his senior; the joint Sant’Anna Metterza for the church of Sant’Ambrogio, with the Virgin and Child by Masaccio and the Saint Anne by Masolino, dates from about 1424 and shows the new manner already complete: figures with weight and shadow, faces of plain Tuscan people, drapery that hangs the way drapery does.

The Brancacci Chapel in Santa Maria del Carmine, which the silk merchant Felice Brancacci had endowed and which Masolino and Masaccio painted together starting in 1424, contains the great body of his surviving work. The Tribute Money, in which a continuous narrative of the Gospel passage unfolds in a single panoramic landscape under one source of cold light; the Expulsion of Adam and Eve, in which the first couple stagger weeping out of Eden; the Saint Peter Distributing Alms and Saint Peter Healing with His Shadow — these set out, in a single decade, the programme that Leonardo, Raphael, and Michelangelo would take up. About 1425 he painted, for Santa Maria Novella, the Trinity fresco: the first picture in Western art constructed mathematically in true Brunelleschian perspective, with a barrel-vaulted chapel that recedes through the wall.

In late 1427 or early 1428 he went to Rome, called either by Masolino or by his own ambition to look again at the antique. He died there, suddenly, in the autumn of 1428, twenty-six years old. The cause was never established; the gossip Vasari reports, that he was poisoned by a rival painter, is on no good evidence. The Brancacci was finished by Filippino Lippi after the chapel had stood half-painted for nearly sixty years, and the half-century’s painters who pilgrimaged to it — Fra Angelico, Botticelli, Leonardo, Raphael, Michelangelo — left their copies in their notebooks. Vasari, writing more than a hundred years later, called him the first painter who put the modern manner of art into practice.
