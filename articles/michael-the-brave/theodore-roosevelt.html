Theodore Roosevelt

26th President of the United States, 1858–1919

A sickly, asthmatic boy who remade himself into a champion of “the strenuous life,” Roosevelt poured ferocious energy into everything he touched—Harvard, the New York legislature, cattle ranching in the Dakota Badlands, and reform of the city police. As Assistant Secretary of the Navy he agitated for war with Spain, then resigned to raise a volunteer cavalry regiment, the Rough Riders, and led their celebrated charge at San Juan Hill in 1898.

Elected vice-president, he became, at forty-two, the youngest president in history when an anarchist assassinated William McKinley in 1901. He wielded power with relish, breaking up the great trusts, securing a “Square Deal” for ordinary Americans, and pushing through the Pure Food and Drug Act. A passionate naturalist, he set aside vast tracts of forest and created national parks and monuments, often in the company of the conservationist John Muir. He forced through the Panama Canal, sent the Great White Fleet around the globe, and won the Nobel Peace Prize for mediating the Russo-Japanese War.

After leaving office in 1909 he hunted in Africa and explored an uncharted Amazon river that nearly killed him. Embittered by his successor Taft, he split the Republican Party in 1912 by running as the “Bull Moose” candidate—surviving an assassin’s bullet to finish a speech—and so handed victory to Woodrow Wilson. He died in 1919, his restless face later carved into Mount Rushmore.
