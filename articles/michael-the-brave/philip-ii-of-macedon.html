Philip II of Macedon

King of Macedon, 382–336 BCE

Philip II came to the throne of Macedon in 359 BCE, inheriting a backward, divided kingdom threatened on every side. As a youth he had been a hostage at Thebes, then the foremost military power in Greece, where he studied the revolutionary tactics of its great general Epaminondas. He returned home determined to remake his realm.

He transformed the Macedonian army into the most formidable force of the age, arming his infantry with the long pike, the sarissa, drilling them into a deep, bristling phalanx, and combining them with heavy cavalry and siege engines in a professional standing army loyal to himself. With it, and with lavish bribery and cunning diplomacy, he extended Macedon's power year by year over Thessaly, Thrace, and the cities of the north.

His growing dominance alarmed the southern Greeks, and at Athens the orator Demosthenes thundered against him in the speeches known as the Philippics. But in 338 BCE, at the Battle of Chaeronea, Philip and his young son Alexander crushed the combined armies of Athens and Thebes, ending the age of the free city-states. He bound the Greeks into the League of Corinth under his own leadership.

Master of Greece, Philip proclaimed a great war of vengeance against the Persian Empire and sent an advance force into Asia. But in 336, at the wedding of his daughter, he was struck down by an assassin's dagger. The conquest he had planned fell to his son Alexander, who, wielding Philip's superb army, would carry it farther than Philip himself had ever dreamed.
