Cato the Elder

Roman statesman, orator, and censor, 234–149 BC

Marcus Porcius Cato was born of sturdy farming stock at Tusculum and rose as a "new man" without noble ancestry. He learned soldiering in the long war against Hannibal, serving under Fabius Maximus, and then climbed the whole ladder of Roman office—quaestor, aedile, praetor in Sardinia, consul in 195 BC, and finally censor in 184. In each post he made himself the champion of old Roman simplicity against what he saw as the corrupting tide of Greek luxury and wealth.

It was his censorship that fixed his fame and his title. He revised the senatorial rolls, struck at extravagance with heavy taxes, and disciplined public morals with a severity that became legendary. He was also a pioneer of Latin letters: the first to publish his orations, the author of the oldest surviving work of Latin prose, a handbook on farming, and of the Origines, the first history of Rome written in Latin. His running feud with the great Scipio Africanus, whom he helped drive from public life, showed the hard edge of his politics.

In old age, sent on an embassy to Carthage, he was alarmed to find the old enemy prosperous once more, and thereafter he is said to have ended every speech in the Senate, on whatever subject, with the words "Carthage must be destroyed." His relentless agitation helped bring on the Third Punic War, which opened in the very year of his death in 149 BC. His austere example endured for centuries, not least in his great-grandson, who would bear his name into the last struggles of the Republic.
