Khosrow II

Last great Sasanian King of Kings, r. 590–628

Khosrow II, called Parviz, “the Victorious,” was the grandson of Khosrow I and the last of the great Sasanian kings. He came to the throne in 590 amid the murder of his father, only to be driven out almost at once by the rebel general Bahram Chobin. He fled to the Byzantines, and the Emperor Maurice restored him to power in exchange for territory — a debt of gratitude that shaped the catastrophe to come.

When Maurice was overthrown and killed in 602, Khosrow seized the pretext to make war on the empire that had abandoned his benefactor. For more than two decades his armies swept all before them, overrunning Syria, taking Jerusalem in 614 — carrying off the relic of the True Cross — conquering Egypt, and in 626 standing before the walls of Constantinople itself. The Sasanian realm reached its greatest extent, and his court at Ctesiphon overflowed with plunder; in Persian legend his love for the Christian queen Shirin became an immortal romance.

But the Emperor Heraclius struck back with daring, carrying the war into the Persian heartland and shattering Khosrow’s forces near Nineveh in 627. Defeat bred rebellion, and in 628 Khosrow was deposed and put to death by his own son. The long war had bled both empires white, and within a generation the exhausted Sasanian state fell to the armies of Islam — the same faith whose Prophet, Muhammad, had, by tradition, sent Khosrow a letter calling him to submit, which the king of kings contemptuously tore to pieces.
