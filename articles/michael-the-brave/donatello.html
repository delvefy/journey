Donatello

Florentine sculptor, c.1386–1466

Donato di Niccolò di Betto Bardi — Donatello by Florentine diminution — was born about 1386 at Florence, the son of a wool-carder who had been on the wrong side of the Ciompi revolt. He learnt his trade in the workshop of Lorenzo Ghiberti during the casting of the first Baptistery doors, then accompanied his older friend Brunelleschi to Rome in the early 1400s to draw the antique. He returned with eyes trained on the human body of late-classical sculpture and gave the first generation of Florentine humanists the figures they had been arguing for: the David in marble (1408) for the cathedral, the Saint Mark on Orsanmichele (1411–13) that Michelangelo would call the most honest portrait of an apostle ever cut, and the Saint George (c.1416) whose pedestal relief of the dragon is the earliest surviving Italian work in true linear perspective.

For the Medici and the great Florentine guilds he produced a body of work whose virtuosity and emotional range had no parallel. The bronze David, naked and ambiguous, of around 1440 — the first free-standing male nude since antiquity — stood in the courtyard of Cosimo’s new palace. The Cantoria for the cathedral, finished in 1438, was a riot of putti in a frieze of golden mosaic. Through Cosimo’s good offices he was lent in 1443 to the Republic of Venice to cast the equestrian Gattamelata in Padua, the first life-size bronze rider since the Marcus Aurelius on the Capitoline, and the model for every equestrian monument since. He stayed in Padua for ten years, leaving the high altar of the Santo with its great Crucifix and four bronze reliefs.

The late work was harsher and stranger. The wooden Magdalene for the Baptistery is gaunt and emaciated almost to caricature; the pulpits of San Lorenzo, his last commission from Cosimo and his son Piero, treat the Passion in a crowded, almost expressionist relief whose figures press out of their frames. Donatello died at Florence on 13 December 1466, two years after his patron, and was buried at his own request beside Cosimo in San Lorenzo. He had taught a generation: Bertoldo di Giovanni in his workshop carried the manner forward into the boyhood of Michelangelo, and the bronze methods he had perfected were the ground from which Verrocchio, Pollaiuolo, and the rest were to work.
