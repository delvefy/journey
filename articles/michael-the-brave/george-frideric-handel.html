George Frideric Handel

German-born English Baroque composer, 1685–1759

Born in Halle the same year as Bach, in the same corner of Germany, Handel took a very different path. Against his father’s wishes he devoted himself to music, learned his craft in Hamburg, and then spent formative years in Italy, where he mastered the Italian operatic style and won fame in Rome, Florence, and Venice. Cosmopolitan and ambitious where Bach was rooted and provincial, he eventually made his home in London and became a naturalised British subject.

For decades he dominated the London stage with Italian opera, composing dazzling works such as Rinaldo and Giulio Cesare and running opera companies through triumphs and financial ruin alike. He enjoyed royal favour from the first, writing music for Queen Anne and later for the Hanoverian kings; the suites known as the Water Music were performed for George I on the Thames, and the Music for the Royal Fireworks celebrated a later peace.

When public taste turned against Italian opera, Handel reinvented himself with the English oratorio, a form he made his own. The greatest of these, Messiah, with its famous “Hallelujah” chorus, became one of the most beloved works in all of music. Grand, dramatic, and tuneful, his music made him a national institution; he was mourned as a public figure and buried in Westminster Abbey, his oratorios remaining a living part of English musical life ever after.

The road continues to George I of Great Britain.
