Louis Armstrong

American jazz trumpeter and singer, 1901–1971.

Born in the slums of New Orleans to a teenage mother who at times turned to prostitution, Armstrong grew up amid the city's brass-band funerals, river-boat dances, and Storyville bars. Arrested at twelve for firing a pistol on New Year's Eve and sent to the Colored Waif's Home, he learned the cornet there and on release began playing parade and dance jobs around the Black Storyville district. The bandleader Joe "King" Oliver took him as a protégé, and in 1922 summoned him north to join the Creole Jazz Band in Chicago, the engagement that lifted him out of New Orleans and into the recorded history of jazz.

From his Hot Five and Hot Seven sessions of 1925–1928 — "Potato Head Blues," "West End Blues," "Heebie Jeebies," whose stumbled scat chorus he popularized as a vocal style — Armstrong remade jazz from a collective ensemble music into a soloist's art. His trumpet phrasing, with its long swung lines, syncopated displacements, and sovereign command of pitch and dynamics, set the rhythmic vocabulary every later horn player would inherit. From the 1930s he led big bands, toured the world as "Ambassador Satch" under State Department sponsorship, and reached the largest popular audience of any African-American entertainer of his generation, with hits like "Hello, Dolly!" (which knocked the Beatles off the top of the charts in 1964) and his sentimental late single "What a Wonderful World."

His comic stage persona was sometimes attacked by younger Black musicians as Uncle Tom mugging, but in 1957 he cancelled a State Department tour of the Soviet Union to protest President Eisenhower's failure to enforce school integration at Little Rock, telling reporters that the President had "no guts" — a remark that helped push the federal troops into Arkansas. He died in his sleep in Queens in 1971, the most widely loved musician of the American century.
