Hiero II of Syracuse

King of Syracuse, c. 308–215 BCE

Hiero rose from the army of Syracuse to seize power in the turbulent years after the Greek adventurer Pyrrhus of Epirus withdrew from Sicily. Elected general and then proclaimed king around 270 BCE, he soon faced the outbreak of the great struggle between Rome and Carthage for control of the island.

At first he sided with Carthage, but with shrewd judgement he made peace with Rome in 263, at the start of the First Punic War, and bound Syracuse to the Roman cause. For the next half-century he remained Rome's faithful ally, supplying grain and money to her armies, and in return his city enjoyed an unbroken peace and prosperity rare in that violent age.

His long reign was a golden age for Syracuse. He governed wisely, framed a famous and equitable system of taxation, and adorned the city with great public works. Above all he was the patron and kinsman of Archimedes, the greatest scientist of antiquity, who served him by designing engines of war, the colossal ship Syracusia, and—by the famous story—by detecting the fraud in the king's golden crown.

Hiero died about 215 BCE, nearly ninety years old, in the midst of the Second Punic War, having kept Syracuse loyal to Rome even as Hannibal ravaged Italy. His death proved a calamity for the city: his young grandson Hieronymus rashly went over to Carthage, drawing down upon Syracuse the Roman siege in which Archimedes himself would perish.
