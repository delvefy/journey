Adam Smith

Scottish economist and moral philosopher, 1723–1790

A central figure of the Scottish Enlightenment, Smith was born in the port town of Kirkcaldy and became professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow. His first great work, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), grounded ethics in human sympathy—our capacity to imagine ourselves in another’s place. Throughout his life his closest companion was the philosopher David Hume, whose friendship shaped his thinking and whose serene death Smith movingly described.

In the 1760s Smith left academic life to tutor a young duke on a grand tour of the Continent. In France and Switzerland he met leading thinkers of the day, including the aged Voltaire, whom he revered, and the physiocrats who were rethinking the sources of national wealth. The leisure of these years allowed him to begin his masterpiece.

Published in 1776, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations founded the modern study of economics. In it Smith explained how the division of labour multiplies productivity, how self-interested exchange is guided as if by an “invisible hand” to serve the common good, and why the artificial restraints of mercantilism impoverish nations. He argued for free markets and limited but essential government, and his ideas would dominate economic thought for generations and shape the policies of the industrialising world.
