Gordian III

Roman emperor, r. 238–244

Gordian III was born in 225, the grandson of the elderly senator Gordian who, with his son, raised the revolt in Africa against Maximinus Thrax in the turbulent year 238. When that rebellion swiftly collapsed and the two Gordians died, the Senate set up emperors of its own, Pupienus and Balbinus, but the people of Rome clamoured for the boy Gordian, and he was made Caesar. After Maximinus was killed by his troops and the Praetorian Guard murdered Pupienus and Balbinus, the thirteen-year-old was left, almost by accident, sole emperor.

Power, of course, lay in other hands. For some years the empire was ably steered by his father-in-law, the praetorian prefect Timesitheus. Under that guidance the regime stabilized and met a new danger in the East, where the resurgent Sasanian Persia of Shapur I pressed on Roman Mesopotamia.

The young emperor went to war against Shapur, and at first the campaign prospered. But Timesitheus died, and his place was taken by the ambitious Philip, an Arab officer. In 244 Gordian III died on the Persian frontier — whether killed in a defeat that Shapur boasted of in his great inscription, or murdered in a plot by Philip, the sources disagree. He was not yet twenty. Philip the Arab took the purple in his stead, and the boy emperor passed into the long roll of the short-lived rulers of the third-century crisis.
