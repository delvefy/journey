Edward III

King of England, 1312–1377

Edward of Windsor was born on 13 November 1312 at Windsor Castle, the eldest son of Edward II and Isabella of France. He was fourteen when his mother and her lover Roger Mortimer deposed his father in 1327 and crowned him king; for the next three years he ruled in name while Mortimer governed in fact. In October 1330 Edward, with a handful of companions, broke into Mortimer's lodgings at Nottingham Castle through a secret passage and arrested him, sending him to the gallows at Tyburn. He had married Philippa of Hainault in 1328; their long and affectionate marriage produced thirteen children, including the future Black Prince and John of Gaunt.

The reign was dominated by war. A first Scottish campaign won the battle of Halidon Hill in 1333. In 1337 he revived his claim to the French throne through his mother, opening what later writers would call the Hundred Years' War. The naval victory at Sluys in 1340 was followed by the crushing of a far larger French army at Crécy on 26 August 1346, where the English longbow and the cool tactical sense of the sixteen-year-old Black Prince won an evening of slaughter; Calais surrendered the following year. After the interlude of the Black Death of 1348–50 — which prompted the Statute of Labourers and the Ordinance of Labourers — Edward won an even more complete victory at Poitiers in 1356, where King John II of France was captured. The Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 gave him an enlarged Aquitaine in full sovereignty in exchange for renouncing the French crown.

At home he created institutions that long outlived him: the Order of the Garter in 1348, the staple of wool at Calais, the use of English in the law courts, and a chancery and exchequer governed jointly with Parliament. The war turned against him after 1369; his beloved Philippa died in 1369 and the Black Prince in 1376; the Good Parliament impeached his ministers and pensioned off his mistress Alice Perrers. Edward died at Sheen on 21 June 1377, having reigned fifty years.
