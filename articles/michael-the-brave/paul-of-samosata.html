Paul of Samosata

Bishop of Antioch and heresiarch, fl. 260–272 AD

Paul of Samosata became bishop of the great city of Antioch around 260, at a time when Syria lay under the sway of Palmyra. Alongside his church office he held a lucrative civil post in the administration of the Palmyrene queen Zenobia, and his critics accused him of living like a secular grandee—surrounded by retainers, seated on a lofty tribunal, and revelling in wealth and pomp unbecoming a bishop.

His teaching troubled his fellow bishops still more. Paul was said to hold a form of “monarchian” or adoptionist doctrine: that Christ was not the eternal Son made flesh but a man, born of Mary, in whom the divine Word of God had come to dwell and who was thereby raised to divine dignity. To his opponents this denied the true and eternal divinity of Christ.

A series of synods met at Antioch to examine him. The respected bishop Firmilian of Caesarea guided the proceedings, and other leading churchmen took part. At the final synod, around 268, Paul was condemned, deposed, and excommunicated, and the priest Malchion exposed his errors in formal debate.

Protected by Zenobia, however, Paul simply refused to surrender the church buildings and remained in possession. Only in 272, when the emperor Aurelian crushed Palmyra and retook Antioch, was the matter resolved: appealed to as arbiter, Aurelian ruled that the church should be given to the bishop recognised by the bishops of Italy and Rome—and Paul was at last expelled. The case became a famous early precedent for imperial intervention in a dispute of the Church.
