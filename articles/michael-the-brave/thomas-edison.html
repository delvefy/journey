Thomas Edison

American inventor and businessman, 1847–1931

The most prolific inventor of the modern age, Edison held over a thousand patents and transformed everyday life more than almost any man of his century. A near-deaf former telegraph operator with little formal schooling, he built at Menlo Park, New Jersey, the world’s first industrial research laboratory—an “invention factory” that turned discovery into a systematic enterprise and earned him the nickname “the Wizard of Menlo Park.” Genius, he famously declared, was one percent inspiration and ninety-nine percent perspiration.

From his workshops poured a torrent of inventions: the phonograph, which first recorded and replayed the human voice; vast improvements to the telephone and telegraph; and an early motion-picture system. His crowning achievement was the practical incandescent light bulb and, with it, the entire system of electrical generation and distribution needed to light cities. The electric companies he founded grew into a great industrial empire.

His commitment to direct current embroiled him in the bitter “War of the Currents” against his former employee Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse, a contest he ultimately lost. Yet his prestige only grew. He befriended and mentored the young Henry Ford, and the two industrial titans remained close for the rest of their lives. Honoured around the world, Edison worked nearly to the end, dying in 1931; on the night of his funeral, lights were dimmed across America in tribute.
