Cecil Rhodes

1853–1902, mining magnate and Prime Minister of the Cape Colony

Cecil John Rhodes was born in Bishop's Stortford, Hertfordshire, the fifth son of a vicar of the Church of England. A weak chest sent him out at seventeen to join his elder brother Herbert on the cotton fields of Natal, and from there in 1871 to the new diamond diggings at Kimberley. Between bouts of digging he matriculated as an undergraduate at Oriel College, Oxford, in 1873, taking eight years over a degree he interrupted to consolidate the Kimberley claims. With the financier Alfred Beit and the credit of Nathan Rothschild he absorbed his rival Barney Barnato and in 1888 formed De Beers Consolidated Mines, which by his death controlled nine-tenths of the world's diamond supply.

Diamonds were merely the means. Rhodes wanted a continuous British dominion "from the Cape to Cairo" and a federation of the English-speaking peoples. He bought the gold reefs of the Witwatersrand, obtained from Lobengula of the Ndebele the concession that became Rhodesia in 1890, and used his British South Africa Company to extend Crown rule north of the Limpopo by chartered force. As prime minister of the Cape from 1890 he conciliated the Afrikaner Bond at first, but his career was wrecked in January 1896 when his lieutenant Leander Starr Jameson, with his connivance, led a botched filibustering raid into the South African Republic with the aim of overthrowing Paul Kruger. Rhodes resigned the premiership and the chartered company's directorship.

He nonetheless held Kimberley through its siege in the Boer War, courted Rudyard Kipling at the Cape, and from his retreat at Groote Schuur drafted the will that would endow the Rhodes Scholarships at Oxford for students drawn from the English-speaking world and Germany. He died of heart failure at Muizenberg in March 1902, aged forty-eight, and was buried, by his own direction, on the granite hilltop of Matobo in Rhodesia overlooking the World's View.

The road continues to Rudyard Kipling.
