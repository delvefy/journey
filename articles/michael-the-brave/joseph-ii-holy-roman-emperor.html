Joseph II, Holy Roman Emperor

Holy Roman Emperor, 1741–1790

Joseph II was the great reforming emperor of the Enlightenment, the very model of the philosopher on the throne. Born in 1741, the eldest son of Maria Theresa, he became Holy Roman Emperor on his father's death in 1765 and co-ruled the Habsburg dominions with his mother, chafing for two decades against her caution before becoming sole ruler in 1780.

Then the reforms poured out. In a single decade he abolished serfdom, granted toleration to Protestants, Orthodox, and Jews, dissolved hundreds of monasteries he judged useless, overhauled the law and the taxes, and asserted the state's control over the Catholic Church—the program later called Josephinism. But his headlong pace and contempt for local custom bred revolt from Hungary to the Austrian Netherlands, and much of his work was undone; embittered, he is said to have composed his own epitaph as a man who failed in all he attempted.

In foreign affairs he allied with Catherine the Great against the Ottomans, sparred with Frederick the Great over Bavaria, and travelled to France to counsel his sister Marie Antoinette on her troubled marriage.

A devoted patron of music in his Vienna, he supported Gluck, kept Salieri at his court, and championed Mozart, commissioning operas and supplying the famous verdict that one of them had "too many notes." When he died in 1790, mourned in an early cantata by the young Beethoven, he left his throne to his brother Leopold II.
