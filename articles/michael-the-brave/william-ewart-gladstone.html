William Ewart Gladstone

British statesman and Prime Minister, 1809–1898

The towering Liberal statesman of the Victorian age, Gladstone served as Prime Minister four separate times across three decades, longer than any other figure of his century. He entered Parliament in 1832 as a high Tory, a protégé of Sir Robert Peel, and followed Peel out of the party over the repeal of the Corn Laws. Step by step he moved leftward, becoming a reforming Chancellor of the Exchequer of legendary thrift and finally the conscience and leader of the Liberal Party.

His career was defined by a famous and bitter duel with the Conservative Benjamin Disraeli, the two men personifying the opposing temperaments of the era. As premier he extended the vote to working men, introduced the secret ballot, opened the civil service to merit, and established national elementary education. Above all he was consumed in his later years by the cause of Home Rule for Ireland, which split his party and twice failed but reshaped British politics for a generation.

A man of fierce moral and religious earnestness, a classical scholar who wrote on Homer and championed the excavator Schliemann, the “Grand Old Man” was adored by the public and heartily disliked by Queen Victoria, who found his lecturing manner insufferable and preferred his rival. He retired at last in 1894, aged eighty-four, and died four years later, mourned as a giant of parliamentary democracy.
