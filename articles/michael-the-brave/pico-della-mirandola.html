Pico della Mirandola

Italian humanist, “Phoenix of the Wits,” 1463–1494

Giovanni Pico was born on 24 February 1463 at Mirandola, in the small Po-valley county of which his family were sovereigns. He was a child of prodigious memory — said to know the Aeneid by heart at ten — and made the round of the Italian universities: canon law at Bologna at fourteen, philosophy at Ferrara, Padua under the Averroist Nicoletto Vernia, Pavia, and Paris. By twenty-three he had read the Greek philosophers in the original, learnt Hebrew and Aramaic to read the Cabala, taken some Arabic, and conceived a programme that no scholar had attempted: to demonstrate that all the philosophical and theological traditions of the human race — Hellenic, Latin, Hebrew, Cabalistic, Hermetic, Islamic, Christian — were in fundamental agreement on the truths of religion.

In December 1486 he posted at Rome his nine hundred theses, the Conclusiones, drawn from every tradition he had read; he invited the scholars of Europe to come to Rome to dispute them and offered to pay their travel. The Oratio de hominis dignitate, written as the prefatory address — with its great fable of God planting the human, alone among creatures, without a fixed nature, free to make himself into beast or angel — became the most famous statement of Renaissance humanism. Innocent VIII appointed a commission, which condemned thirteen of the theses as suspect. Pico published an Apology defending them; the pope condemned the lot; Pico fled to France, was briefly arrested at Vincennes, and was rescued only by the intervention of Lorenzo de’ Medici, who brought him home to Florence under cover of his protection.

The Florentine years made him a friend of Marsilio Ficino at Careggi and a follower of Savonarola at San Marco, whose vehement preaching he had heard, perhaps with his own conversion in mind. He produced the Heptaplus, a Cabalistic commentary on Genesis (1489), and the unfinished Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem, the first sustained intellectual assault on judicial astrology. Innocent’s successor Alexander VI absolved him in 1493. Pico died at Florence on 17 November 1494, the same day Charles VIII entered the city; the cause was attributed to a fever but, in 2007, exhumation revealed arsenic poisoning, by whose hand has never been settled. He was buried beside his friend Poliziano in the convent of San Marco, both in the habit of the order he had been about to take.

The road continues to Savonarola.
