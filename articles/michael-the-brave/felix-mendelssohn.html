Felix Mendelssohn

German composer, 1809–1847

Mendelssohn was born in Hamburg into a wealthy and cultivated family, a grandson of the Enlightenment philosopher Moses Mendelssohn. A prodigy whose early gifts rivalled Mozart’s, he wrote the sparkling Octet and the overture to A Midsummer Night’s Dream while still in his teens, and as a youth was brought to play for the aged Goethe, who delighted in him as in a second Mozart.

In 1829, at the age of twenty, he conducted a revival of Bach’s St Matthew Passion in Berlin, the first performance since the composer’s death, an event that ignited the modern rediscovery of Bach. Travel furnished further inspiration: a visit to Scotland produced the Hebrides overture and the "Scottish" Symphony, and Italy the radiant "Italian" Symphony. He went on to found the Leipzig Conservatory and to make the city’s Gewandhaus Orchestra the finest in Europe.

His mature works, the beloved Violin Concerto in E minor, the oratorios St Paul and Elijah in the grand tradition of Handel, and the famous Wedding March, combined romantic warmth with classical poise and made him hugely popular, not least in England, where he was a favourite of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert. Tireless to the point of self-destruction, he was shattered by the sudden death of his sister Fanny, herself a gifted composer, and died only months later in 1847, aged just thirty-eight.
