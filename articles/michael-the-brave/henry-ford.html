Henry Ford

American industrialist, founder of the Ford Motor Company, 1863–1947

Born on a farm near Dearborn, Michigan, Ford preferred machinery to ploughing and rose to become chief engineer at the Edison Illuminating Company in Detroit. There he built his first horseless carriage, the Quadricycle, in 1896, and won the personal encouragement of Thomas Edison, who told the young man to keep at his gasoline engine. After two failed ventures he founded the Ford Motor Company in 1903.

His genius lay less in invention than in production. The Model T of 1908 was a simple, rugged, affordable car for ordinary people, and to build it cheaply Ford perfected the moving assembly line at his Highland Park plant in 1913, cutting the time to assemble a car from hours to minutes. In 1914 he stunned industry by paying workers five dollars a day—roughly double the going wage—reducing turnover and turning his own workforce into customers. “Fordism” became shorthand for mass production itself, and the vast River Rouge complex its monument.

Ford put America on wheels, but his record is shadowed. He clung to the Model T long past its day, fought bitterly against unions until 1941, and used his newspaper, The Dearborn Independent, to publish vicious antisemitic tracts that were admired abroad; in 1938 he accepted a decoration from Nazi Germany. He died in 1947, having reshaped not only manufacturing but the texture of modern life, leaving the company to his grandson.
