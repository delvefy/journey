Lord Byron

English Romantic poet, 1788–1824

George Gordon, the sixth Baron Byron, awoke one morning in 1812 to find himself famous. The first cantos of Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage, recounting a brooding young wanderer’s travels through a Europe convulsed by war, made him the literary sensation of Regency London. Handsome, lame, aristocratic, and scandalous, he created in his verse and his person the figure that bears his name—the “Byronic hero,” the gifted, guilt-haunted, defiant outsider who would haunt European Romanticism.

Driven from England in 1816 by debt and the scandal of his broken marriage and rumoured incest, he went into permanent exile. By Lake Geneva he spent the famous summer with Percy and Mary Shelley that gave birth to Frankenstein; in Italy he settled into the life of a celebrated and dissolute expatriate. There he wrote his masterpiece, the sprawling, satirical, endlessly digressive Don Juan, which turned the old legend of the seducer into a comic survey of the whole modern world.

Ever in love with liberty and with action, Byron threw his fame and fortune behind the Greek struggle for independence from the Ottomans. He sailed to Missolonghi to organise the cause, but fell ill and died of fever in 1824, aged thirty-six. Greece mourned him as a national hero, and his legend swept the Continent, admired by Goethe and imitated by a generation.
