Alexander III of Russia

1845–1894, Emperor of Russia, “the Peacemaker”

Alexander Alexandrovich Romanov was the second son of Tsar Alexander II and Empress Marie of Hesse-Darmstadt, born in the Anichkov Palace in St Petersburg. He was not raised as heir; the throne was intended for his elder brother Nicholas, and Alexander's education was that of a soldier. When Nicholas died of meningitis in Nice in 1865, he inherited both the title of tsarevich and Nicholas's Danish fiancée, Princess Dagmar, whom he married in 1866 as Maria Feodorovna and to whom he remained, unusually for a Romanov, faithful.

His father's assassination by the People's Will on 1 March 1881 confirmed the prejudices he had absorbed from his tutor, the procurator of the Holy Synod Konstantin Pobedonostsev. The draft constitution his father had signed that morning was torn up, the autocracy reaffirmed, and a wave of reaction began. Russification was pressed on the borderlands; the universities were placed under state control; Jews were confined more strictly to the Pale of Settlement and many were driven into emigration by the pogroms that followed his accession; the zemstvos were curbed and the “land captains” established over the peasants.

Yet his reign was almost wholly peaceful abroad. He kept Russia out of war for thirteen years, earning the epithet Mirotvorets (Peacemaker). Bismarck's Three Emperors' League collapsed under his reign, and in its place Alexander turned, against family sentiment, to a Franco-Russian alliance signed by his ministers in 1891 and 1892. The Trans-Siberian Railway was begun in 1891. He patronized Russian arts, granted Pyotr Tchaikovsky a lifetime pension, and tolerated, if without enthusiasm, the late writing of Leo Tolstoy.

A giant of a man — he was said to bend horseshoes for amusement — he held up the collapsed roof of a derailed imperial train at Borki in 1888 long enough for his family to escape, and never quite recovered. Nephritis killed him at the Crimean palace of Livadia in November 1894, aged forty-nine. His eldest son, the diffident Nicholas II, succeeded him.
