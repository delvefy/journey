Decius

Roman emperor, r. 249–251

Gaius Messius Quintus Decius was born about 201 in the Danube province of Pannonia and rose as a senator and soldier. Sent by Philip the Arab to restore order among the troubled Danube legions, he was proclaimed emperor by them and, marching into Italy, defeated and killed Philip near Verona in 249. On taking power he attached to his name that of the revered Trajan, styling himself Traianus Decius, as if to summon back the empire’s golden age.

His brief reign is remembered above all for religion. In 250 he issued an edict requiring every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a certificate proving it — the first systematic, empire-wide persecution of Christians. Many were martyred, the bishop of Rome among them; many more “lapsed” and sacrificed, leaving a wound in the Church that men like Cyprian would struggle to heal, while the aged scholar Origen was imprisoned and tortured and never fully recovered.

The persecution outlived its author only briefly. The Goths under their king Cniva poured across the Danube, and in 251, at the marshy field of Abritus in Moesia, Decius and his son were trapped and slaughtered with their army — the first Roman emperor ever to fall in battle against a foreign foe. The catastrophe deepened the crisis that was tearing the third-century empire apart.
