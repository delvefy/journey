Sophocles

Athenian tragedian, c. 497–406 BCE

Sophocles was the most honoured of the three great tragic poets of classical Athens. Born around 497 BCE at Colonus, just outside the city, he grew up in the proud, confident Athens of the Persian Wars and lived through almost the whole of its golden age. As a youth, by tradition, he was chosen to lead the boys' chorus that celebrated the victory at Salamis.

He entered the dramatic competitions and in 468 won first prize, defeating the veteran Aeschylus himself. Over a career of sixty years he wrote some hundred and twenty plays and won around twenty victories, never finishing last. He enlarged the art of tragedy, adding a third actor, increasing the chorus, and introducing painted scenery, and he turned the drama toward the searching portrayal of individual character.

Of his many plays only seven survive complete, but they are among the masterpieces of world literature—Antigone, with its clash of conscience and law; Electra; Ajax; and above all Oedipus the King, which Aristotle would praise as the perfect model of tragic construction, the relentless unfolding of a man's discovery of his own terrible fate.

Sophocles was also a respected public man—a treasurer of the imperial funds, a general alongside Pericles in the war against Samos, and a magistrate in Athens's darkest hours. Serene and beloved, he died in 406 BCE at about ninety, just before his city's final defeat, and the comic poets pictured him passing contentedly into the underworld. His vision of human greatness and suffering has never lost its power.
