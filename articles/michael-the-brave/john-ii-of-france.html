John II of France

King of France, "le Bon", 1319–1364

John was born on 26 April 1319, the eldest son of Philip VI of France and Joan of Burgundy. At thirteen he was created Duke of Normandy and married Bonne of Luxembourg, sister of the future Emperor Charles IV. The marriage gave him an early circle of Bohemian and German connections; eleven children followed, including the future Charles V, Louis of Anjou, John of Berry, and Philip the Bold of Burgundy, who would each found a great cadet line. Bonne died of plague in 1349. Within a year his father died and he was crowned at Reims on 26 September 1350.

The reign opened in disaster. The Black Death had carried off perhaps a third of his subjects, the realm was in chronic monetary crisis from devaluations of the currency, and the Anglo-French war that his father had lost on the field of Crécy had only paused. In 1351 he founded the Order of the Star to give his nobility a chivalric body to rival Edward III's Garter; many of its knights were killed at Mauron the following year. Charles the Bad of Navarre, his son-in-law and cousin, intrigued and rebelled; the constable Charles de la Cerda was murdered on his orders in 1354. On 19 September 1356 he met the Black Prince at Maupertuis outside Poitiers and fought there in the front line, surrounded, until forced to surrender — the second French king to be taken prisoner in two generations.

Brought to England with his youngest son Philip — who at the battle had stood beside him crying out warnings of blows from left and right and so earned the title Philip the Bold — he spent four years as a royal guest in London. The Treaty of Brétigny of 1360 fixed his ransom at three million gold écus and an enlarged Aquitaine. He returned to France in October 1360, leaving his other sons and great nobles as hostages. When the Duke of Anjou broke his parole and fled in 1363, John, against the advice of his council, returned to England to honour the bond. He died at the Savoy Palace in London on 8 April 1364, and was buried with royal solemnity at Saint-Denis.
