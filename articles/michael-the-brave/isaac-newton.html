Isaac Newton

English physicist and mathematician, 1643–1727

Newton was born to a farming family in Lincolnshire and rose to become Lucasian Professor at Cambridge. When the university closed during the plague years he retreated to the country, and in that period of solitude he later said he laid the foundations of his work on calculus, optics, and gravitation. Reserved, secretive, and quick to take offence, he poured his energy into mathematics, experiment, and private studies of alchemy and theology.

His masterpiece, the Principia Mathematica of 1687, prompted and financed by his friend Edmond Halley, set out the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, showing that the same force that pulls an apple to the ground holds the planets in their orbits. From these principles he derived the laws of planetary motion that Kepler had found empirically, uniting the heavens and the earth under a single mathematical framework and overturning the vortices of Cartesian physics.

In optics he showed with prisms that white light is a mixture of colours, built the first reflecting telescope, and argued that light is made of tiny particles, a view he defended against Huygens’s waves. Independently of Leibniz he developed the calculus, and the bitter dispute over priority between them divided European mathematics for decades. In later life he served as Master of the Mint, reforming the coinage and pursuing counterfeiters, and presided over the Royal Society. By the time of his death he was revered as the model of the scientific genius, and his physics governed the understanding of nature for more than two centuries.
