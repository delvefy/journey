Galileo Galilei

Italian astronomer and physicist, 1564–1642

Galileo was born in Pisa in 1564, the son of the lutenist and music theorist Vincenzo Galilei. He entered the University of Pisa as a medical student, abandoned medicine for mathematics, and in 1589 returned there as a young professor. Three years later he moved to the chair of mathematics at Padua, where he spent eighteen of his most productive years. From experiments with rolling balls on inclined planes he drew the law of falling bodies; from a pendulum’s swing, the principle of its isochronism.

In the summer of 1609 he heard of a Dutch spyglass, ground better lenses, and turned the new instrument on the sky. Within months he had seen the mountains of the Moon, the four large satellites of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, and the resolution of the Milky Way into stars. Sidereus Nuncius, published in 1610 and dedicated to Cosimo II de’ Medici, made him a celebrity and bought him the post of court mathematician in Florence. Each observation told against the Ptolemaic system; the phases of Venus in particular could not be reconciled with an Earth-centred cosmos.

In 1616 the Roman Inquisition, advised by Cardinal Bellarmine, declared the motion of the Earth formally heretical. For sixteen years Galileo trod carefully. Encouraged by the election of his old admirer Maffeo Barberini as Pope Urban VIII, he wrote the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, published in 1632. The book’s thin pretence of neutrality fooled no one in Rome. He was summoned, tried, forced to recant, and condemned to house arrest at his villa in Arcetri. There, blind in his last years, he completed the Two New Sciences of 1638, the foundation of modern mechanics. He died in January 1642.
