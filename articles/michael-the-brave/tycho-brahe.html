Tycho Brahe

Danish astronomer, 1546–1601

Tycho Brahe was a Danish nobleman who gave his life to the stars at a time when the heavens were still mapped by the naked eye. As a young man he lost part of his nose in a duel and wore a metal prosthesis ever after, one of many flamboyant details in a famously larger-than-life career. In 1572 he observed a brilliant new star—what we now call a supernova—and in 1577 a great comet; both, he showed, lay far beyond the moon, shattering the old belief that the celestial realm was changeless and that comets were mere events in the air.

King Frederick II of Denmark, recognizing his value, granted him the island of Hven and the means to build Uraniborg, a splendid observatory and research institution. There, over two decades, Tycho and his assistants compiled astronomical measurements of unprecedented accuracy, the finest ever made without a telescope. He proposed his own compromise system, in which the planets circle the sun while the sun circles a stationary Earth—an attempt to keep the advantages of Copernicus without setting the Earth in motion.

Falling out with the Danish crown, he left for Prague to become imperial mathematician to the Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II. There he took on a brilliant young assistant, Johannes Kepler, and on Tycho’s death in 1601 his priceless store of observations passed to him. From that trove of data Kepler would derive the laws of planetary motion—so that Tycho’s patient measurements became the foundation of the new astronomy.

The road continues to Frederick II of Denmark.
