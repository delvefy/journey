Antonio Vivaldi

Venetian composer and violinist, 1678–1741

Antonio Vivaldi was the Venetian master whose driving rhythms and brilliant solo writing did more than any other to fix the form of the Baroque concerto. Born in Venice in 1678, the son of a barber who was also a professional violinist at St Mark's, he was trained by his father and ordained a priest in 1703. His red hair earned him the nickname il Prete Rosso, the Red Priest; chronic ill health soon excused him from saying Mass, and music became his whole vocation.

For much of his career he served as violin master at the Ospedale della Pietà, a Venetian foundling home famous for the musical accomplishments of its girls. For their orchestra he poured out concertos by the hundred, experimenting tirelessly with the three-movement fast–slow–fast design and the alternation of tutti and soloist. From this torrent came the four violin concertos known as The Four Seasons, among the most recognizable music ever written, each prefaced by a descriptive sonnet.

He was also a busy man of the theatre, composing and staging operas across Italy and boasting, with some exaggeration, of having written ninety-four of them. His sacred works, above all the radiant Gloria, show the same energy turned to devotion.

His fame faded in his own lifetime. Hoping for imperial patronage, he travelled to Vienna, only for his protector the emperor to die; Vivaldi himself died there in 1741 and was buried in a pauper's grave. Forgotten for nearly two centuries, his music was rediscovered in the twentieth and now stands at the centre of the repertoire. He had built his art on the foundations laid by an older Roman master of the violin, Arcangelo Corelli.
