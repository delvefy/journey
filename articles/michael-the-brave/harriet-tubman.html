Harriet Tubman

American abolitionist and Underground Railroad conductor, c.1822–1913

Harriet Tubman was born into slavery as Araminta Ross about 1822 in Dorchester County, on Maryland’s Eastern Shore. Hired out and beaten from childhood, she suffered a near-fatal head injury as a teenager when an overseer hurled an iron weight meant for another, leaving her with lifelong seizures and vivid visions she understood as messages from God. In 1849, fearing she would be sold south, she fled alone to Philadelphia and freedom.

She would not keep that freedom to herself. Returning to Maryland again and again over the following decade, she guided some seventy enslaved people — family, friends and strangers — northward along the secret network of the Underground Railroad, travelling by night and outwitting patrols and slave-catchers. She boasted that she never lost a passenger, and admirers called her Moses. So bold were her rescues that she joined the abolitionist John Brown in planning his raid on Harpers Ferry, recruiting supporters for a cause she revered.

When the Civil War came she served the Union army in South Carolina as cook, nurse, scout and spy, and in June 1863 guided the Combahee River raid, which freed more than seven hundred enslaved people — the first woman to lead an armed expedition in the war. Afterward she settled in Auburn, New York, on land bought from her friend William H. Seward, where she cared for aged and indigent freedpeople, founded a home for them, and lent her voice to the campaign for women’s suffrage. She died there on 10 March 1913, honoured as one of the great liberators of her age.
