Xenophon

Athenian soldier and historian, c. 430–354 BCE

Xenophon was an Athenian soldier, historian, and writer, a pupil of Socrates whose varied life and clear, graceful prose made him one of the most widely read authors of antiquity. Born around 430 BCE, he grew up amid the Peloponnesian War and fell under the spell of Socrates, whose conversation he would later set down in writing.

In 401 BCE, against Socrates' advice, he joined the army of Greek mercenaries that the Persian prince Cyrus the Younger led against his brother, the Great King. When Cyrus was killed in the moment of victory at the battle of Cunaxa and the Greek generals were treacherously seized, Xenophon helped rally the leaderless Ten Thousand and guide them on their long fighting retreat from the heart of Mesopotamia to the Black Sea—an epic he immortalized in the Anabasis.

Afterward he served alongside the Spartans and conceived a warm admiration for their king Agesilaus II; for taking the Spartan side he was exiled from Athens and settled on an estate in the Peloponnese, where he wrote. His Hellenica continued Greek history from the point at which Thucydides broke off, while his Cyropaedia offered an idealized portrait of Cyrus the Great as the model ruler.

He also composed the Memorabilia and kindred works defending the memory of Socrates against the charges that had condemned him. Practical and observant rather than profound, Xenophon left writings on history, philosophy, horsemanship, household management, and the hunt that shaped the image of classical Greece for later ages. He died around 354 BCE.
