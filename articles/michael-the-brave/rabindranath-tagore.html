Rabindranath Tagore

1861–1941, Bengali polymath, the first non-European Nobel laureate

Rabindranath Thakur was born in the great Jorasanko mansion of north Calcutta, the youngest of the surviving sons of Debendranath Tagore, leader of the reform-Hindu Brahmo Samaj, and grandson of the merchant Dwarkanath. He was tutored at home in a household where literature, music, philosophy and the natural sciences were daily breath. A teenage trip to England in 1878 to read law ended in his return without a degree; he had decided to be a poet. He published his first verse before twenty and a play, Valmiki Pratibha, that he composed and acted in himself.

From 1890 his father gave him management of the family estates at Shilaidaha and Shahzadpur in East Bengal, and the years spent on the houseboat among the peasants of the Padma deepened both his vernacular Bengali and his social sympathies. In 1901 he founded an experimental school at Santiniketan in rural Bengal, the seed of what in 1921 became the international university Visva-Bharati, conceived as a meeting place of east and west. The English translation of Gitanjali, the volume of devotional lyrics that he himself rendered into prose-poems and that W. B. Yeats introduced in London in 1912, brought him the Nobel Prize in Literature the following year, the first ever awarded to an Asian.

He renounced his knighthood in 1919 after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and, while he disagreed with Gandhi about non-cooperation and the place of the West, the two remained lifelong friends. He travelled the continents indefatigably — Europe, the United States, Japan, China, the Soviet Union, Argentina — and met Albert Einstein in 1930 for a famous public dialogue on reality and truth. He wrote two thousand songs (the Rabindra Sangeet), painted in old age in a haunting expressionist style, and saw two of his lyrics adopted as the national anthems of India and Bangladesh. He died at Jorasanko on 7 August 1941, the morning the city stopped to mourn its Gurudev.
