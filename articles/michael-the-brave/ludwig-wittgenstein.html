Ludwig Wittgenstein

Austrian-British philosopher, 1889–1951

Wittgenstein was born in Vienna into one of the richest families of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a household of immense culture and recurring tragedy. He trained first as an engineer, studying aeronautics in Manchester, but problems in the foundations of mathematics drew him to Cambridge, where he became the pupil and then the equal of Bertrand Russell. Russell judged him a genius almost at once.

While serving in the Austrian army in the First World War he completed the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921), a spare sequence of numbered propositions arguing that language pictures the world, that the limits of language are the limits of thought, and that the most important things, ethics and the mystical, lie beyond what can be said: "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." Believing he had solved the problems of philosophy, he gave away his inherited fortune and withdrew to teach in remote Austrian villages.

In 1929 he returned to Cambridge, his arrival celebrated by John Maynard Keynes, and began to dismantle his own earlier system. His later thought, gathered posthumously in the Philosophical Investigations (1953), abandoned the picture theory for the idea that meaning is use, that language is a web of practical "language-games," and that many philosophical puzzles are confusions bred by language itself. Few thinkers have founded two distinct schools; Wittgenstein shaped both logical positivism and ordinary-language philosophy, and remains among the most studied philosophers of the modern age.
