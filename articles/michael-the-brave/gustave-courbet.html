Gustave Courbet

Leader of French Realism, 1819–1877

Gustave Courbet was the painter who founded the Realist movement and broke the grip of academic idealism on European art. Born in 1819 at Ornans in rural eastern France, he turned his back on the polished myths and histories the Salon prized and resolved to paint only what he could actually see—the lives, labours, and faces of ordinary people.

He did so on a heroic scale. A Burial at Ornans rendered a provincial funeral on a canvas the size once reserved for kings and saints, and The Stone Breakers laid bare the grind of poverty. When the great exhibition of 1855 spurned him, he answered by building his own Pavilion of Realism and posting a defiant manifesto. His vast allegory The Painter's Studio gathered his allies about him, the poet Baudelaire and the theorist Proudhon among them.

A staunch republican, he refused the Legion of Honour from the hand of Napoleon III. When the Paris Commune rose in 1871 he led its commission on the arts and was blamed for pulling down the Vendôme Column, a monument to Napoleonic glory. After the Commune fell he was jailed and then ruined by an order to pay for the column's rebuilding, and he fled into exile in Switzerland, where he died in 1877.

His example—that the everyday world was worthy of great art—opened the road to Manet and the Impressionists. Among the fellow Realists of his generation, the supreme painter of the peasant and the soil was Jean-François Millet.
