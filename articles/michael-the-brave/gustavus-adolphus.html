Gustavus Adolphus

King of Sweden, the “Lion of the North,” 1594–1632

Gustavus Adolphus inherited the Swedish throne in 1611 as a youth of sixteen, with his country at war on several fronts, and turned it within two decades into one of the great powers of Europe. Working with his able chancellor Axel Oxenstierna, he reformed the state, its finances, and above all its army. He is often called the father of modern warfare for his innovations: lighter, more mobile artillery, disciplined infantry combining musket and pike in shallow lines, the close coordination of foot, horse, and guns, and a national army of trained, regularly paid soldiers.

With this instrument he prevailed in hard wars against Denmark, Russia, and Poland, securing Sweden’s dominance around the Baltic. Then, in 1630, he intervened decisively in the Thirty Years’ War, landing in Germany to defend the Protestant cause and check the power of the Holy Roman Emperor. Subsidized by Cardinal Richelieu’s France, which sought to weaken the Habsburgs, he swept south and at the Battle of Breitenfeld in 1631 won a crushing victory over the imperial army under Tilly, transforming the course of the war.

His triumph was brief. In November 1632, at the Battle of Lützen against the imperial general Wallenstein, Gustavus led a cavalry charge into the fog and was cut down and killed, though his army held the field. His death robbed the Protestant cause of its champion, but he had made Sweden a great power and left the throne to his young daughter, Christina.

The road continues to Christina of Sweden.
