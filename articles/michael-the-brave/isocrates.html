Isocrates

Athenian rhetorician and teacher, 436–338 BCE

Isocrates was among the most influential teachers of the ancient world, though he seldom spoke in public himself. Of a frail voice and retiring temperament, he studied under the great Sophist Gorgias and absorbed the example of Socrates, then turned from the law courts to the written word and the classroom.

Around 390 BCE he opened a school of rhetoric in Athens that became the most celebrated of its age, a rival to Plato's Academy. There he trained a generation of statesmen, orators, and historians, teaching not narrow trickery but a broad cultivation of eloquence, judgement, and civic virtue—an ideal of education through the study of language and literature that would echo through the humanism of later ages.

He used his polished written discourses to shape opinion across the Greek world. In the Panegyricus and other works he preached the cause of pan-Hellenic unity, urging the quarrelsome city-states to lay aside their wars and join together against the Persian Empire. Despairing that Athens or Sparta would lead such a crusade, he at last appealed to Philip II of Macedon to unite the Greeks for the great enterprise.

He lived to the great age of ninety-eight. According to tradition, when news came of Philip's crushing victory at Chaeronea in 338—the end of Greek liberty, though also the unity he had longed for—the old man, in grief, starved himself to death. His prose style and his vision of a liberal education left a mark on Western culture lasting far beyond his lifetime.

The road continues to Gorgias.
