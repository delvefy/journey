Ambrose

Bishop of Milan and Doctor of the Church, c. 339–397 AD

Ambrose was born at Trier into a great Roman Christian family and trained for public life, rising to govern the province around Milan. In 374, when the city's bishopric fell vacant and Arians and orthodox Christians clashed over the succession, the governor went to keep the peace—and the crowd, to his astonishment, acclaimed him bishop, though he was not yet even baptised. Within a week he was baptised, ordained, and consecrated, and he gave away his wealth to the poor and the Church.

He became the most forceful churchman of his age. A staunch defender of Nicene orthodoxy, he faced down the Arian court of the empress Justina, refusing to surrender a basilica and holding it with his congregation in prayer. He advised a succession of western emperors and resisted the pagan senator Symmachus's plea to restore the Altar of Victory. Above all, he asserted that the emperor stood within the Church, not above it: after Theodosius ordered a massacre at Thessalonica, Ambrose barred him from communion until he performed public penance—a stunning demonstration of spiritual authority over the throne.

Ambrose was also a great pastor and writer, who brought congregational hymn-singing and chant into the Western liturgy and whose preaching helped draw the young Augustine toward conversion; it was Ambrose who baptised him. Counted among the four original Doctors of the Latin Church, he died at Milan in 397, having done as much as any man to shape the relationship of Church and state in the Christian West.
