Elizabeth I of England

Queen of England, 1533–1603

Elizabeth Tudor was born in September 1533 at Greenwich, the only surviving child of Henry VIII by his second queen, Anne Boleyn. Anne’s execution and the annulment of the marriage three years later left the princess illegitimate, demoted, and lucky to live. Educated by Roger Ascham and a series of Cambridge humanists, she grew into one of the best-read women of her age, fluent in Latin, French and Italian. Under her Catholic half-sister Mary I she was briefly imprisoned in the Tower on suspicion of complicity in Wyatt’s rebellion; on Mary’s death in November 1558 she succeeded almost without opposition.

The first acts of her reign defined it. The Religious Settlement of 1559 restored royal supremacy over the Church, prescribed a reformed Book of Common Prayer, and steered a careful middle course between Geneva and Rome that survived, with strain, for the next forty years. Refusing all proposals of marriage, including those of her former brother-in-law Philip II of Spain, she made her unmarried state a political instrument and held her ministers—chiefly William Cecil, Lord Burghley, and his son Robert—to her purposes for decade after decade.

Two long contests shaped her middle years. Mary, Queen of Scots, deposed and fled to England in 1568, became a focus for Catholic plots; after years of confinement Elizabeth reluctantly signed her death warrant in 1587. The following summer, in retaliation and in long-meditated ambition, Philip II launched the Armada against England. Its defeat in the Channel and its scattering in the autumn storms became the founding myth of English sea-power. Privateers like Francis Drake, soldiers like the Earl of Essex, and an unprecedented flowering of poetry and drama under William Shakespeare and his contemporaries gave the late Elizabethan decade its glamour. The queen herself died at Richmond in March 1603, the last of the Tudors, and her crown passed peacefully to her cousin James VI of Scotland.
