Empedocles

Presocratic philosopher of Acragas, c. 494–434 BCE

Empedocles was a philosopher, poet, and reputed wonder-worker of Acragas, one of the great Greek cities of Sicily. Born into a wealthy aristocratic family around 494 BCE, he was also a champion of its democracy, and tradition holds that he refused the offer of kingship. He set out his teaching not in prose but in two great hexameter poems, On Nature and Purifications.

His most influential idea was that all things are made from four eternal “roots”—earth, water, air, and fire—which mingle and separate under the sway of two opposing cosmic powers, Love, which draws them together, and Strife, which forces them apart, in an endless alternating cycle. This doctrine of the four elements, refined and disputed for two thousand years, became a foundation of ancient science. He answered Parmenides's denial of change by holding the elements themselves unchanging while letting their mixtures come to be and pass away.

He was also a religious teacher in the tradition of Pythagoras, preaching the transmigration of souls and purification through abstinence from bloodshed and meat. He claimed near-divine powers, boasting that he could heal the sick and even call back the dead, and crowds followed him as a kind of god on earth.

His death became legend: it was said that, to prove he had become a god, he leapt into the fiery crater of Mount Etna, which betrayed him by casting out one of his bronze sandals. Whatever the truth, his ideas, transmitted and criticised by Aristotle, shaped the course of natural philosophy for millennia.
