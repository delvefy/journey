Jacques-Louis David

French Neoclassical painter, 1748–1825

Jacques-Louis David was born in Paris in 1748 and trained at the Académie Royale under Joseph-Marie Vien. After four attempts he won the Prix de Rome in 1774 and spent five years in Italy, where he turned away from the Rococo of Boucher and Fragonard towards a stark new manner drawn from Roman antiquity and from Poussin. His Oath of the Horatii of 1784 and Death of Socrates of 1787, exhibited at the Salon, made him at a stroke the leading painter in France and the visual standard-bearer of moral seriousness.

When revolution came he plunged into politics. He sat in the Convention, voted for the execution of Louis XVI, served on the Committee of General Security, and was Maximilien Robespierre's intimate friend and propagandist. His Death of Marat of 1793, painted within weeks of Marat's murder in the bath, became the great icon of the Republic. He drew Marie Antoinette in profile as she rode in the tumbril to the guillotine. Thermidor caught him with his patron; he was twice arrested and narrowly escaped Robespierre's fate.

Released and rehabilitated, he made his peace with the new regime and then with Napoleon, whose coronation he commemorated in a vast canvas and whom he portrayed crossing the Alps on a rearing horse and in his Tuileries study. He was Premier Peintre to the Emperor and ran the most influential teaching studio in Europe, training Antoine-Jean Gros, Anne-Louis Girodet, François Gérard and many others.

After Waterloo he was exiled with the regicides and finished his life in Brussels, painting mythological scenes until his death in 1825. His Roman manner and his draughtsmanship dominated European art for a generation, and the pupil who carried his classicism furthest into the new century, refining it into a private cult of line, was Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres.
