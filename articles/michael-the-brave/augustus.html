Augustus

First Roman emperor, 63 BC – AD 14

Gaius Octavius was born in 63 BC, the grandnephew of Julius Caesar, and was only eighteen when Caesar’s murder transformed his life: the dictator’s will had adopted him as son and heir. The boy plunged into the chaos of the dying Republic with astonishing nerve, courted at first by the Senate and the orator Cicero as a counterweight to Mark Antony, then changing course to join Antony and Lepidus in the Second Triumvirate. Together they avenged Caesar, destroying Brutus and Cassius at Philippi, while their bloody proscriptions claimed thousands, Cicero among them.

The partners soon divided the Roman world and then turned on one another. The struggle ended at the naval battle of Actium in 31 BC, where Octavian’s fleet broke that of Antony and Cleopatra; within a year both were dead and Egypt was his. At thirty-three he stood, like the conqueror he was descended in spirit from, master of the entire Mediterranean—but where Caesar had flaunted his dominance, Octavian veiled it.

In 27 BC he handed his powers back to the Senate, which returned them under a new name and gave him the title Augustus. He ruled as princeps, first citizen, preserving the forms of the Republic while holding its substance, and so founded the empire that would endure for centuries. His long reign brought the Pax Romana: settled frontiers, reformed administration, and a flowering of letters under his friend Maecenas that produced Virgil’s Aeneid. He boasted that he had found Rome a city of brick and left it one of marble, and a grateful posterity named a month for him. When he died in AD 14, power passed smoothly to his stepson Tiberius, and the age of emperors was firmly begun.
