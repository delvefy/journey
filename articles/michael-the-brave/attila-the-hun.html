Attila the Hun

King of the Huns, c.406–453

Attila came to power over the Huns about 434, ruling at first beside his brother Bleda, whom he later killed to reign alone. From their grasslands beyond the Danube the Huns had built a steppe empire that drew tribute from settled peoples and terror from the Roman world. Attila pressed the Eastern emperor Theodosius II for ever larger payments of gold, ravaging the Balkan provinces when they were withheld, until the very name of the Huns became a byword for devastation and he was remembered by Christians as the Scourge of God.

In 450 a strange opportunity arose when Honoria, sister of the Western emperor, sent Attila her ring in appeal against an unwanted marriage; he chose to read it as a betrothal and demanded half the Western Empire as her dowry. Rebuffed, he led a vast host into Gaul in 451, only to be checked at the great Battle of the Catalaunian Plains by a coalition of Romans under the general Aetius and Visigoths under their king, the first serious reverse of his career.

The next year he poured into Italy, storming Aquileia and spreading panic toward Rome, but withdrew after a celebrated meeting with Pope Leo I near the river Po — whether moved by the pontiff, by plague and famine in his army, or by news of Roman counterattack, no one can say. In 453 Attila died suddenly on the night of a new marriage, and his sons quarrelled away the empire he had forged. Within a year the Hunnic power that had shaken two empires was broken forever.
