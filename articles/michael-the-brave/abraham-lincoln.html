Abraham Lincoln

16th President of the United States, 1809–1865

Born in a Kentucky log cabin and almost entirely self-educated, Lincoln split rails, kept store, and taught himself law before rising in Illinois politics. His series of debates with Stephen Douglas in 1858, centred on the morality and the spread of slavery, made him a national figure, and in 1860 he was elected the first Republican president. His victory prompted the slave states of the South to secede, and within weeks the Civil War had begun.

Determined above all to preserve the Union, Lincoln steered the North through four years of the bloodiest war in American history with a rare blend of patience, political cunning, and moral clarity. In 1863 the Emancipation Proclamation turned the struggle into a war against slavery itself, and at the dedication of a battlefield cemetery he distilled the nation’s purpose into the few immortal sentences of the Gettysburg Address.

He found at last in Ulysses S. Grant a general who would fight, and under Grant’s relentless campaigns the Confederacy collapsed in the spring of 1865. Lincoln had just secured the constitutional abolition of slavery and begun to plan a generous reconstruction when, days after the surrender, he was shot at Ford’s Theatre by the actor John Wilkes Booth and died the next morning. Mourned as the savior of the Union and the Great Emancipator, he left the war to be finished and remembered by his commanding general, Ulysses S. Grant.
