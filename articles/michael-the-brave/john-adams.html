John Adams

Second President of the United States, 1735–1826

A Massachusetts lawyer of fierce principle, Adams first won notice by defending the British soldiers accused in the Boston Massacre, insisting that even the unpopular deserved a fair trial. Yet he became one of the most determined advocates of American independence. In the Continental Congress he pressed the case relentlessly, proposed George Washington as commander of the army, and urged the young Thomas Jefferson to compose the Declaration of Independence, which Adams then defended on the floor against all doubters.

During the war he served abroad, joining Benjamin Franklin in France, securing loans and recognition from the Dutch Republic, and helping negotiate the 1783 Treaty of Paris that confirmed American independence. He became the first United States minister to Britain, formally received by George III, the monarch against whom he had rebelled.

Returning home, Adams served two terms as Washington’s vice president before winning the presidency in 1796. His single term was consumed by crisis with revolutionary France, which he steered toward an honourable peace despite pressure for war; the controversial Alien and Sedition Acts of his administration, however, damaged him. Divisions among the Federalists helped cost him reelection to Jefferson in 1800. In retirement he carried on a remarkable late correspondence with his old rival Jefferson, and by a famous coincidence the two men died on the same day, the fiftieth anniversary of independence.
