Ludovico Sforza

Duke of Milan, “il Moro”, 1452–1508

Ludovico Maria Sforza, born in 1452, was a younger son of Francesco Sforza, the condottiero who had seized the duchy of Milan. Nicknamed “il Moro” for his dark complexion, he took control of the state as regent for his young nephew Gian Galeazzo and ruled in fact long before he ruled in name, securing the ducal title for himself in 1494. His court at Milan and at the castle of Pavia was among the most brilliant in Europe: he employed Donato Bramante on his churches and kept Leonardo da Vinci for nearly two decades, commissioning the Last Supper and the colossal bronze horse that was never cast.

His marriage in 1491 to Beatrice d’Este tied him to the cultured house of Ferrara. To shore up his contested legitimacy he married his niece to the Emperor Maximilian I in 1493, buying imperial investiture with an enormous dowry. But his fatal stroke was diplomatic: fearing the kingdom of Naples, in 1494 he encouraged Charles VIII of France to march into Italy and press the Angevin claim. The invasion succeeded too well, shattered the peninsula’s fragile balance, and opened the long Italian Wars.

The weapon turned in his hand. Louis XII of France, heir to a Visconti claim on Milan itself, invaded in 1499 and drove him out. Ludovico briefly recovered the city with Swiss mercenaries, but at Novara in 1500 those troops refused to fight their countrymen in French pay, and he was taken prisoner. He spent his last eight years confined in France and died in the château of Loches in 1508, the architect of a catastrophe he had not foreseen.
