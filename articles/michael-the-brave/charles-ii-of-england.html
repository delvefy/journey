Charles II of England

King of England, Scotland, and Ireland, 1630–1685

The eldest surviving son of Charles I, the young prince came of age amid civil war. When Parliament’s victory ended in his father’s execution in 1649, Charles was proclaimed king in Scotland but crushed by Oliver Cromwell’s army at Worcester in 1651. His escape became legend—hiding in an oak tree and slipping in disguise through a hostile country to the coast—and began nine years of penniless exile in France and the Low Countries.

Cromwell’s death and the collapse of the Commonwealth opened the way for the Restoration of 1660, when Charles returned to general rejoicing. Witty, easygoing, and fond of pleasure, the “Merry Monarch” revived the theatre, kept a glittering and scandalous court, and took a genuine interest in the new science, granting the royal charter to the Royal Society. His reign weathered the Great Plague, the Great Fire of London—after which Christopher Wren rebuilt the city—and fierce political storms over his Catholic brother’s succession.

Beneath the affable surface he was a shrewd and secretive politician. In the clandestine Treaty of Dover he took subsidies from his cousin Louis XIV of France in return for promises that edged England toward Catholicism. Leaving many illegitimate children but no lawful heir, he was received into the Catholic Church on his deathbed and succeeded by his brother, James II.

The road continues to Louis XIV.
