Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

German idealist philosopher, 1770–1831

Hegel was born in Stuttgart and educated at the Protestant seminary in Tübingen, where his fellow students included the future philosopher Schelling and the poet Hölderlin. He came of age amid the upheavals of the French Revolution, which he greeted with enthusiasm, and he spent his early career as a private tutor, newspaper editor, and headmaster before securing a university chair. The collapse and remaking of Europe under Napoleon convinced him that history itself was rational, the working-out of an idea across time.

His first major work, the Phenomenology of Spirit (1807), traced the journey of consciousness toward self-knowledge and was famously finished as French troops occupied Jena. In the Science of Logic, the Encyclopedia of the Philosophical Sciences, and the Philosophy of Right he built one of the most ambitious systems in the history of thought, absolute idealism, in which reality unfolds as Spirit (Geist) coming to comprehend itself through a dialectical movement of contradiction and resolution. History, he argued, was the progress of the consciousness of freedom.

From 1818 he held the chair of philosophy in Berlin and dominated German intellectual life. After his sudden death in a cholera epidemic, his followers split into conservative "Right" and radical "Left" Hegelians; the latter, above all the young Karl Marx, inverted his dialectic into a materialist account of class struggle. Difficult, comprehensive, and endlessly contested, Hegel shaped Marxism, existentialism, and much of modern philosophy of history.

The road continues to Arthur Schopenhauer.
