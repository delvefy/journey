Rudolf Carnap

German philosopher of logic and science, 1891–1970

Rudolf Carnap was born in 1891 at Ronsdorf, near Wuppertal in the German Rhineland, the son of a ribbon manufacturer of strict Reformed piety. He studied physics and philosophy at Jena, where he was decisive among Gottlob Frege's tiny audience and where he read Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica with a convert's fervour. After service in the First World War he completed a doctorate at Jena, took his Habilitation at Vienna in 1926, and joined Moritz Schlick's seminar at the city's university—the body of philosophers, mathematicians, and scientists that the world would soon know as the Vienna Circle.

His Logical Construction of the World of 1928 attempted to derive the whole of empirical knowledge by logical construction from a basic stock of immediate experiences, in the spirit of Russell's external-world program. The Vienna Circle's 1929 manifesto, of which Carnap was the principal author, announced its program of "logical empiricism": philosophy as the logical analysis of scientific language, with the elimination of metaphysics as its negative goal.

His Logical Syntax of Language of 1934 went further, treating philosophy itself as a branch of formal syntax, and gave Carnap's name to the position that meaningful statements are either analytic or empirically verifiable. He sparred publicly with Heidegger, whose "the Nothing nothings" he held up as a model of pure nonsense, and he debated through three decades with Karl Popper, whose criterion of falsifiability he revised but never adopted.

The Nazi seizure of Germany and Austria scattered the Vienna Circle. Carnap emigrated to the United States in 1935, taught at Chicago and then at UCLA, and turned his late work to the theory of inductive probability, of semantics, and of modal logic. He died at Santa Monica in 1970. His Vienna-trained sparring partner and the great enemy of inductive justification, who would reshape twentieth-century philosophy of science around the criterion of falsifiability, was Karl Popper.
