William Wilberforce

British abolitionist and politician, 1759–1833

Wilberforce was born in Hull to a wealthy merchant family and entered Parliament at twenty-one, where he became a close friend of the young William Pitt. A profound evangelical conversion in the mid-1780s transformed an easy-living young politician into a man of fierce moral purpose, and he resolved to devote his career to two great objects: the reformation of national morals and the abolition of the slave trade.

From 1787 he led the long parliamentary struggle against the trade in human beings, introducing bill after bill and laying before the Commons the damning evidence amassed by Thomas Clarkson and his allies. Urged on near the end of his life by the aged John Wesley and sustained by the circle of evangelical reformers known as the Clapham Sect, he saw his persistence rewarded when the Slave Trade Act of 1807 outlawed the traffic throughout the British Empire.

He spent the rest of his life pressing for the abolition of slavery itself. Worn out and in failing health, he lived just long enough to learn that the Slavery Abolition Act, freeing the enslaved across the Empire, had passed through Parliament; he died three days later, in 1833, and was buried in Westminster Abbey. He had also championed missionary work, prison reform, and the prevention of cruelty to animals, embodying the conscience of his age.
