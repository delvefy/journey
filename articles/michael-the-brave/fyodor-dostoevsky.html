Fyodor Dostoevsky

Russian novelist, 1821–1881

Dostoevsky was born in Moscow, the son of a doctor at a hospital for the poor, and his first novel, Poor Folk (1846), won immediate praise from the influential critic Vissarion Belinsky. But in 1849 his involvement with a circle of young radicals led to his arrest. Condemned to death, he was reprieved only at the last moment, as he stood before a firing squad, and sent instead to four years of hard labour in Siberia. The ordeal shattered and remade him, deepening the religious faith and fascination with suffering that would mark all his later work.

From the 1860s he produced the sequence of novels on which his fame rests: Notes from Underground, Crime and Punishment, The Idiot, Demons, and finally The Brothers Karamazov (1880). Written often in haste and under crushing debts worsened by a compulsion to gamble, and punctuated by epileptic seizures, they probe guilt, free will, faith and atheism, and the human capacity for both degradation and grace with unmatched psychological intensity.

A fervent Orthodox Christian and Russian patriot in his last years, Dostoevsky saw in his nation a spiritual mission to a Europe he believed was decaying into materialism. His 1880 speech in honour of the poet Pushkin moved its audience to ecstasy and sealed his standing as a national prophet. He died the following year. His exploration of the divided modern soul left a profound mark on Nietzsche, Freud, and the whole of existential thought.
