Alexander Severus

Last Severan emperor of Rome, 208–235 AD

Alexander Severus was a cousin of Elagabalus, raised to power by the same formidable grandmother, Julia Maesa, who had Elagabalus adopt him and then discarded the elder boy in his favour. When the Praetorians murdered Elagabalus in 222, the thirteen-year-old Alexander became emperor. Throughout his reign the real authority lay with his mother Julia Mamaea and a council of experienced senators and jurists, among them for a time the celebrated legal scholar Ulpian.

His government won praise for its moderation and good order after the excesses of his predecessor. He restored the dignity of the old Roman religion, yet was famously tolerant in matters of faith, reportedly keeping images of Christ and other holy figures in his private shrine; it was his mother who invited the Christian teacher Origen to expound the new religion. But the very virtues of the civil administration could not supply what the age most demanded—military command.

Abroad he faced grave dangers: a newly aggressive Persian empire under its first Sassanid king in the east, and restless Germanic peoples on the Rhine. When Alexander, guided by his mother, sought to buy peace from the Germans with subsidies rather than crush them in battle, the soldiers despised him as weak and dominated by a woman. In 235 the legions mutinied, proclaimed the rough soldier Maximinus, and cut down Alexander and Julia Mamaea together. His death ended the Severan dynasty and opened the long anarchy of the Crisis of the Third Century.

The road continues to Origen.
