Marcus Garvey

Jamaican Black nationalist and Pan-Africanist, 1887–1940

Marcus Mosiah Garvey was born on 17 August 1887 at St Ann’s Bay, on the north coast of Jamaica, the youngest surviving child of a stonemason. Apprenticed to a printer, he became a foreman and led an early printers’ strike, then travelled through Central America and on to London, where contact with African and West Indian intellectuals and the writings of Booker T. Washington sharpened his sense that people of African descent across the world shared a common cause. Returning to Jamaica in 1914 he founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association, dedicated to racial pride, economic self-reliance and the redemption of Africa.

In 1916 he moved to the United States and rebuilt the UNIA in Harlem, where his oratory and his newspaper, the Negro World, drew a following that at its height claimed millions across the Americas, the Caribbean and Africa. He staged vast conventions, sold shares in the Black Star Line, a Black-owned shipping company meant to link the diaspora and carry settlers to Africa, and proclaimed himself provisional president of an Africa not yet free. No other Black movement of the age matched its scale.

The Black Star Line failed amid mismanagement, and in 1923 Garvey was convicted of mail fraud in connection with its stock; imprisoned in 1925, he had his sentence commuted and was deported to Jamaica in 1927. He spent his last years organizing in Kingston and then London, where he died in 1940, his movement scattered. Yet his insistence that Black people look to Africa outlived him: Rastafarians read his words as prophecy, and Pan-African leaders from Kwame Nkrumah to the founders of later Black movements claimed him as an ancestor. Jamaica named him its first National Hero.
