Scipio Africanus

Roman general who defeated Hannibal, 236–183 BC

Publius Cornelius Scipio rose to greatness amid the catastrophes of the Second Punic War. He survived the slaughter at Cannae as a young officer, and after his father and uncle were killed campaigning in Spain, he volunteered for the command there though barely of age. In 209 BC he stunned the Carthaginians by seizing their stronghold of New Carthage in a single bold assault, and within a few years he had driven them out of Spain altogether.

Elected consul, Scipio pressed—over the cautious objections of Fabius Maximus—to carry the war into Africa itself. Allying with the Numidian prince Masinissa, he forced Carthage to recall Hannibal from Italy, and in 202 BC the two greatest commanders of the age met at Zama. Scipio's victory ended the war, broke Carthaginian power, and won him the honorific "Africanus." He later accompanied his brother east to help defeat Antiochus III at Magnesia, extending Roman dominance over the Hellenistic world.

Yet glory bred envy. In his later years Scipio's political enemies, foremost among them the stern censor Cato the Elder, hounded him and his brother with charges of corruption in the so-called trials of the Scipios. Proud and embittered, the conqueror of Hannibal withdrew from public life to his country estate at Liternum, where he died around 183 BC, asking that his ungrateful city not have his bones. He is remembered as one of Rome's supreme generals, a man who never lost a battle.
