Ramesses II

Pharaoh of Egypt, r. c. 1279–1213 BC

Ramesses II, remembered as Ramesses the Great, was the third pharaoh of Egypt’s Nineteenth Dynasty and one of the longest-reigning and most celebrated kings of the ancient world, ruling for some sixty-six years from about 1279 BC. The son of Seti I, he inherited a resurgent empire and devoted his prodigious energies to defending and glorifying it. The Greeks knew him by a version of his royal name as Ozymandias.

His most famous campaign was against the Hittite empire at the Battle of Kadesh, about 1274 BC, an enormous clash of chariots that he proclaimed across Egypt as a personal triumph, though it ended in stalemate. Years later he sealed peace with the Hittite king Hattusili III in a treaty that survives in both Egyptian and Hittite versions — the earliest international peace accord known to history. At home he built on a scale never surpassed: the rock-cut temples of Abu Simbel with their colossal seated figures, the Ramesseum at Thebes, vast additions to Karnak and Luxor, and a new capital in the Delta.

He took many wives, chief among them his beloved Nefertari, and fathered something near a hundred children, outliving many of them before his son Merneptah succeeded him. Egyptian memory and later tradition made him the very image of pharaonic majesty — and Jewish tradition came to cast him as the Pharaoh against whom Moses contended. Long after, the toppled wreckage of one of his giant statues inspired Percy Bysshe Shelley’s sonnet on the vanity of earthly power, in which the boast “look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair” is mocked by the surrounding emptiness. Ramesses died about 1213 BC, nearly ninety years old.
