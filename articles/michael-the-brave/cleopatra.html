Cleopatra

Last pharaoh of Egypt, 69–30 BC

Cleopatra VII was born in 69 BC into the Ptolemaic dynasty, the Macedonian Greek house that had ruled Egypt since the death of Alexander. She was formidably educated and, almost alone among her line, troubled to learn the Egyptian tongue of her subjects. On her father’s death she shared the throne with her younger brother Ptolemy XIII, but the partnership soon collapsed into civil war, and she found herself driven from power.

Her recovery of it turned on Rome. When Julius Caesar arrived in Alexandria in 48 BC, she had herself carried secretly into his presence, won his support, and saw her brother destroyed; she became Caesar’s lover and bore a son she named for him. After Caesar’s murder she cast her lot with another Roman, Mark Antony, binding him to her in a partnership of love and policy that produced three children and a grand design to govern the eastern Mediterranean from Alexandria.

That design ran headlong into Caesar’s heir, Octavian, who turned Roman opinion against Antony as the slave of a foreign queen. The reckoning came at the naval battle of Actium in 31 BC, where their fleet was broken and they fled to Egypt. As Octavian advanced on Alexandria the following year, Antony took his own life, and Cleopatra, rather than be led in chains through Rome in a conqueror’s triumph, followed him in death—by tradition through the bite of an asp. With her ended three centuries of Ptolemaic rule and the long independence of Egypt, which now became a province of Rome. Yet no ancient queen has so gripped the imagination of the centuries since.
