Edward I of England

King of England, 1239–1307

Edward I, called Longshanks for his height and later the Hammer of the Scots, was born in 1239, the eldest son of Henry III and Eleanor of Provence. His early career was shaped by the baronial revolt led by Simon de Montfort: after a period of vacillation he became his father’s chief commander and crushed the rebels at the Battle of Evesham in 1265, where Montfort was killed. In 1270 he left England on the Ninth Crusade, reaching Acre and surviving an assassin’s poisoned blade. He was still abroad when Henry III died in 1272, and was crowned only on his return in 1274.

As king he was a relentless legislator. The Statutes of Westminster, Gloucester, Mortmain and Quo Warranto reshaped English land law and royal administration, and his summons of the Model Parliament in 1295 set a lasting precedent for representation. In 1290 he expelled the Jews from England, the first such national expulsion in Europe. Between 1277 and 1283 he conquered Gwynedd, ending the line of Llywelyn ap Gruffudd and ringing Wales with great stone castles—Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech, Beaumaris.

His most consuming struggle was with Scotland. Invited to arbitrate the disputed succession after 1290, he chose John Balliol as king, then treated him as a vassal until the Scots allied with France. Edward invaded in 1296, deposed Balliol and carried off the Stone of Scone, but rebellion flared under William Wallace and then Robert the Bruce. War with Philip IV of France over Gascony drained his treasury at the same time. Edward died in 1307 at Burgh by Sands, marching north once more against the Bruce, and was buried at Westminster under a plain tomb inscribed, by tradition, “Hammer of the Scots.”
