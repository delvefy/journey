Gemistos Plethon

Byzantine philosopher, last of the Platonists, c.1355–1452

George Gemistos was born about 1355, probably at Constantinople, into a family attached to the patriarchal court. He took the surname Plethon — the “full” or “abundant,” an antique echo of Platon — in his maturity, and the substitution turned out to be programmatic. As a young man he studied at the Ottoman court of Murad I or Bayezid I at Edirne under a teacher named Elissaeus, said to have been a Jewish Averroist, who introduced him to Persian Neoplatonism and astrology. Returning to Constantinople, he was suspect for his open Hellenism; about 1410 Manuel II exiled him honourably to the Despotate of the Morea, where he taught at Mistra for the rest of his long life and became chief judicial officer of the despotate.

His memoranda to Manuel II and to the despot Theodore II — the addresses on the affairs of the Peloponnese (1416) and on virtue (1419) — proposed nothing less than the refounding of the Peloponnese as a Hellenic republic on Platonic lines: a citizen militia in place of mercenaries, abolition of monastic landholding, division of the people into producers, soldiers, and rulers. None of it was carried out. In 1438–39 he was attached, despite his age, to the embassy of John VIII to the Council of Ferrara-Florence. He took little interest in the doctrinal negotiations, but his afternoons in Florence were spent lecturing to interested Italians on the differences between Plato and Aristotle — De differentiis was the short Greek treatise he composed there — and it was this informal academy that fired Cosimo de’ Medici’s patronage of the young Marsilio Ficino and the Platonic Academy of Careggi.

He died at Mistra in June 1452, perhaps a year short of a hundred, just before the Ottomans took Constantinople. After his death his Book of Laws, modelled on Plato, was found among his papers and burned by his pupil Gennadius Scholarios, who had become patriarch of Constantinople and was scandalised by its programme of an explicit return to the Olympian gods read allegorically. Sigismondo Malatesta of Rimini brought his bones back from Mistra in 1465 and reburied them in the Tempio Malatestiano under an inscription naming him “the prince of the philosophers of his time.” His direct legacy in the West was the Florentine Platonic revival, the indirect legacy of which fills the next century of European thought.
