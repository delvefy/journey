William Hazlitt

1778–1830, English essayist and critic

William Hazlitt was born at Maidstone in Kent, the son of a Unitarian minister. The family followed religious controversy across Ireland and to the United States before returning to Wem in Shropshire, where Hazlitt grew up in his father's library. In 1798 he walked from Wem to Nether Stowey to hear Samuel Taylor Coleridge preach, and the visit, recalled long after in “My First Acquaintance with Poets,” brought him face to face with William Wordsworth as well. He intended at first to be a painter, studied in the Louvre, and as a young man portrayed his lifelong friend Charles Lamb in the costume of a Venetian senator.

From about 1812 he turned wholly to writing. He reviewed plays for the Morning Chronicle and the Times, championing the new realism of Edmund Kean against the older grand manner of John Philip Kemble. His Characters of Shakespear's Plays (1817) read each tragedy and comedy as a psychological study, and his lectures on the English poets, the comic writers, and the elizabethan dramatists drew large London audiences.

He revered the French Revolution and Napoleon Buonaparte to the end, breaking publicly with the older Lake poets when they recanted their radicalism, and after Waterloo poured his last decade into a four-volume Life of Napoleon (1828–1830). The Spirit of the Age (1825) sketched in turn Jeremy Bentham, William Godwin, Edmund Burke, Walter Scott, Lord Byron, Coleridge, Wordsworth, Pitt the Younger, and the young Keats and Shelley, the most influential gallery of contemporary portraits in English. The painter J.M.W. Turner also drew his attention in essays on imitation.

His private life was less serene: an unsatisfactory first marriage, the disastrous obsession with his landlord's daughter recorded in the Liber Amoris (1823), and chronic poverty pursued him to the end. He died of stomach cancer in a Soho lodging in September 1830, leaving the famous last words, “Well, I've had a happy life.”
