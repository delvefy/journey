Bernabò Visconti

Lord of Milan, c. 1323–1385

Bernabò Visconti, born around 1323, came to power in mid-century sharing the lordship of Milan with his brothers, among them Galeazzo II. When the inheritance was divided he took the city of Milan and the eastern lands, ruling with a violence that became proverbial. He waged a long and bitter feud with the papacy, was excommunicated more than once, and met the popes’ censures with open contempt — a defiance around which lurid stories gathered, including the tale that he forced two papal envoys to swallow the parchment of the bull they had brought him.

He taxed his subjects heavily, kept a famous and ruinous passion for hunting dogs that he billeted upon the populace, and married Beatrice Regina della Scala of the ruling house of Verona, with whom he had a great brood of children whose marriages he used to weave alliances across Italy and beyond. His court stood within the wider Visconti world that had given shelter to the poet Petrarch, and his reputation reached northern Europe: Geoffrey Chaucer came to Milan on an embassy in 1378 and later set Bernabò among the fallen great in the Monk’s Tale, “scourge of Lombardy.” To bind the formidable English captain John Hawkwood to his service, he gave him an illegitimate daughter in marriage.

His end came from within his own house. In 1385 his nephew Gian Galeazzo, son of Galeazzo II, feigned a pilgrimage, seized him on the road outside Milan, and shut him up in the castle of Trezzo. There Bernabò died within months, reportedly poisoned, and his nephew gathered the whole Visconti dominion into the single state that would soon become the Duchy of Milan.
