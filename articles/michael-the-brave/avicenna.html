Avicenna

Persian polymath and physician, c.980–1037

Ibn Sina, known in the West as Avicenna, was born about 980 near Bukhara in Transoxiana, then under the Persian Samanid dynasty. A prodigy who is said to have known the Quran by heart at ten and to have mastered logic, mathematics and medicine in his teens, he won early favour by curing the Samanid ruler, which gave him the run of the royal library. The collapse of his patrons launched him on a restless career, serving a succession of Persian princes as physician and vizier while writing prodigiously through political upheaval and on the road.

His two greatest works dominated learning for centuries. The Canon of Medicine gathered the medical knowledge of the Greeks — above all Galen — and the Islamic world into a single ordered system that remained a standard textbook in Europe and Asia for some six hundred years. The Book of Healing, despite its title a vast encyclopedia of philosophy and science, set out his metaphysics and natural philosophy.

Building on Aristotle, Avicenna drew a famous distinction between essence and existence and argued from the contingency of the world to a single “Necessary Existent”; his “floating man” thought experiment sought to prove the soul’s awareness of itself apart from the body. His system became the dominant philosophy of the eastern Islamic world, provoking the great refutation of al-Ghazali and, in turn, the defence mounted by Averroes. Translated into Latin, his ideas passed to the schoolmen, and Thomas Aquinas grappled closely with them. Avicenna died at Hamadan in 1037, the most influential thinker of the Islamic golden age.
