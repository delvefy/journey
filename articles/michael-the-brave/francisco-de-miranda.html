Francisco de Miranda

Venezuelan revolutionary precursor, 1750–1816.

Sebastián Francisco de Miranda y Rodríguez de Espinoza was born in Caracas to a Canary Islands shopkeeper who had bought criollo status. After a Jesuit education at the local university he sailed to Spain at twenty-one, purchased a captaincy in the Princess's Regiment, and over the next decade served in North Africa, Cuba, and the Yucatán. In 1781, sent as a Spanish officer to the Gulf coast, he fought at Bernardo de Gálvez's siege of Pensacola against the British and so took, against his own crown, an early part in the American Revolution. Already he was reading prohibited Enlightenment authors and recording his thoughts in the diary — the Colombeia, sixty-three folio volumes — he would keep until his death.

Hounded by Spanish counter-intelligence in 1783 he deserted into the United States, where he toured the new republic and met George Washington, Alexander Hamilton, Henry Knox, and Thomas Paine. The next eighteen years of his life are the wandering apprenticeship of a one-man independence movement: Russia, where Catherine the Great gave him uniforms and the rank of colonel; the courts of Sweden, Denmark, Prussia, and the Italian states; Constantinople; London, where he lobbied William Pitt the Younger and Jeremy Bentham for British backing; revolutionary France, where as a máriscal de camp under Dumouriez he fought at Valmy and Jemappes and was twice tried and acquitted by Robespierre's Revolutionary Tribunal. He returned to London, opened in his Grafton Way house the planning centre of Spanish-American independence, and there received the visiting young Bernardo O'Higgins and, in 1810, his fellow Venezuelans Andrés Bello and the young Simón Bolívar.

His 1806 expedition from New York — financed by US merchants and accompanied by William Stephens Smith, John Adams's son-in-law — landed at Coro and was repulsed by the Spanish. In 1811 he returned to Caracas under the patriot junta, took the title Generalissimo in 1812, and after the catastrophic earthquake and the loss of Puerto Cabello surrendered to the royalists in a capitulation Bolívar considered treasonous. Bolívar handed him over to the Spanish, who shipped him to the dungeons of La Carraca near Cádiz. He died there in 1816, in irons, sixty-six years old. The black, blue, and yellow tricolor he had flown over his 1806 ship survived him as the flag of Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador.
