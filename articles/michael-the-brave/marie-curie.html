Marie Curie

Polish-French physicist and chemist, 1867–1934

Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw under Russian rule, she was barred as a woman from higher education at home and saved for years to study in Paris, arriving nearly penniless to enrol at the Sorbonne. There she met and married the physicist Pierre Curie, and together they took up the puzzle of the mysterious rays emitted by uranium. Coining the word “radioactivity,” she isolated two new elements, polonium—named for her homeland—and radium, processing tons of pitchblende by hand in a leaky shed.

In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre and Henri Becquerel, becoming the first woman to win a Nobel. After Pierre was killed in a street accident in 1906, she took over his professorship, the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. In 1911 she won a second Nobel, in Chemistry, for the discovery of radium and polonium—still the only person honoured in two different sciences.

During the First World War she equipped mobile radiography units, the “little Curies,” and personally drove them to the front to help surgeons locate shrapnel. Her daughter Irène would later win a Nobel of her own. Years of unprotected exposure to radiation ruined her health, and she died in 1934 of a blood disease almost certainly caused by her life’s work. The new physics of the atom that she helped open was carried forward by contemporaries such as Ernest Rutherford.
