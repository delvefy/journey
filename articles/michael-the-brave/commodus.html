Commodus

Roman emperor, 161–192 AD

Commodus was the son of the philosopher-emperor Marcus Aurelius, raised up as co-ruler by his father and inheriting sole power on Marcus's death in 180. Unlike the adopted heirs who had given Rome its run of able emperors, he was born to the purple, and he proved a disastrous departure from his father's example. He quickly bought peace on the Danube frontier where Marcus had fought so long, and returned to a life of pleasure in Rome, leaving the business of government to a succession of grasping favourites.

An assassination plot involving his own sister early in the reign left him fearful and vengeful, and he turned to executing senators and seizing their wealth. His vanity swelled into open megalomania: he identified himself with Hercules, parading in a lion's skin, renamed the months and even the city of Rome after himself, and—most scandalous of all—descended into the arena to fight as a gladiator and slay beasts before the people, a spectacle the eyewitness Cassius Dio recorded with horror.

Misrule, paranoia, and extravagance hollowed out the state, ending the long golden age of the Antonines. On the last day of 192 a conspiracy of his closest attendants, after poison failed, had the wrestler Narcissus strangle him in his bath. His death plunged Rome into the violent Year of the Five Emperors, from which Septimius Severus would emerge as master of the empire. The Senate damned his memory, though Severus, seeking the lustre of the old dynasty, later had him declared a god.
