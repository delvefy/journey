Umar ibn al-Khattab

Second caliph of Islam, c.583–644

Umar was born around 583 in Mecca, a man of the Quraysh known in his youth for his physical strength and his fierce hostility to the new religion — until his sudden conversion made him one of Islam’s most formidable champions. His daughter Hafsa became a wife of the Prophet, binding him closely to Muhammad, and his blunt, austere sense of justice earned him the title al-Faruq, he who distinguishes right from wrong. As chief counsellor to Abu Bakr, the first caliph, he was the natural choice to succeed him in 634.

His decade as caliph transformed a regional faith into a world empire. His armies broke the two great powers of the age: at Yarmouk in 636 they wrested Syria from Byzantium, and Umar himself, riding humbly into the conquered Jerusalem, received its surrender; at Qadisiyyah and Nahavand they shattered the Sasanians and opened all Persia; and to the west they took Egypt. Few conquests in history have been so swift or so lasting.

Umar was as much a builder of institutions as of empire. He founded the register of pensions, established the Islamic calendar dating from the Hijra, planted the garrison cities of Kufa, Basra and Fustat from which the new lands were governed, and appointed judges to administer justice — all while living with a simplicity that became proverbial. In 644 he was stabbed at prayer in Medina by a Persian captive and died, naming a council to choose his successor. To Sunni Muslims he remains the model of the just and rightly-guided ruler.
