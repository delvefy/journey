Mikhail Bakunin

Russian revolutionary, founder of collectivist anarchism, 1814–1876

Mikhail Bakunin was the great apostle of anarchism and, with Karl Marx, one of the two poles of nineteenth-century revolutionary socialism. Born in 1814 into the Russian nobility, he was captivated as a young man by German philosophy, immersing himself in Hegel before forsaking Russia for the wandering life of a professional revolutionary in the West.

He plunged into the revolutions of 1848 and 1849, agitating across Europe and helping to lead the insurrection at Dresden, where the composer Richard Wagner fought among the rebels. Captured, he was twice sentenced to death, surrendered to the Russian authorities, and buried for years in the Peter and Paul Fortress before being exiled to Siberia—from which, in 1861, he escaped clear around the world and back to Europe.

In his final and most famous phase he preached a thoroughgoing anarchism: the abolition of the state, the church, and private property, and their replacement by a free federation of communes. Within the First International he fell into furious conflict with Marx, pitting his anti-authoritarian vision against Marx's centralized socialism, until the quarrel split the movement and Bakunin was expelled.

His doctrines lit anarchist movements across Italy, Spain, and Russia, and his cry that “the urge to destroy is also a creative urge” echoed among rebels for generations; the novelist Turgenev even drew a hero from him. He died, exhausted, in Bern in 1876. The thinker on whose foundations he had built, the first man ever to call himself an anarchist, was Pierre-Joseph Proudhon.
