Elizabeth Gaskell

1810–1865, English novelist and biographer

Elizabeth Cleghorn Stevenson was born in Chelsea, London, the daughter of a Unitarian preacher turned Treasury clerk. Her mother died within a year of her birth, and the child was sent to be raised by her aunt at Knutsford in Cheshire, the small market town she would transform into the setting of Cranford. In 1832 she married William Gaskell, a Unitarian minister in Manchester, and entered the working life of the manufacturing North, which became the subject of her early fiction.

The death of her infant son William in 1845 turned her to writing as consolation. Mary Barton (1848), a novel of starving handloom-weavers and the moral wreckage of laissez-faire Manchester, made her name and won her the friendship of Charles Dickens, who published Cranford in his Household Words from 1851. The serial portrait of the slow, female-led life of a country town established her popularity, and the industrial novels Ruth (1853) and North and South (1854–55) cemented her reputation as a moral chronicler of industrial Britain.

In 1850 she travelled to the Lake District, where she met Charlotte Brontë at the home of a mutual friend. The friendship that followed, deep but brief, ended with Charlotte's death in 1855. Patrick Brontë asked Mrs Gaskell to write his daughter's life, and The Life of Charlotte Brontë (1857) was the result — a candid Victorian biography that, despite a libel suit forcing revisions, fixed the parsonage at Haworth in the public imagination and rescued the youngest sister Anne from near oblivion.

She was a close friend of Florence Nightingale, whom she visited at Lea Hurst during the writing of North and South, and corresponded with Thomas Carlyle and William Wordsworth. Her finest single novel, Wives and Daughters, was running serially in the Cornhill when she died suddenly of heart failure in November 1865 at fifty-five. The novel was published unfinished the following year.
