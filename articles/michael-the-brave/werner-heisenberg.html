Werner Heisenberg

German theoretical physicist, 1901–1976

Trained at Munich under Arnold Sommerfeld and at Göttingen under Max Born, Heisenberg spent formative years in Copenhagen as the favourite of Niels Bohr. In 1925, while recovering from hay fever on the barren island of Helgoland, he hit upon a wholly new mechanics, expressing the observable properties of atoms in arrays of numbers—what Born recognised as matrices. This matrix mechanics was the first complete formulation of quantum theory.

Two years later he stated the uncertainty principle: the more precisely one knows a particle’s position, the less precisely one can know its momentum, a limit built into nature itself. With Bohr he forged the Copenhagen interpretation, and in 1932, at the age of thirty, he received the Nobel Prize. His mysterious 1941 visit to the occupied Bohr in Copenhagen, its purpose still disputed, became one of the haunting episodes of the war.

Heisenberg remained in Germany under the Nazis, enduring attacks as a “white Jew” for teaching relativity, and became the leading figure of the German nuclear programme. Whether he failed to build a bomb through incompetence or quiet reluctance is debated to this day; interned at Farm Hall in England, he and his colleagues were secretly recorded reacting with shock to news of Hiroshima. After the war he rebuilt German physics. His opposite number in that race, who did deliver the bomb, was the American J. Robert Oppenheimer.
