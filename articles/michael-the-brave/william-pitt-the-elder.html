William Pitt the Elder

British statesman, “the Great Commoner,” 1708–1778

Pitt entered Parliament as a young man and made his name as a fierce orator, attacking the cautious and corrupt management of Robert Walpole and championing a more aggressive, patriotic foreign policy. His independence and his appeal to opinion outside the narrow political class earned him the nickname of the Great Commoner, and though kings distrusted him, the public came to see him as the embodiment of national pride.

His finest hour came in the Seven Years’ War. Taking effective control of the war effort, he poured resources into the navy and into subsidising allies in Europe while striking at French power overseas. The result was the “year of victories” in 1759, when British forces triumphed across the globe, capturing Quebec and securing Canada, gaining the upper hand in India, and winning command of the seas. Under his direction Britain emerged as the dominant imperial and maritime power.

Created Earl of Chatham, he served as prime minister in the 1760s, though illness and a difficult temper limited his effectiveness. In his final years he turned his eloquence to the crisis with the American colonies, urging conciliation and warning against the policies of George III’s ministers that were driving the colonists to rebellion, even as he insisted on preserving the empire. He collapsed while speaking in the House of Lords and died soon after. His son, William Pitt the Younger, would carry the family name to even greater heights.

The road continues to George III of the United Kingdom.
