Homer

Greek epic poet, traditionally 8th century BC

Homer stands at the very fountainhead of Western literature, yet almost nothing about him can be known for certain. Tradition held that he was a blind bard from Ionia, perhaps from Chios or Smyrna, who composed and recited his verse aloud; but scholars have long debated whether one poet, several, or a whole tradition of oral singers lay behind the works that bear his name—the famous “Homeric question.” What is not in doubt is the monumental power of the two epics ascribed to him.

The Iliad tells of a few terrible weeks in the tenth year of the Greek siege of Troy, centred on the wrath of the hero Achilles, and ends not with the city’s fall but with the burial of his enemy Hector—a meditation on rage, honour, mortality, and pity. The Odyssey follows the cunning Odysseus through ten years of wandering, monsters, and homecoming. Composed in rolling hexameters and rich in vivid simile, both became the shared inheritance of the Greek world.

For antiquity Homer was simply “the Poet,” the schoolbook of Greece whom Plato wrestled with and Aristotle analysed, the model Virgil set out to rival, and the inspiration the young Alexander the Great carried into Asia. Dante crowned him sovereign of poets, and millennia later Heinrich Schliemann, taking the Iliad as history, dug into the earth in search of his Troy. No poet has cast a longer shadow.
