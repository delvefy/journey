Alexander III of Scotland

King of Scots, 1241–1286

Alexander III was born in 1241, the only son of Alexander II, and came to the throne as a boy of seven in 1249. His long minority was dominated by rival factions of nobles, but his position was anchored by an early marriage: in 1251, at York, the ten-year-old king wed Margaret, daughter of Henry III of England, binding the Scottish crown to the English court and making the future Edward I his brother-in-law.

Taking power in his own right in the 1260s, Alexander pursued the long Scottish ambition of mastering the western seaboard. When Haakon IV of Norway led a great fleet to defend his island possessions, the inconclusive clash at Largs in 1263 and Haakon’s death on the return voyage left the initiative with the Scots; by the Treaty of Perth in 1266 Norway ceded the Hebrides and the Isle of Man. The decades that followed were remembered, perhaps with hindsight’s warm exaggeration, as a golden age of peace, good harvests and firm royal justice.

The tragedy of his reign was dynastic. His two sons and his daughter all died before him, leaving as heir only an infant granddaughter, Margaret, the Maid of Norway. On a wild night in March 1286 Alexander, hurrying along the Fife coast to rejoin his young second wife, became separated from his guides in the dark and was found dead the next morning at the foot of an embankment near Kinghorn, his neck broken. With him died the settled order of his kingdom. The Maid’s death soon after left thirteen claimants disputing the crown, and the realm turned for arbitration to the one neighbour strong enough—and ambitious enough—to exploit the vacancy.

The road continues to Henry III of England.
