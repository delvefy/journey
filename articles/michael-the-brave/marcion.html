Marcion

Christian heretic, c.85–160

Marcion was born about 85 at Sinope on the Black Sea, the son, it is said, of a bishop, and a wealthy shipowner by trade. Around 140 he came to Rome and joined the Christian congregation there, making it a large gift, but within a few years his teaching had set him so far apart that he was expelled and his money returned. He then organized his followers into a rival church that spread with alarming speed across the empire.

His doctrine was a stark dualism. The God of the Hebrew scriptures, the stern lawgiver and creator of the material world, was, Marcion held, an inferior deity altogether different from the good and previously unknown God of love whom Jesus had revealed. He therefore rejected the entire Old Testament and, revering Paul as the one apostle who had truly understood the Gospel, assembled the first known Christian canon — an edited gospel and ten of Paul’s letters, stripped of what he judged to be Jewish corruption.

It was a fateful provocation. By proposing a fixed and narrow scripture of his own, Marcion forced the wider Church to define which writings it would accept, hastening the formation of the New Testament. The orthodox struck back hard: Justin Martyr and Irenaeus wrote against him, Tertullian devoted his longest work to demolishing his system, and Polycarp is said to have called him to his face the firstborn of Satan. He died about 160, condemned as a heretic but, in spite of himself, one of the makers of the Christian Bible.
