Hector Berlioz

French Romantic composer, 1803–1869

Berlioz was born near Grenoble and sent to Paris to study medicine, but he abandoned the dissecting room for the conservatoire against the fierce opposition of his family. Two revelations seized the impressionable young man: the symphonies of Beethoven, which showed him what music could be, and the plays of Shakespeare, performed by an English company whose leading actress, Harriet Smithson, became the object of a consuming infatuation and, eventually, his wife.

Out of that obsession came the Symphonie fantastique (1830), an audacious, autobiographical work that follows an artist’s opium-haunted passion through a recurring "fixed idea," a march to the scaffold, and a grotesque witches’ sabbath. With it Berlioz announced himself as the great pioneer of programme music and of the modern orchestra, whose colours he commanded as no one before him; his treatise on orchestration became a textbook for generations.

Further masterpieces followed: the symphony with viola Harold in Italy, written after a commission from Paganini; the dramatic symphony Roméo et Juliette; The Damnation of Faust after Goethe; a colossal Requiem; and the epic opera Les Troyens after Virgil. Never fully embraced in his own France, he supported himself by writing brilliant and acid musical criticism and by touring abroad as a conductor. He died in Paris in 1869, his genius more honoured beyond his country’s borders than within them.
