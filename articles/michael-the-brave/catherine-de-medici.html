Catherine de’ Medici

Queen and regent of France, 1519–1589

Caterina Maria Romola di Lorenzo de’ Medici was born in Florence in April 1519. Both parents died within weeks, leaving her the sole legitimate heir to the senior Medici line. She passed her childhood as a useful pawn in convents and Roman palaces, valued for the wealth she carried. In 1533 her cousin Pope Clement VII arranged her marriage, at fourteen, to the second son of Francis I of France, the future Henri II. She brought a vast dowry, Tuscan pastrycooks, and the appetite for the Italian arts that would mark her household for half a century.

For her first decade in France she was overshadowed by her husband’s mistress Diane de Poitiers, and for nine years was childless. From 1544 she bore ten children in twelve years, of whom seven survived. When Henri II died of a jousting wound in 1559, her eldest son Francis II reigned for seventeen months under the influence of the Guises and his Scottish queen, Mary Stuart. On his death in 1560 Catherine became regent for the ten-year-old Charles IX, and she remained the effective ruler of France through the long sequence of religious wars that began at Wassy in 1562.

Her policy oscillated between conciliation and ruthlessness. The Edict of Saint-Germain (1562) and the colloquy of Poissy tried to find a middle path. The wedding of her daughter Marguerite to Henry of Navarre in August 1572 was meant to seal that path; instead it ended in the St Bartholomew’s Day Massacre, for which Catherine carries the heaviest historical responsibility. She continued as queen mother and unofficial regent under her favourite son, Henry III, after his accession in 1574, consulted to the last by astrologers including Nostradamus, sponsoring court festivals and architectural projects (the Tuileries, the addition to the Louvre), and dying at Blois in January 1589, six months before the assassination of her last surviving son.
