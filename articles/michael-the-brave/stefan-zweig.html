Stefan Zweig

1881–1942, Austrian novelist, biographer, and chronicler of "the world of yesterday"

Stefan Zweig was born in Vienna into the prosperous Jewish family of a textile manufacturer in the closing decades of the Habsburg empire. He took his doctorate in 1904 with a dissertation on Hippolyte Taine, travelled tirelessly in his twenties through India, Africa and the Americas, and made his apprenticeship in poetry and translation before turning to the form he made his own: the dense, psychologically penetrating novella. Amok, Letter from an Unknown Woman, Confusion, Twenty-four Hours in the Life of a Woman and Chess Story became among the most translated works of any twentieth-century German-language author.

He served briefly in the Austro-Hungarian war archives in 1914, befriended Romain Rolland in Switzerland during his anti-war exile, and after 1918 made his house on the Kapuzinerberg above Salzburg a meeting place for the European literary aristocracy: Thomas Mann, Hermann Hesse, Joseph Roth, Hugo von Hofmannsthal, Sigmund Freud, Albert Einstein, Maxim Gorky, James Joyce. He produced popular psychological biographies — Marie Antoinette, Mary Stuart, Erasmus of Rotterdam, Magellan, Castellio against Calvin — that read as moral lessons addressed to his own catastrophic century, and the trio of essays Three Masters on Balzac, Dickens and Dostoevsky.

The Nazi seizure of Austria in March 1938 forced his exodus, first to London and Bath, where he had taken British citizenship in 1940, then to New York and finally to the small town of Petrópolis in the hills above Rio de Janeiro. There in early 1942, with his second wife Lotte, he completed his autobiography Die Welt von Gestern ("The World of Yesterday") and the short novel Schachnovelle. Despairing of the future of Europe and the spiritual home he had lost, the couple took an overdose of veronal together on the evening of 22 February 1942. He was sixty.
