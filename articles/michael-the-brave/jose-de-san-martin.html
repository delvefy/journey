José de San Martín

Argentine general and liberator, 1778–1850.

Born in Yapeyú, in what is today Argentine Corrientes, the son of a Spanish lieutenant-governor of Jesuit missions, San Martín was taken to Spain at six and enrolled at the Seminary of Nobles in Madrid and then in the Murcia infantry regiment at eleven. Through twenty-two years of Spanish service he campaigned in North Africa, the Pyrenees, and most of all in the Peninsular War against Napoleon, distinguishing himself at the Battle of Bailén in 1808 and reaching the rank of lieutenant colonel. Disillusioned with the absolutism of Ferdinand VII's restoration, he resigned, sailed for London, and from there in 1812 returned to Buenos Aires to offer his sword to the new revolutionary government.

He at once trained the cavalry that won the small but decisive engagement at San Lorenzo on the Paraná in 1813, took charge of the Army of the North against the royalists in Upper Peru, and then conceived the strategic stroke that defined him: rather than batter again through Bolivia, he would raise an army at Mendoza, lead it over the Andes by the high passes, fall on the royalists from the unguarded west, free Chile, and from there strike at the heart of Spanish power in Lima. The Crossing of the Andes in January 1817, by five separate columns through passes above 4,000 metres, was an operation of austere logistical genius. Chacabuco that February delivered Santiago to Bernardo O'Higgins; Maipú in April 1818 secured Chilean independence; in 1820 he sailed from Valparaíso to Peru and the next August entered Lima as Protector.

His meeting with Simón Bolívar at Guayaquil in July 1822, held in private and recorded only in elliptical witnesses, ended his American career. Convinced that two liberators could not finish the war together, he resigned the Protectorate, refused honours and pensions, and sailed for Europe. He lived austerely with his daughter Mercedes in Brussels, Paris, and finally Boulogne-sur-Mer, declined the dictatorship of Argentina, and died there in 1850 without seeing his homeland again. His remains were repatriated in 1880 to the Buenos Aires cathedral, where they lie in a marble mausoleum guarded by ceremonial grenadiers.
