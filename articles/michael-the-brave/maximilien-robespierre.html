Maximilien Robespierre

French revolutionary leader, 1758–1794

A provincial lawyer from Arras, steeped in the writings of Rousseau, Robespierre was elected to the Estates-General in 1789 and rose to prominence through the Jacobin Club, where his rigid principles and incorruptible reputation earned him a devoted following. In the early Revolution he opposed the death penalty and championed broad suffrage, but as the new republic was beset by war and internal revolt his views hardened, and he came to argue that the king, Louis XVI, must die for the nation to live.

From 1793 he dominated the Committee of Public Safety, the body that ruled revolutionary France during its gravest crisis. In the name of public safety and republican virtue he presided over the Reign of Terror, in which thousands were sent to the guillotine, including not only aristocrats and the deposed king and queen but also revolutionary rivals such as the Girondins and, in turn, Danton and his followers. He sought to refound society on a civic religion, the Cult of the Supreme Being.

His insistence that virtue must be enforced by terror, and a law that stripped the accused of defence, spread fear even among his colleagues. In July 1794, the month of Thermidor, the Convention turned on him; arrested amid chaos and wounded in the jaw, he was guillotined the next day without trial. His fall ended the Terror, and he remains one of history’s most debated figures, claimed by some as a martyr of equality and condemned by others as the embodiment of revolutionary fanaticism.
