Rudyard Kipling

1865–1936, English novelist, poet, and Nobel laureate

Joseph Rudyard Kipling was born in Bombay to John Lockwood Kipling, a Sanskrit-reading art-school principal, and Alice MacDonald. His earliest language was Hindustani, picked up from the household ayah, and he heard his first stories from servants in the compound. At five he and his sister were sent to a Southsea boarding-house, where he was bullied and beaten for six years, an experience he turned into Baa Baa, Black Sheep. After United Services College in Devon he returned to India in 1882 as a sub-editor at the Civil and Military Gazette in Lahore, and at twenty-one was already publishing the verses and sketches that became Departmental Ditties and Plain Tales from the Hills.

The fame travelled to London ahead of him. He arrived in 1889 by way of San Francisco — pausing in Elmira to interview Mark Twain — and within five years had produced Barrack-Room Ballads, the two Jungle Books, and married Carrie Balestier of Vermont, where they settled and where his daughter Josephine was born. A bitter quarrel with his brother-in-law drove him back to England. From Bateman's, a Sussex house he bought in 1902, came Kim, the Just So Stories, Puck of Pook's Hill, and the recessional poems.

He was the laureate of the imperial moment without ever holding the laureateship; he refused the offer twice, along with the Order of Merit. In 1907 he became the first English-language writer to win the Nobel Prize for Literature, at forty-one still the youngest ever to do so. The First World War cost him his only son John, killed at Loos in September 1915, and Kipling devoted his last decades to the Imperial War Graves Commission, choosing for its monuments Ecclesiasticus's line "Their name liveth for evermore." He died in London in January 1936 and was buried in Poets' Corner.
