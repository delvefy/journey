Mao Zedong

Chinese revolutionary and founder of the People's Republic, 1893–1976

Mao Zedong was born in 1893 at Shaoshan, a village in Hunan, the son of a prosperous peasant grain dealer. He read the Confucian classics at the village school and the new Western-influenced literature—translations of Adam Smith and Herbert Spencer, the new journalism out of Shanghai—at the Hunan teachers' college in Changsha. He served briefly in the army during the 1911 revolution and went to Beijing in 1918 as assistant librarian at Beida, where he read Marx and Lenin under the librarian Li Dazhao and the young Chen Duxiu. In 1921 he was a founding delegate of the Chinese Communist Party.

The collapse of the first United Front with the Kuomintang in 1927 sent him into the countryside. Against orthodox Comintern doctrine, he argued that revolution in China would be carried not by industrial proletarians but by the peasants. The Long March of 1934–35, the six-thousand-mile retreat of the Red Army from Jiangxi to the loess caves of Yan'an, made him the unrivalled leader of the Party. From Yan'an he wrote the essays "On Practice," "On Contradiction," and "On Protracted War" that codified Maoism, fought the Japanese occupation, and in 1949 expelled Chiang Kai-shek's nationalist government across the Taiwan Strait. On 1 October 1949, from the rostrum of Tiananmen, he proclaimed the People's Republic.

His twenty-seven years in power transformed China and cost it tens of millions of lives. Land reform redistributed the rural economy. The Great Leap Forward of 1958–62, an attempt to industrialise overnight through backyard steel furnaces and rural communes, produced the worst famine in recorded history. The Cultural Revolution of 1966–76 unleashed the Red Guards on the Party itself, smashed China's universities and historical heritage, and exiled tens of millions to the countryside. He played the Sino-Soviet split, breaking with Khrushchev's Russia, and in 1972 he opened relations with Richard Nixon's United States.

He died in Beijing on 9 September 1976, having outlived almost all his founding rivals. His portrait still hangs over Tiananmen Square. The Russian revolutionary whose pamphlets had taught the young Mao Marxism at the Beida library, who had first shown how a small disciplined party could seize and hold power in a backward agrarian country, was Vladimir Lenin.
