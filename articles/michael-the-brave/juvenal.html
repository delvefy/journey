Juvenal

Roman satirist, c. 55–c. 140 CE

Decimus Junius Juvenalis, known as Juvenal, was the last and the fiercest of the great Roman verse satirists. Little is certain of his life—he was born around 55 CE, perhaps at Aquinum, and tradition holds that he suffered a period of exile—but his sixteen Satires, written under Trajan and Hadrian, secured him a place beside Horace as a master of the form.

Where Horace had smiled, Juvenal raged. "Indignation makes the verse," he declared, and he turned that indignation on the whole spectacle of imperial Rome: the corruption of the great houses, the swarming foreigners, the perils of the crowded and burning city in his famous third satire, the catalogue of female vice in his sixth, and the emptiness of human ambition in his tenth.

No Latin poet has bequeathed more phrases to later speech. From him come "bread and circuses," the bitter formula for a people bought with food and games; "who will guard the guardians themselves?"; "a sound mind in a sound body"; and the "rare bird" of the proverb. His verse made him a model for friends and successors alike, and he addressed his contemporary, the epigrammatist Martial, with warm respect.

Read and quarried throughout antiquity and the Middle Ages, Juvenal became the fountainhead of European satire. John Dryden rendered him into English verse, and Samuel Johnson recast his third and tenth satires as the celebrated poems "London" and "The Vanity of Human Wishes," carrying Juvenal's voice into a new age.
