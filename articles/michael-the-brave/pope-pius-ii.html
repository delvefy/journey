Pope Pius II

Humanist and pope, 1405–1464

Enea Silvio Piccolomini was born on 18 October 1405 at Corsignano near Siena, into a noble family fallen on hard times. A dazzling Latinist, he made his name in the literary world of the early Renaissance with poetry, a widely read love story and a stream of elegant letters, and he threw himself into the conciliar politics of the day, serving the Council of Basel and an antipope before attaching himself to the court of Emperor Frederick III, who crowned him poet laureate and employed him as imperial secretary.

Reconciling with Rome, he took holy orders late and rose with startling speed to bishop, cardinal and, in 1458, the papal throne, choosing the name Pius after Virgil’s “pious Aeneas.” The dominant cause of his pontificate was the Ottoman advance: with Constantinople newly fallen to Mehmed II, Pius summoned the Congress of Mantua to launch a crusade and even composed a remarkable letter urging the sultan to accept Christianity. He leaned on learned allies such as the Greek cardinal Bessarion to rally Christendom, but the princes of Europe gave him little.

A humanist to the last, Pius wrote his Commentaries, the only autobiography ever composed by a reigning pope, and rebuilt his birthplace as the model Renaissance town of Pienza. In 1460 his bull Execrabilis condemned any appeal from pope to council, asserting papal supremacy against the conciliar movement he had once served. He died at Ancona in August 1464, waiting in vain for the crusade fleet that never sailed, his learning outlasting his frustrated policy.
