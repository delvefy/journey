Émile Zola

French novelist and critic, 1840–1902

Zola was the founder and theorist of naturalism, which sought to apply the methods of science to the novel, depicting human beings as products of heredity and environment with unflinching, documentary frankness. His monumental achievement was the twenty-novel cycle Les Rougon-Macquart, tracing one family across the Second Empire and laying bare the lives of miners, peasants, washerwomen, market traders, and prostitutes. Germinal, his harrowing epic of a coal-miners’ strike, and L’Assommoir, on the ravages of drink, are among its masterpieces.

A child of Aix-en-Provence, he had grown up the inseparable friend of the painter Paul Cézanne, and as a young Paris critic he became an early and fierce champion of the new painting, defending Édouard Manet when the public jeered. His own gatherings drew the realist and naturalist writers of the day around the older Flaubert.

In 1898 Zola risked everything on a point of justice. Convinced that the Jewish army officer Alfred Dreyfus had been falsely convicted of treason, he published his thunderous open letter J’accuse…!, accusing the army of a cover-up. Convicted of libel, he fled briefly to England, but his intervention turned the tide and helped clear Dreyfus. He died in 1902, asphyxiated by a blocked chimney—possibly murder—a hero of the Republic and of conscience.
