Uthman ibn Affan

Third caliph of Islam, c.576–656

Uthman was born about 576 into the wealthy Umayyad clan of the Quraysh and was a prosperous merchant when he became one of the early converts to Islam. He married the Prophet’s daughter Ruqayyah and, after her death, her sister Umm Kulthum, earning the epithet Dhul-Nurayn, “possessor of two lights.” Famed for his generosity, he poured his fortune into the young community’s causes. On the death of Umar in 644, the council appointed to choose a successor selected Uthman as the third caliph.

Under him the conquests rolled on, into North Africa and across the seas, where the first Muslim fleet checked the navy of Byzantium. His most enduring act was to fix the text of the Quran: fearing that variant readings might divide the faithful, he had an authoritative version prepared and copies sent to the great cities, ordering the rest destroyed — the recension that Muslims have used ever since.

Yet his reign ended in catastrophe. Charged with favouring his Umayyad relatives, whom he set over rich provinces — Muawiya among them in Syria — he lost the confidence of many, and in 656 rebels besieged him in his house at Medina and killed him as he sat reading the Quran. His blood, unavenged, opened the first great civil war of Islam, for his kinsmen demanded justice from his successor Ali. Honoured by Sunnis as the third of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs, Uthman left a community that would never again be wholly united.
