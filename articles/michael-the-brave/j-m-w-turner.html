J.M.W. Turner

English Romantic painter of light, 1775–1851

Joseph Mallord William Turner was born in 1775 in Covent Garden, the son of a London barber. A precocious draughtsman, he was admitted to the Royal Academy schools at fourteen and exhibited there as a teenager. He travelled the British countryside selling watercolour views, fed himself on the topographical print trade, and by twenty-four had been elected an Associate of the Royal Academy.

From early on his ambition was history painting in landscape, with Claude Lorrain as his model and rival. The Peace of Amiens of 1802 let him cross to Paris and the Alps, and he returned with a new appetite for sublime mountain weather. Through the 1810s and 1820s he poured out enormous canvases—Snow Storm: Hannibal and his Army Crossing the Alps, Dido Building Carthage, The Fighting Temeraire—in which the human subject is steadily dissolved into atmosphere.

He read Goethe's Theory of Colours and answered it with the prismatic Light and Colour (Goethe's Theory) of 1843. His late pictures—the steamboats in storms, the Rain, Steam, and Speed of a locomotive crossing a Maidenhead viaduct, the Norham Castle Sunrise—reduce land, sea and sky to washes of yellow and white that anticipate Impressionism by a generation. The young critic John Ruskin, in Modern Painters, mounted a five-volume defence of him against accusations of insanity, and Turner became Britain's most famous living painter.

He was a secretive man who kept a second household under an assumed name, hoarded thousands of unsold pictures, and at his death in 1851 left them all to the nation. In his will he stipulated that two of his Carthaginian landscapes hang forever between two of Claude's in the National Gallery. Monet, in his London exiles, was openly indebted to him. The contemporary English landscape painter whose career ran in steady, more naturalistic parallel to Turner's own was John Constable.
