Ignatius of Loyola

Founder of the Society of Jesus, 1491–1556

Íñigo López de Loyola was born in 1491 in the Basque country, the youngest son of a noble house, and spent his youth as a courtier and soldier with a taste for arms and vanity. His life turned on a wound. In 1521, while defending Pamplona against a French army, a cannonball shattered his leg, and during a long and painful convalescence the only books to hand were lives of Christ and the saints. Reading them, he resolved to give his life to God.

He laid down his sword at the mountain shrine of Montserrat and withdrew to nearby Manresa, where months of prayer, fasting and inner struggle produced the core of his Spiritual Exercises, a methodical programme of meditation that became the order’s great instrument. After a pilgrimage to Jerusalem he went back to school as a grown man among boys, studying at Barcelona, Alcalá and Salamanca — where suspicious inquisitors briefly detained him — and finally at the University of Paris. There he drew to himself a band of devoted companions, Francis Xavier among them, and in 1534 they bound themselves by vows at Montmartre.

In 1540 Pope Paul III formally approved their brotherhood as the Society of Jesus, and the companions elected Ignatius their first Superior General. From a few rooms in Rome he governed an order that grew with astonishing speed, founding colleges across Europe and sending missionaries to India, Japan and the Americas. Disciplined, obedient and learned, the Jesuits became the sharpest weapon of the Catholic reformation. Ignatius directed it all until his death in 1556, leaving behind his Exercises, the order’s Constitutions, and a new model of religious life that bore his stamp for centuries.

The road continues to Francis Xavier.
