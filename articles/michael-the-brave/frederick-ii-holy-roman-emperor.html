Frederick II, Holy Roman Emperor

Holy Roman Emperor, 1194–1250

Frederick II, grandson of Frederick Barbarossa, was born in 1194 and orphaned young, a boy-king of Sicily left under the guardianship of the most powerful of medieval popes, Innocent III. From that precarious beginning he rose to be crowned Holy Roman Emperor in 1220 and to rule an arc of lands from the Baltic to the African shore of the Mediterranean. Contemporaries called him Stupor Mundi, the Wonder of the World, for the dazzling, sceptical brilliance of his court at Palermo, where Latin, Greek and Arabic learning mingled, where he founded the University of Naples and wrote a careful treatise on the falconry he loved.

His reign was consumed by a duel with the papacy that never truly ended. Excommunicated more than once by Gregory IX and at last solemnly deposed by Innocent IV at the Council of Lyon in 1245, he was branded by his enemies as the Antichrist himself. Yet in 1228, though under the ban of the Church, he sailed on the Sixth Crusade and recovered Jerusalem not by the sword but by negotiation with the sultan al-Kamil, crowning himself king in the Holy Sepulchre—a feat that outraged friend and foe alike.

In Italy he fought the revived Lombard League for control of the north, winning a great victory at Cortenuova in 1237 but breaking his strength against the walls of cities like Parma. For his own kingdom of Sicily he issued the Constitutions of Melfi, one of the most advanced bodies of law of the age. He died suddenly in 1250 with his struggle unresolved; within a generation his Hohenstaufen line was extinguished, and the empire sank into a long interregnum. Few rulers have so fascinated posterity, suspended forever between the medieval world and something stranger and more modern.
