Niccolò Paganini

Italian violin virtuoso, 1782–1840

Paganini was born in Genoa and trained relentlessly from early childhood by an ambitious father, developing a command of the violin without precedent in the history of the instrument. By his thirties he had begun the concert tours that would carry his fame across Italy and then all of Europe, drawing audiences into a state of near-hysteria.

His technique seemed to contemporaries scarcely human. He extended the possibilities of the violin with dazzling left-hand pizzicato, ringing harmonics, and feats performed on a single string, and his gaunt figure, hollow cheeks, and theatrical manner fed a persistent rumour that he had sold his soul to the devil in exchange for such powers. His 24 Caprices for unaccompanied violin remain a summit of the repertoire, a compendium of the difficulties he had conquered.

More than any single player, Paganini created the figure of the Romantic virtuoso, the soloist as a being apart, and his example reached far beyond his own instrument: hearing him, the young Liszt resolved to become the Paganini of the piano. His haunting themes were taken up by later composers from Schumann and Brahms to Rachmaninoff. He amassed and gambled away fortunes, and his health, long undermined, gave way in Nice in 1840; the diabolical legends surrounding him delayed a consecrated burial for years.
