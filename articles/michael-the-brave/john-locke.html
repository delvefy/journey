John Locke

English philosopher, 1632–1704

John Locke was the founder of philosophical empiricism and of political liberalism, the most influential English thinker of his age. Born in 1632 and educated at Oxford, he trained as a physician and man of science, working alongside Robert Boyle and counting Isaac Newton among his friends. His life took its decisive turn when he entered the service of the Earl of Shaftesbury as physician and confidential adviser, binding his fortunes to that turbulent statesman and driving him into exile in Holland until the Glorious Revolution of 1688 made his ideas safe to publish.

In 1689 he brought out the two works that remade modern thought. The Essay Concerning Human Understanding argued that the mind begins as a blank sheet and that all our ideas are drawn from experience, founding the empiricist tradition against the doctrine of innate ideas associated with Descartes.

The Two Treatises of Government demolished the divine right of kings and held instead that legitimate authority rests on the consent of the governed, exists only to protect the natural rights of life, liberty, and property, and may rightly be overthrown when it betrays that trust. His Letter Concerning Toleration made a parallel case for freedom of conscience.

Locke's empiricism passed to Hume, and his politics to Voltaire and the philosophers of the Enlightenment; above all his language of natural rights and consent flowed directly into Jefferson's Declaration of Independence, making him a father of the modern liberal state. His ideas would be refined by Montesquieu into a theory of balanced government.
