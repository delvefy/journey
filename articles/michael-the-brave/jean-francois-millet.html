Jean-François Millet

French painter of peasant life, 1814–1875

Jean-François Millet was born in 1814 to a comfortable farming family at Gruchy in Normandy, and the rhythms of country labour stayed with him for life. He studied first at Cherbourg and then in Paris under Paul Delaroche, but he found the Salon's appetite for mythology and history alien to him. After early years scraping a living with pastels and portrait commissions, he resolved to paint the people he had grown up among: the sowers, the gleaners, the shepherds.

In 1849 he settled at Barbizon on the edge of the Forest of Fontainebleau, where Théodore Rousseau and a small colony of landscape painters had gathered. There Millet produced the canvases that fixed his reputation. The Sower of 1850, a dark labouring figure striding across a hillside at dusk, struck Salon viewers as both heroic and unsettling. The Gleaners of 1857 set three stooped women picking the leavings of a harvest field and was read by critics as a quiet indictment of rural poverty. The Angelus of 1859, two peasants pausing in their potato field at the sound of the evening bell, became one of the most reproduced images of the century.

Millet insisted he was not a political painter, only a faithful one, but conservatives saw socialism in his subjects all the same. By the 1860s acclaim and a measure of prosperity had reached him, and the State began commissioning his work. He died at Barbizon in 1875.

His dignifying of peasant life and the broad weight of his draughtsmanship made him an idol of the next generation, and no painter was more devoted to copying and translating his compositions than the young Dutchman Vincent van Gogh.
