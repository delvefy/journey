Pliny the Younger

Roman lawyer and letter-writer, c. 61–c. 113 AD

Born at Como and orphaned young, Pliny was adopted by his celebrated uncle and educated in Rome under the great teacher of rhetoric Quintilian. He became one of the leading advocates of his day, especially in cases of inheritance, and climbed steadily through the ranks of Roman office under the Flavians and Trajan—quaestor, tribune, praetor, and finally consul in the year 100. A rich and conscientious man, he was a generous benefactor to his native town and a patron of writers, among them the poet Martial.

His lasting fame rests on his Letters, nine books of polished correspondence that paint an unrivalled portrait of the life, friendships, and concerns of the Roman governing class. Two of them, addressed to his friend the historian Tacitus, give the only eyewitness description of the eruption of Vesuvius that killed his uncle, recounting the terror of that day with extraordinary vividness.

Late in life Trajan sent him to govern the troubled province of Bithynia, and the official letters he exchanged with the emperor form a tenth book of his collection. Among them is the famous exchange on what to do about the Christians, in which Pliny describes their worship and Trajan lays down that they were not to be hunted out but punished only if formally accused—the earliest such record from a Roman official. With his published panegyric of Trajan and his letters, Pliny left posterity a uniquely intimate window onto his age.
