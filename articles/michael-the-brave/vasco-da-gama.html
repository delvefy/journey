Vasco da Gama

Portuguese navigator, opener of the sea route to India, c. 1460–1524

Vasco da Gama was born around 1460 at Sines on the Alentejan coast, the third son of Estêvão da Gama, a minor nobleman who served as alcaide of the local castle. He grew up in a kingdom that had already spent half a century pushing along the African coast, and he was probably trained as a sailor in his teens. His first documented service was at twenty-seven, when King John II sent him to seize French ships in retaliation for piracies in the Algarve. The king died before the long-projected voyage to India could be entrusted to him; his successor Manuel I made the appointment in 1497.

The fleet of four ships sailed from Lisbon on 8 July 1497. Bartolomeu Dias accompanied them as far as the Cape Verdes; from there they made a great westward sweep across the South Atlantic, the volta do mar, and at last rounded the Cape of Good Hope in November. Up the eastern African coast at Mozambique, Mombasa and Malindi they took on Muslim and Indian pilots; with their help they crossed the Arabian Sea in a single twenty-three-day run to Calicut, anchoring on 20 May 1498. The Zamorin of Calicut received them grudgingly, their cheap presents were laughed at, and their attempts to trade for pepper went badly; but they brought home enough spice to repay the cost of the voyage sixtyfold.

Da Gama returned to Lisbon in August or September 1499, having lost two of his four ships and most of his crew, including his brother Paulo, to scurvy. Manuel I made him admiral of the Indies and count of Vidigueira. His second voyage in 1502–1503, with twenty heavily armed ships, was punitive: he bombarded Calicut and burned a pilgrim ship returning from Mecca with all on board. Twenty years of retirement followed at Évora and Vidigueira. In 1524, ageing and irritable, he was sent out a third time as Viceroy to reform the Estado da Índia; he died at Cochin on Christmas Eve, three months after arriving.
