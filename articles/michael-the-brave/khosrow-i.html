Khosrow I

Sasanian King of Kings, r. 531–579

Khosrow I, surnamed Anushirvan and remembered ever after as “the Just,” succeeded his father Kavad I to the Persian throne in 531 and reigned for nearly half a century as the greatest of the Sasanian monarchs. He came to power amid the upheaval of the Mazdakite movement, an egalitarian religious uprising that had threatened the nobility; Khosrow crushed it and used the restored order to remake the state, reforming taxation onto a fixed and predictable basis, reorganizing the army, and strengthening the class of landed gentry who served as its cavalry.

His long reign was bound up with the rivalry of Byzantium. He concluded the so-called Eternal Peace with the Emperor Justinian in 532, broke it to sack the great city of Antioch in 540, and fought a series of campaigns — against the armies of Belisarius among others — before a fifty-year truce was sealed in 562. To his subjects, however, he was above all the pattern of the just king, a memory that endured deep into Islamic times.

Khosrow made his court a beacon of learning. He welcomed the Greek philosophers who scattered when Justinian closed the Academy of Athens, and he gathered scholars who translated works of medicine, philosophy and statecraft from Greek, Syriac and Sanskrit; from India came the fables that became Kalila wa Dimna and the game of chess. The academy at Gondishapur flourished under his patronage. He died in 579, leaving a realm at its height, which his grandson Khosrow II would lead to its last great wars.
