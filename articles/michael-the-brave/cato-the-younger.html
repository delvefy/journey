Cato the Younger

Roman Stoic and defender of the Republic, 95–46 BC

Marcus Porcius Cato, great-grandson of the famous censor, became in his own age the living emblem of old Roman virtue. A devoted Stoic from youth, he was renowned for an incorruptibility so absolute that men swore by it; he refused bribes, kept the public accounts with ferocious honesty, and made a creed of his stubbornness. In the senatorial debate over the Catilinarian conspirators in 63 BC he spoke against the leniency urged by Julius Caesar and carried the Senate to vote their death.

From then on he was the most implacable of Caesar's opponents. He fought the ambitions of the First Triumvirate of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus, and resisted every step toward one-man rule. When civil war finally broke out in 49 BC, Cato, though he had never trusted Pompey, threw in his lot with him and the Senate as the lesser danger to liberty.

The cause failed. After Pompey's defeat and death and the rout of the republicans at Thapsus, Cato held the city of Utica in Africa. Refusing to live under Caesar's mastery or to accept the humiliation of his pardon, he took his own life there in 46 BC, reportedly after a night reading Plato on the immortality of the soul. The manner of his death made him a martyr of the free Republic. His daughter Porcia married Brutus, and through the writings of Cicero, Seneca, Lucan, and many after them, Cato passed into history as the very model of principled resistance to tyranny.
