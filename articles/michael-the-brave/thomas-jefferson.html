Thomas Jefferson

Third President of the United States, author of the Declaration of Independence, 1743–1826

A Virginia lawyer, planter, and scholar of wide-ranging curiosity, Jefferson was chosen at thirty-three to draft the Declaration of Independence, giving the American Revolution its most enduring words on liberty and the equality of men, even as he himself held many people in slavery. Steeped in the thought of the Enlightenment, he drew on Locke and others to frame the colonies’ case against the British Crown in language that would echo through later struggles for freedom.

He served as wartime governor of Virginia, as minister to France on the eve of its revolution, and as the first secretary of state under President Washington, where his agrarian, states’-rights vision clashed sharply with the financial and centralising program of Alexander Hamilton. Out of this rivalry grew the first American political parties. After a term as vice president he was elected president in 1800.

As president his greatest stroke was the purchase of the vast Louisiana Territory from Napoleon’s France in 1803, which at once doubled the size of the United States and opened the continent to westward expansion; he dispatched Lewis and Clark to explore it. In retirement at his beloved Monticello he founded the University of Virginia and resumed a famous correspondence with his old friend and rival John Adams. The two men died within hours of each other, on the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration they had helped to create.
