Daniel Webster

American statesman and orator, 1782–1852

A New Hampshire farmer’s son who became the most celebrated lawyer and orator of his generation, Webster made his reputation before the Supreme Court, where in a string of landmark cases—Dartmouth College, McCulloch v. Maryland, Gibbons v. Ogden—he persuaded Chief Justice John Marshall to strengthen contracts, the national bank, and federal power over the states. His booming voice and granite presence earned him the nickname “the Godlike Daniel.”

In the Senate he became the supreme champion of the Union. Answering a states’-rights challenge in the Webster–Hayne debate of 1830, he closed with words schoolchildren would memorize for a century: “Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable.” With Clay and Calhoun he formed the Great Triumvirate that dominated the Senate, and as Secretary of State he settled a dangerous border dispute with Britain in the Webster–Ashburton Treaty.

His support for the Compromise of 1850, with its hated Fugitive Slave Law, was a last desperate effort to save the Union but cost him the friendship of the antislavery North. Like his rival Clay he longed for the presidency and never won it, and like Clay he died in 1852, as the compromises both men had built began to crumble. The jurist whose great decisions he had helped to shape was John Marshall.
