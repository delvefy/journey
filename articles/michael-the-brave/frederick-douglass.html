Frederick Douglass

American abolitionist, orator and statesman, 1818–1895

Frederick Douglass was born into slavery as Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey, about February 1818, in Talbot County, Maryland. Separated from his mother as an infant, he was sent as a boy to Baltimore, where a slaveholder’s wife began teaching him to read before being forbidden to continue; he pressed on in secret, later calling literacy the road from slavery to freedom. After a brutal year hired out to a “slave-breaker,” whom he finally fought to a standstill, he escaped north in 1838 disguised as a sailor and settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts, taking the name Douglass.

Drawn into William Lloyd Garrison’s anti-slavery movement, he became its most electrifying speaker, and his Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass (1845) — so eloquent that some doubted a former slave could have written it — made him famous on both sides of the Atlantic. Breaking with Garrison over whether the Constitution could be turned against slavery, he founded his own newspaper, The North Star, in Rochester, and argued that political action and the ballot, not moral suasion alone, would end bondage.

During the Civil War he pressed Abraham Lincoln to make emancipation a war aim and to enlist Black soldiers, recruiting for the Union army himself; two of his sons served. A lifelong champion of equal rights, he had stood at the 1848 Seneca Falls convention for women’s suffrage and never abandoned the cause. In his later years he held federal office as marshal and recorder of deeds for the District of Columbia and as United States minister to Haiti. He died in Washington on 20 February 1895, the towering conscience of nineteenth-century America.
