Cosimo de’ Medici

Florentine banker, Pater Patriae, 1389–1464

Cosimo was born at Florence on 10 April 1389, the elder son of Giovanni di Bicci de’ Medici, a banker of the Mugello who had built up a small partnership of three branches and an opening to the Roman curia. Cosimo joined the bank in his teens, attended the Council of Constance with the Roman branch, and by 1420, when his father retired, had taken over a firm whose specialty was managing the papal account and whose method was to keep its branches juridically independent so that the failure of one would not bring down the rest. By the 1430s the Medici were the richest house in Italy.

The Albizzi oligarchs of the old patrician families saw the danger. In September 1433 he was arrested on a manufactured charge and sentenced to ten years’ exile in Padua and then Venice. The exile lasted under a year. The next Signoria, drawn by the regular sortition of the bag from a list his agents had quietly stacked, recalled him in October 1434; the Albizzi went into the exile he had endured, and Cosimo returned to a Florence he would govern, without title or office, for thirty years. He kept the constitution of the republic intact and ran it from his palace on the Via Larga, through control of taxation, the borse from which magistrates were drawn, and a daily traffic of clients and friends.

It was Cosimo who paid for Filippo Brunelleschi to vault the dome of the cathedral; who took in Gemistos Plethon and the Greek embassies during the Council of Florence in 1439, settled the bills, and was set on fire for Plato; who founded the Platonic Academy at his villa at Careggi under the young Marsilio Ficino with the Greek codices he had bought through his agent Niccolò Niccoli; who built the Convent of San Marco and gave its frescoes to Fra Angelico; who commissioned Donatello’s bronze David for the courtyard of the Palazzo Medici, designed by Michelozzo. He read Cicero on duty in retirement. On his death at Careggi on 1 August 1464 the Signoria voted the title Pater Patriae on his tomb in San Lorenzo, the only such inscription Florence ever made.
