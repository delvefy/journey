Bertrand Russell

British philosopher, logician, and reformer, 1872–1970

Born into a great Whig aristocratic family—his grandfather had twice been prime minister—Russell was orphaned young and raised in a strict household, finding refuge in mathematics. At Cambridge he set out to give mathematics an unshakeable foundation in logic, and with his teacher Alfred North Whitehead produced the monumental Principia Mathematica. His discovery of a paradox at the heart of set theory and his theory of descriptions helped found the analytic tradition that would dominate twentieth-century philosophy, and he shaped the young Ludwig Wittgenstein.

Russell was also a fearless public man. His opposition to the First World War cost him his Cambridge fellowship and landed him in prison, yet he never stopped writing for a wide audience, producing the hugely popular History of Western Philosophy and provocative books on marriage, education, and religion. In 1950 he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature.

In old age he became the world’s most famous campaigner against nuclear weapons. In 1955 he joined Albert Einstein in issuing the Russell–Einstein Manifesto, which warned that humanity must choose between renouncing war and facing extinction, and it gave rise to the Pugwash movement of scientists. Still protesting—against the Vietnam War—into his late nineties, he died in 1970, perhaps the most influential British thinker of his century.
