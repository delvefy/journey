Thutmose III

Pharaoh of Egypt, r. c. 1479–1425 BC

Thutmose III, often called the Napoleon of Egypt, was the greatest warrior-king of the Eighteenth Dynasty and the architect of Egypt’s largest empire. He came to the throne as a small child, and for some two decades real power was held by his formidable stepmother Hatshepsut, who ruled first as regent and then as pharaoh in her own right. Only after her death did Thutmose at last govern alone, and late in his reign the names and images of Hatshepsut were chiselled from many monuments — whether from old resentment or dynastic calculation has long been debated.

Freed to act, he proved a soldier of genius. In some seventeen campaigns he marched again and again into the Levant and Syria, breaking the coalitions of Canaanite princes and the power of the kingdom of Mitanni, and carrying Egypt’s frontier to the Euphrates. His first and most famous victory, at Megiddo around 1457 BC, was recorded in such detail on the walls of Karnak that it stands as the earliest battle in history of which a full account survives.

The wealth of conquered lands flowed back to Egypt, and Thutmose poured it into temples, obelisks and monuments, several of which — carried off in later ages — now stand in Rome, Istanbul, London and New York. Administrator as well as conqueror, he laid the foundations of the prosperity that his great-grandson Amenhotep III would enjoy at the empire’s zenith. He died about 1425 BC, leaving the throne to his son Amenhotep II.
