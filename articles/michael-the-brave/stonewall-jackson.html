Stonewall Jackson

Confederate general, 1824–1863

Orphaned young in the Virginia hill country, Thomas Jonathan Jackson clawed his way into West Point and fought in the Mexican War before settling into the life of an eccentric, intensely devout professor at the Virginia Military Institute, where cadets mocked his oddities. War transformed him. At the first great battle of Bull Run in 1861 his brigade held firm as others broke, and a fellow officer rallied his men by pointing to Jackson standing “like a stone wall”—a nickname that stuck to him forever.

In the spring of 1862 he conducted the Shenandoah Valley campaign, a dazzling series of marches and surprise attacks that tied down far larger Union forces and entered the textbooks as a model of mobile warfare. As Lee’s most trusted lieutenant he delivered crushing flank attacks, none more brilliant than at Chancellorsville in May 1863, where he marched his corps across the enemy’s front and rolled it up at dusk.

That triumph was his last. Returning in the darkness, he was shot by his own confused pickets; his arm was amputated, and he died of pneumonia eight days later. Lee mourned that he had lost his right arm, and many believed the Confederacy never recovered from his death. Devout, austere, and relentless, Jackson became a legend of the war. He served the Southern cause led by its president, Jefferson Davis.
