Luis de Góngora

Spanish Baroque poet, 1561–1627

Born in Córdoba and trained for the Church, Luis de Góngora y Argote became the most daring and controversial poet of the Spanish Golden Age. His early ballads and biting sonnets were widely admired, but his fame—and the storm around him—rests on the dense, ornate long poems of his maturity, the Fábula de Polifemo y Galatea and the unfinished Soledades. Packed with Latinate words, bold metaphors, and tangled syntax, they created a deliberately difficult, jewel-like style that admirers and detractors alike named culteranismo, or simply Gongorism.

His manner split literary Spain into camps and ignited a famous and ferocious feud with Francisco de Quevedo, the champion of a leaner, more pointed wit. The two poets savaged each other in verse for years; Quevedo mocked Góngora’s great nose and converso ancestry, Góngora repaid him in kind. He stood at the centre of a brilliant generation that included Lope de Vega and Cervantes, and the young Diego Velázquez painted his shrewd, weary face in a celebrated portrait of 1622.

Góngora spent his last years as a chaplain at the court in Madrid, dogged by debt and disappointment, and returned to Córdoba to die in 1627. Long dismissed as obscure, his reputation was triumphantly revived three centuries later by the poets of Spain’s “Generation of 1927,” who hailed him as a master. His difficult brilliance shaped the very idea of the Baroque.
