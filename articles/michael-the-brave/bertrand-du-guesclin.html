Bertrand du Guesclin

Constable of France, c. 1320–1380

Bertrand du Guesclin was born around 1320 near Dinan in Brittany, of obscure and minor nobility, and was by all accounts coarse and ugly and made for war. He learned his trade in the chaotic Breton war of succession, fighting as a leader of ambushes and raids rather than in the chivalric set-piece, and his loyalty to the French crown carried him upward as the Hundred Years’ War turned against France. In 1364 he won the battle of Cocherel against the Navarrese of Charles the Bad, securing the throne for the newly crowned Charles V.

His next service was to rid France of the Free Companies, the masterless bands of mercenaries ravaging the countryside. He led them over the Pyrenees into Spain to fight for Henry of Trastámara against Peter of Castile, was captured by the Black Prince at the disaster of Nájera in 1367, and was ransomed to return and help put Henry on the Castilian throne at Montiel.

In 1370 Charles V made him Constable of France, the kingdom’s chief soldier. With cold patience he avoided great pitched battles and instead retook the English strongholds one by one, wearing down the gains the enemy had made at the Treaty of Brétigny and recovering most of what France had lost. He died in 1380 while besieging a town in the southern uplands, and the grateful king had him buried among the royal tombs at Saint-Denis, an almost unheard-of honour for a Breton squire and a mark of how far his sword had carried him.

The road continues to Charles II of Navarre.
