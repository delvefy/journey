Charles VI of France

King of France, "le Bien-Aimé" and "le Fol", 1368–1422

Charles was born at the Hôtel Saint-Pol in Paris on 3 December 1368, the eldest surviving son of Charles V and Joanna of Bourbon. His father's death made him king on 16 September 1380; he was eleven. His four uncles — Louis of Anjou, John of Berry, Philip the Bold of Burgundy, and Louis of Bourbon — governed the regency, taxed the kingdom heavily, and lost the army at Roosebeke in 1382 and at Aljubarrota in 1385. Charles married Isabeau of Bavaria in 1385, and in 1388 declared himself of age, dismissed his uncles, and recalled his father's old officials — the so-called Marmousets.

On 5 August 1392, riding through the forest of Le Mans on an expedition against Brittany, the king suddenly attacked his own household, killed four knights, and had to be physically subdued. The first of forty-four documented episodes of mania and lucid intermission had begun, and Charles VI never fully ruled France again. In January 1393 the Bal des Ardents — a masque danced by the king and five courtiers in pitch-and-flax costumes — ended when a torch set them alight; four men died and Charles was saved by the Duchess of Berry. As his mind closed in and reopened, the regency reverted to his uncles. The two great factions of the realm crystallised: Burgundians around John the Fearless, Armagnacs around Charles's brother Louis, Duke of Orléans, who was murdered in the rue Vieille-du-Temple in November 1407 on John the Fearless's orders.

The civil war that followed opened France to Henry V. Agincourt in 1415 destroyed the Armagnac chivalry; the assassination of John the Fearless at the bridge of Montereau in 1419 by men of the dauphin's household drove Burgundy into the English alliance; and the Treaty of Troyes in May 1420 named Henry V regent and heir to the kingdom, married him to Charles's daughter Catherine of Valois, and disinherited the dauphin. Henry V died at Vincennes in August 1422; Charles outlived him by two months and died at the Hôtel Saint-Pol on 21 October 1422, leaving the future Henry VI as the disputed heir.
