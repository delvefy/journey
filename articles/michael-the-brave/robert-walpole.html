Robert Walpole

British statesman, regarded as the first Prime Minister, 1676–1745

Walpole was a Norfolk squire who rose through the Whig party to dominate British politics for more than twenty years. His opportunity came with the collapse of the South Sea Bubble in 1720, a financial disaster that ruined many and discredited his rivals; Walpole, who had warned against the speculation, emerged as the man trusted to restore confidence. Taking the chief financial offices of the Crown, he built an unrivalled command of patronage, the Commons, and the royal household.

Though the title did not yet formally exist, Walpole is generally counted as Britain’s first prime minister, the model for the office that followed. He governed for two reigns, serving George I and then George II, the latter’s confidence secured partly through the support of Queen Caroline. His guiding principles were peace abroad, low taxes, and stability at home, captured in his reputed motto to let sleeping dogs lie, and he made the official residence at 10 Downing Street the seat of the premiership.

His long ascendancy bred fierce opposition, from the writings of Bolingbroke to the rising young orator William Pitt, and satirists mocked his system of management and influence as corruption. War with Spain, which he had tried to avoid, finally undermined him, and in 1742 he resigned, accepting an earldom as Earl of Orford. He retired to his great house at Houghton, having shown how a skilful manager of men could wield enduring power in the new parliamentary monarchy.
