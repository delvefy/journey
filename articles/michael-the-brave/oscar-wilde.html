Oscar Wilde

Irish poet and playwright, 1854–1900

Born in Dublin and educated at Oxford under the aesthetes Ruskin and Pater, Wilde became the most brilliant talker and most quotable wit of his age, the high priest of “art for art’s sake.” Famous for his flamboyant dress and dazzling paradoxes long before he had written anything of weight, he conquered London society and lectured across America, where he reportedly declared at customs that he had nothing to declare but his genius.

His genius proved real. He wrote the chilling novel The Picture of Dorian Gray, the lush tragedy Salome, fairy tales of surprising tenderness, and a string of glittering social comedies—Lady Windermere’s Fan, An Ideal Husband, and his masterpiece The Importance of Being Earnest—whose epigrams have never lost their sparkle. At the height of his fame he was the most successful playwright in London.

Then came catastrophe. His love affair with Lord Alfred Douglas led him to sue the young man’s father for libel; the case collapsed and turned on Wilde himself, who was convicted of “gross indecency” and sentenced to two years’ hard labour. Broken in health and bankrupt, he poured his suffering into the long letter De Profundis and The Ballad of Reading Gaol. Released, he went into exile in Paris under an assumed name and died there, destitute, in 1900, aged forty-six.
