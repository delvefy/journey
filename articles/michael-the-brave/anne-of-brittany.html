Anne of Brittany

Duchess of Brittany, twice Queen of France, 1477–1514

Anne was born at the château of Nantes on 25 January 1477, elder daughter and only surviving heir of Francis II, Duke of Brittany, and Margaret of Foix. The duchy had kept its sovereignty between France and Plantagenet England for two centuries by playing one off against the other; her father’s court turned to the same diplomacy with the Habsburgs and with the Beauforts when Louis XI of France pressed for the eventual reversion. Francis II died on 9 September 1488, two months after his army was destroyed by the French at Saint-Aubin-du-Cormier; the eleven-year-old Anne was duchess in her own right, and the war of the Breton succession was on her shoulders.

In December 1490 she was married by proxy to Maximilian of Austria, then King of the Romans. The marriage was a defiance of the Treaty of Le Verger, and the regent Anne of Beaujeu of France took it as casus belli. French troops occupied most of Brittany through the winter; Rennes was besieged; in November 1491 Anne yielded, the Habsburg marriage was declared unconsummated and dissolved by the curia, and on 6 December 1491 she was married, in haste, to Charles VIII of France in the chapel of Langeais. The contract specified that if Charles died without surviving children, she would marry his successor, in order to keep Brittany attached to the crown.

That was the path. After Charles’s death in 1498 — none of their seven children had lived past three — she returned briefly to Brittany as ruling duchess, then, by the contract, married Louis XII at Nantes on 8 January 1499. The second marriage was the long one; she bore two surviving daughters, Claude (later wife of Francis I) and Renée (later Duchess of Ferrara); a series of stillborn sons broke her health. She fought a careful, lifelong battle to keep Brittany’s separate institutions: the États of Brittany, its courts, the freedom from the French gabelle. She brought to the French court a household of Breton ladies, an Italian humanist tutor for her daughters, and Jean Bourdichon, who illuminated her Grandes Heures. She died at the château of Blois on 9 January 1514 of kidney stones, thirty-six years old; her heart, by Breton custom, was returned in a gold reliquary to her parents’ tomb at Nantes.
