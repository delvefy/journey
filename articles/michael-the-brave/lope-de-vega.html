Lope de Vega

Spanish playwright and poet, 1562–1635

Félix Lope de Vega Carpio was a phenomenon of fertility unmatched in world literature. Cervantes called him a “monster of nature,” and he boasted of writing more than a thousand plays—some hundreds survive—alongside epics, ballads, sonnets, and novels. Almost single-handedly he created the Spanish national theatre, the comedia, fixing its three-act form, mingling the comic and tragic, and setting out his rule-breaking method in a witty verse manifesto, the Arte nuevo de hacer comedias.

His plays drew on history, legend, and the honour code of his society. Fuenteovejuna, in which a whole village shoulders the guilt for killing a tyrannical overlord, remains his most celebrated work; Peribáñez and El caballero de Olmedo are scarcely less admired. He gave the public stage of Madrid an endless stream of action, intrigue, and song, and made the playhouse the great popular art of the Golden Age.

His private life was as turbulent and crowded as his output—marriages, scandalous love affairs, and a late ordination to the priesthood that did not still his passions. He moved amid the literary giants and feuds of his age, rivalrous with Cervantes and entangled in the quarrels of Góngora and Quevedo. Honoured as the “Phoenix of Wits,” he died in Madrid in 1635, mourned by a whole city.
