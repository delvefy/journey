Olympias

Queen of Macedon, c. 375–316 BCE

Olympias was a princess of Epirus, the wife of Philip II of Macedon, and the mother of Alexander the Great—one of the most formidable and dangerous women of the ancient world. Born around 375 BCE into the Aeacid royal house, which claimed descent from Achilles, she was given in marriage to Philip as he built his power, and bore him the son who would conquer Asia.

At the Macedonian court she was a fierce partisan of her son's interests, locked in rivalry with Philip's other wives and devoted to the ecstatic rites of Dionysus, which gave her a reputation for both holiness and menace. When Philip took a young new bride whose children might displace Alexander, the breach grew bitter; and after Philip was assassinated in 336 BCE—a deed in which some ancient writers suspected her hand—Alexander succeeded without challenge.

While Alexander campaigned in the East she wielded great influence in Epirus and Macedon, quarrelling endlessly with the regent Antipater. After her son's death in 323 she threw herself into the wars of the successors to protect the rights of her infant grandson, Alexander IV.

In 317 she seized Macedon and put to death the rival king Philip III and many of her enemies, but the next year Cassander, Antipater's son, cornered and captured her. In 316 BCE he had her killed, and with her fall the cause of Alexander's direct line was all but lost.
