Jason of Pherae

Ruler of Thessaly, d. 370 BCE

Jason of Pherae was the most powerful Thessalian ruler of the early fourth century BCE, who for a few years welded the loose confederation of Thessaly into a single formidable state. By a mixture of force and persuasion he won the office of tagus, the federal war-leader of the Thessalians, and built a large, well-drilled army of cavalry and mercenaries.

Xenophon, our chief source, presents him as a ruler of soaring ambition. Master of the rich Thessalian plains and their famous horsemen, Jason aimed at nothing less than the leadership of Greece, and he is reported to have spoken openly of a great campaign—even of carrying war into the Persian Empire, a design that foreshadowed the later achievements of the kings of Macedon.

His power peaked in the years around the battle of Leuctra in 371, when Thebes and Sparta were exhausting one another; he intervened to mediate and was courted by all sides as the coming arbiter of Greek affairs.

But in 370 BCE, on the very threshold of these schemes, he was assassinated by a group of young men, and his ambitions died with him. His successors at Pherae, among them the cruel Alexander, proved far smaller men, and the chance of a Thessalian hegemony passed away.
