Eratosthenes

Greek polymath and chief librarian of Alexandria, c. 276–194 BC

Born at Cyrene in North Africa and educated at Athens, Eratosthenes was summoned to Alexandria by the Ptolemies to tutor the royal heir and to head the great Library, then the largest collection of knowledge in the world. He worked in almost every field—mathematics, astronomy, geography, history, poetry, and literary criticism—and his rivals nicknamed him “Beta,” the second letter, jibing that he came second in everything; admirers preferred to call him a second Plato.

His most celebrated feat was to measure the size of the Earth. Knowing that at midsummer the sun shone straight down a well at Syene while casting a measurable shadow at Alexandria to the north, he used the difference in angle and the distance between the cities to calculate the planet’s circumference—arriving at a figure astonishingly close to the true value, using nothing but geometry and careful observation. It was one of the greatest achievements of ancient science.

He did much else besides. He effectively founded the discipline of geography, coining the name and drawing a map of the known world crossed by lines of latitude and longitude. He devised a simple method, still taught as the “sieve of Eratosthenes,” for finding prime numbers, and he attempted a scientific chronology of history. A friend and correspondent of Archimedes, who dedicated a work to him, Eratosthenes embodied the confident, wide-ranging curiosity of Alexandrian learning at its height.
