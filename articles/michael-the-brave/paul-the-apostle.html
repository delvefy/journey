Paul the Apostle

Apostle to the Gentiles, c. AD 5–c. AD 67

Paul was born Saul of Tarsus, a Jew of the diaspora who held Roman citizenship and was trained as a Pharisee, by tradition under the rabbi Gamaliel. In his early life he was a zealous opponent of the followers of Jesus, and the Acts of the Apostles places him approving the stoning of the martyr Stephen. While travelling to Damascus to suppress the new movement, he experienced a vision of the risen Christ that overturned his life and turned the persecutor into the faith's greatest advocate.

As the self-described apostle to the Gentiles, Paul undertook a series of missionary journeys across Asia Minor, Greece, and the wider eastern Mediterranean, founding and nurturing communities of believers. His letters to these churches—to the Romans, Corinthians, Galatians, and others—were preserved and gathered into the New Testament, where they form its earliest writings and the foundation of much later Christian theology, above all the doctrines of grace and justification by faith.

Paul insisted that Gentile converts need not adopt the full Jewish law, a position he defended in debate with Peter and the Jerusalem leadership and which decisively shaped the church's future as a universal religion. Arrested in Jerusalem, he exercised his right as a citizen to appeal to the emperor and was sent to Rome. There, by ancient tradition, he was martyred under Nero around the mid-60s. His thought would later ignite the minds of Augustine and Martin Luther and remains among the most influential bodies of writing in the West.

The road continues to Jesus Christ.
