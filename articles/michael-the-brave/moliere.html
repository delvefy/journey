Molière

French playwright and actor, 1622–1673

Born Jean-Baptiste Poquelin, the son of a prosperous royal upholsterer, he turned his back on a comfortable trade for the precarious life of the stage. His first Paris company, the Illustre Théâtre, collapsed in debt—he was even briefly jailed—and he spent more than a decade touring the provinces, learning his craft as actor, manager, and writer of comedies.

Back in Paris, his gifts won the favour and lasting protection of Louis XIV, and under the Sun King’s patronage he created the masterpieces of French comedy. In Tartuffe he flayed religious hypocrisy so sharply that the devout had it banned; in Dom Juan, The Misanthrope, The Miser, The Bourgeois Gentleman, and Les Femmes savantes he held up to ridicule the pretensions, vices, and follies of his age with a humanity that has never dated. He collaborated with the composer Lully on lavish comedy-ballets for the court.

His success made him powerful enemies among rivals and moralists, but the public adored him. In 1673, playing the title role in his own Imaginary Invalid, he was seized by a fit of coughing on stage and died a few hours later. As an actor he was denied Christian burial until the king intervened. So completely does he embody his nation’s genius that French itself is called “the language of Molière.”
