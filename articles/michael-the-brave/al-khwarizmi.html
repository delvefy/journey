Al-Khwarizmi

Persian mathematician, c.780–850

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi was born about 780, probably in the region of Khwarazm in Central Asia, and rose to prominence in Baghdad at the House of Wisdom, the great academy of translation and research fostered by the Abbasid caliph al-Mamun. There, among scholars gathering the learning of Greece, Persia and India, he produced the works that would make him one of the most consequential mathematicians in history.

In his book on calculation “by completion and balancing” — al-jabr, the word from which algebra descends — he set out a systematic method for solving linear and quadratic equations, founding algebra as a discipline in its own right, with the moves justified in the geometric manner of Euclid. In a second treatise he explained the Hindu decimal system of nine digits and a zero, and its rules of reckoning, opening the way for these numerals to spread across the Islamic world and, in time, to Europe.

His influence is written into the language of mathematics itself: the Latin form of his name, Algoritmi, gave us the word algorithm. He also compiled astronomical tables and a geography that corrected and extended the work of Ptolemy. Translated into Latin in the twelfth century, his texts taught Christendom both algebra and the new arithmetic, and Leonardo of Pisa, called Fibonacci, would carry the Hindu-Arabic numerals into the heart of European commerce. Little is known of his life, and he died about 850, but the methods he codified underlie much of the mathematics that followed.
