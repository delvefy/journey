Gabriel García Márquez

Colombian novelist, 1927–2014

Gabriel García Márquez was born on 6 March 1927 at Aracataca, a banana-belt town in the Colombian department of Magdalena. He was raised in his earliest years by his maternal grandparents, and the grandfather — Colonel Nicolás Márquez, a Liberal veteran of the Thousand Days War — together with a household of superstitious aunts furnished the imaginative world of his later fiction. He went to law school in Bogotá in 1947 but never finished, drifting instead into journalism in Cartagena and Barranquilla, where the “Group of Barranquilla” — Álvaro Mutis, Germán Vargas, Alfonso Fuenmayor — taught him to read Joyce, Faulkner, Woolf and Kafka. Of these the decisive shock was Kafka, whose Metamorphosis he later said showed him that one could write the way his grandmother had spoken: in a tone that took the impossible for granted.

He spent the late fifties as a foreign correspondent in Rome and Paris and the early sixties in Mexico City, writing screenplays and the short books — La hojarasca, El coronel no tiene quien le escriba, La mala hora — that taught him his trade without yet revealing his subject. Then in 1965, while driving south toward Acapulco, the opening sentence of Cien años de soledad arrived. He turned the car around, locked himself in for eighteen months, and produced the novel that, on its publication at Buenos Aires in 1967, made magic realism a world literary mode and Macondo a place on every reader’s map.

The international success bought him independence; The Autumn of the Patriarch (1975) and Love in the Time of Cholera (1985) confirmed his range. He received the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1982 and used the platform to defend the Cuba of his close friend Fidel Castro and to plead the cause of Latin America at large. He lived his last forty years in Mexico City and died there of pneumonia on 17 April 2014.
