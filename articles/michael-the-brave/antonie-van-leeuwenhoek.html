Antonie van Leeuwenhoek

Dutch microscopist, “Father of Microbiology,” 1632–1723

A draper and minor official of Delft with no university training, van Leeuwenhoek became, through sheer curiosity and skill, one of the great pioneers of science. He taught himself to grind tiny, jewel-like glass lenses of extraordinary quality, mounting each in a small brass plate to make simple microscopes that magnified far more powerfully than any in the world. His grinding technique he guarded jealously, and it died with him.

Peering through these instruments at pond water, rainwater, scrapings from his own teeth, and countless other samples, he became the first human being to see a hidden living world. He described swarming “animalcules”—the bacteria and protozoa invisible to the naked eye—as well as red blood cells, spermatozoa, muscle fibres, and the flow of blood through capillaries. For half a century he reported these wonders in a stream of letters to the Royal Society in London.

At first his fantastic claims met disbelief, until Robert Hooke and others reproduced his observations and confirmed them, after which he was elected a Fellow. A fellow citizen of the painter Johannes Vermeer, whose estate he administered, van Leeuwenhoek lived to ninety, observing to the very end. By revealing the microscopic realm he founded the science of microbiology and changed forever our picture of life.

The road continues to Robert Hooke.
