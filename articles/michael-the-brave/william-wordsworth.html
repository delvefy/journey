William Wordsworth

1770–1850, English poet, Poet Laureate

William Wordsworth was born at Cockermouth in Cumberland, the second of five children of an attorney who managed estates for the local magnate. His mother died when he was seven, his father five years later, and he was sent to grammar school at Hawkshead in the lakes. He read mathematics indifferently at St John's College, Cambridge, took his degree in 1791, and that summer crossed the Channel into a France already in revolution. He spent the next year there, fell in love with Annette Vallon, fathered a daughter, and only the war with Britain forced him home.

A small legacy in 1795 freed him for poetry. With his sister Dorothy he settled at Racedown and then at Alfoxden in Somerset, where he met Samuel Taylor Coleridge in 1797. Their daily walks and conversations produced the Lyrical Ballads of 1798, with Coleridge's “Rime of the Ancient Mariner” opening the volume and Wordsworth's “Tintern Abbey” closing it. The second edition of 1800 carried a preface in which Wordsworth defined poetry as “the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings” and demanded a language “really used by men.”

From 1799 he lived at Dove Cottage in Grasmere with Dorothy and, from 1802, with his wife Mary Hutchinson. There he wrote the great body of his verse: the autobiographical Prelude (in progress until his death), the Immortality Ode, the Lucy poems, “Resolution and Independence,” many of the sonnets, and the long Excursion. The young John Keats, John Stuart Mill, Thomas De Quincey and Charles Lamb all sought him out at the cottage.

His politics turned conservative after 1812 and he accepted the office of distributor of stamps for Westmorland. Younger Romantics like Byron and Shelley mocked the apostasy, but Wordsworth wrote on. He was made Poet Laureate in 1843 and died at Rydal Mount in April 1850. The Prelude appeared posthumously that year, fixing his stature as the central poet of English Romanticism.
