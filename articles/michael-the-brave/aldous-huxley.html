Aldous Huxley

English writer, author of Brave New World, 1894–1963

Grandson of the biologist Thomas Henry Huxley and brother of the scientist Julian Huxley, Aldous Huxley was born into one of Britain’s great intellectual families. An illness in adolescence left him nearly blind and closed off the scientific career he had planned, turning him instead to literature. After Eton and Oxford he won fame in the 1920s with witty, disenchanted satires of fashionable society such as Crome Yellow and Point Counter Point.

His enduring work is Brave New World (1932), a chilling vision of a future tamed by mass production, genetic engineering, casual pleasure, and the drug soma—a society that worships “Our Ford” and has abolished freedom in the name of stability. It became one of the defining dystopias of the century, forever paired with the work of his one-time pupil at Eton, George Orwell.

In 1937 Huxley settled in California, where his restless mind turned toward mysticism, pacifism, and the expansion of consciousness. He wrote on the “perennial philosophy” underlying the world’s religions and, after experimenting with mescaline, described the experience in The Doors of Perception. A friend of pacifist intellectuals including Bertrand Russell, he died in Los Angeles on 22 November 1963, the same day as the assassination of President Kennedy.
