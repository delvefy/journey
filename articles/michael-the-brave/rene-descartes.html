René Descartes

French philosopher and mathematician, 1596–1650

Descartes was born in France and educated by the Jesuits at La Flèche, where he absorbed the scholastic learning he would later strive to replace. After a period as a gentleman soldier in the opening campaigns of the Thirty Years’ War, he resolved to rebuild human knowledge on secure foundations. For most of his productive life he lived in the Dutch Republic, working in relative seclusion on mathematics, optics, physiology, and philosophy.

His method was to doubt everything that could be doubted until he reached a truth that could not: the certainty of his own thinking, summed up in the formula “I think, therefore I am.” From this single point he tried to reconstruct knowledge of God, the world, and the mind, arguing for a sharp distinction between thinking mind and extended matter that became known as Cartesian dualism. The 1637 Discourse on the Method and the 1641 Meditations set the agenda for much of modern philosophy.

He was also a mathematician of the first rank. In an appendix to the Discourse he founded analytic geometry, joining algebra to geometry through a coordinate system that still bears his name, and he advanced a mechanistic picture of nature governed by matter in motion. Wary after the Church’s condemnation of Galileo, he withheld his own cosmological treatise rather than provoke controversy. Summoned to Stockholm to tutor Queen Christina, he was unused to its harsh winter mornings and died there of pneumonia in 1650, leaving a body of thought that shaped the rationalist tradition for generations.
