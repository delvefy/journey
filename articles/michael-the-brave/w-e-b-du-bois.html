W. E. B. Du Bois

American sociologist and civil-rights leader, 1868–1963

William Edward Burghardt Du Bois was born on 23 February 1868 in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, into a small, long-free Black community. A brilliant student, he studied at Fisk University in Tennessee, where he first saw the segregated South, then at Harvard, where in 1895 he became the first African American to earn a doctorate, and at the University of Berlin. His The Philadelphia Negro (1899) was among the first rigorous sociological studies of a Black community in America, treating racial inequality as a subject for empirical inquiry rather than assumption.

In The Souls of Black Folk (1903) he declared that “the problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color line” and openly challenged Booker T. Washington’s policy of accommodation, insisting on full civil and political rights and on the cultivation of a “talented tenth” of educated leaders. He helped launch the Niagara Movement and, in 1909, co-founded the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, editing its influential magazine The Crisis for nearly a quarter of a century and making it the voice of a generation.

Du Bois also organized a series of Pan-African Congresses, binding the struggle in America to the fate of colonized Africa, and clashed bitterly with Marcus Garvey over strategy and temperament. Increasingly radical and disillusioned with American capitalism, he embraced socialism, joined the Communist Party late in life, and accepted Kwame Nkrumah’s invitation to settle in newly independent Ghana, where he took citizenship and died in Accra on 27 August 1963 — on the eve of the March on Washington, where his passing was announced to the crowd. His scholarship and his insistence on dignity shaped the civil-rights century that followed.
