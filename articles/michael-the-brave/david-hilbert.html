David Hilbert

German mathematician, 1862–1943

David Hilbert was born in 1862 at Königsberg in East Prussia, the city of Kant and of the seven famous bridges. He studied at the Königsberg university with Hermann Minkowski as his close friend and Adolf Hurwitz as his teacher, and in 1895 he was called to Göttingen, where Felix Klein had begun rebuilding the mathematics institute that Gauss and Riemann had once led. He spent the rest of his career there, and made it again the centre of world mathematics.

His early work settled long-standing problems in invariant theory and algebraic number theory. His Foundations of Geometry of 1899 rebuilt Euclid on a rigorous axiomatic basis, and announced the formalist program that would become his life's argument: that mathematics should be founded on a complete, consistent, mechanically verifiable system of axioms. At the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900 he proposed twenty-three problems whose pursuit shaped the twentieth century. He gave Hilbert spaces to functional analysis; in 1915, working in parallel with Einstein, he derived the field equations of general relativity from a variational principle.

Around him Göttingen grew into the strongest school in mathematics ever assembled, with Hermann Weyl, Emmy Noether, Richard Courant, and a stream of visitors that included John von Neumann, Norbert Wiener, and the young Kurt Gödel. He coined the famous defiance, against the metaphysical "we shall not know," with which he closed his retirement address in 1930: "Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen.—We must know. We shall know." Days later Gödel, in Vienna, announced the incompleteness theorems that showed his formalist program was, in its strict form, impossible.

The Nazi seizure of power in 1933 scattered the Göttingen school. Asked by the new education minister whether mathematics at Göttingen had really suffered now that the Jews were gone, Hilbert replied: "Suffered? It does not exist any more." He died in 1943, almost alone. Of his many great pupils and visitors, the Hungarian polymath who carried Hilbert's formalist axiomatic spirit furthest—into quantum mechanics, game theory, computing architecture, and the hydrogen bomb—was John von Neumann.
