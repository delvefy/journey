Marcus Aurelius

Roman emperor and Stoic philosopher, 121–180 AD

Marcus Aurelius was born into a distinguished Roman family and marked out for greatness while still a boy, when the emperor Hadrian, charmed by his seriousness, nicknamed him Verissimus, "the most truthful." Hadrian arranged the line of succession with care, adopting Antoninus Pius on the condition that he in turn adopt Marcus. In 161 Marcus came to the throne, sharing power at first with his adoptive brother Lucius Verus in Rome's first joint emperorship.

His reign, long remembered as the close of a golden age, was in fact an almost unbroken succession of trials. War flared with Parthia in the east; returning armies carried home the devastating Antonine Plague; and above all the northern frontier erupted, drawing Marcus into the grinding Marcomannic Wars against the Germanic tribes of the Danube, where he spent much of his life in camp. Through it all he governed with a conscientious devotion to duty that became legendary.

Amid these campaigns he kept a private notebook of Stoic reflections, written in Greek and addressed only to himself. Published long after as the Meditations, it became one of the best-loved works of philosophy ever written, a moving record of a powerful man schooling himself in humility, endurance, and the acceptance of fate. Yet his judgement failed him in one fateful choice: breaking with the custom of adopting the worthiest heir, he left the empire to his own son Commodus. Marcus died on the frontier in 180, and with him the long peace of the Antonines came to an end.
