John Balliol

King of Scots, c. 1249–1314

John Balliol was born about 1249, heir to one of the wealthiest baronial houses of the age, with lands spread across Scotland, northern England and Picardy; the family had earlier endowed the Oxford college that still bears its name. He was thrust into history by an accident of succession. When the Maid of Norway died in 1290 the Scottish throne stood empty, and a crowd of claimants pressed forward in the dispute known as the Great Cause. The Scots invited Edward I of England to arbitrate, and in 1292, weighing thirteen competitors, he awarded the crown to Balliol, whose descent ran through the eldest daughter of David, Earl of Huntingdon.

The price of Edward’s judgement was submission. Edward insisted on his overlordship, summoned the new king to answer appeals at Westminster and demanded Scottish service in his French wars. Goaded beyond endurance, the Scottish magnates took government into a council of twelve and in 1295 concluded a treaty of mutual aid with Philip IV of France—the beginning of the long Auld Alliance.

Edward’s answer was war. In 1296 he stormed Berwick, crushed the Scots at Dunbar and forced Balliol to abdicate, stripping the royal arms from his surcoat in a humiliation that earned him the nickname Toom Tabard, the empty coat. Imprisoned first in the Tower of London and then released into papal keeping, he withdrew to his family estates in France and lived out his days in obscurity, dying around 1314. The rising waged in his name by William Wallace, and the rival kingship later seized by Robert the Bruce, would prove far more famous than the hollow reign of the king himself.
