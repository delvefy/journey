Gaius Cassius Longinus

Roman general and chief conspirator against Caesar, c. 86–42 BC

Cassius first won note as a young officer in the catastrophe of Carrhae in 53 BC, serving as quaestor to Crassus against the Parthians. When the army was destroyed and Crassus killed, Cassius led the survivors to safety, and two years later he defended the province of Syria with skill, throwing back a Parthian invasion. In the civil war he took Pompey's side and commanded a fleet against Caesar, but after Pharsalus he accepted Caesar's pardon. A convinced Epicurean, he scorned superstition and argued philosophy in his letters to Cicero.

It was Cassius, by the ancient accounts, who was the true instigator of the plot against Caesar. Sharper and more practical than the high-minded Brutus, whose half-sister he had married, he gathered the conspirators and pressed the reluctant Brutus to lend the enterprise his honoured name. Caesar himself is said to have distrusted the lean and watchful Cassius. On the Ides of March, 44 BC, the two led the senators who struck the dictator down.

Driven from Rome, Cassius raised armies and treasure across the eastern provinces, and with Brutus he commanded the last forces of the Republic. At Philippi in 42 BC his wing was broken by Mark Antony, and, wrongly believing that Brutus too had been defeated, he ordered a freedman to kill him. Brutus, finding his body, mourned him as "the last of the Romans." With his death the cause of the old Republic was all but lost.
