H. G. Wells

1866–1946, English novelist, historian, and prophet of the future

Herbert George Wells was born at Bromley in Kent above his father's small china shop. A broken leg in childhood gave him long hours with books from the local library; the failure of the shop sent him at fourteen into apprenticeships as a draper and a chemist's assistant, the soul-grinding shop work he later turned into the comic novel Kipps. A scholarship in 1884 carried him to the Normal School of Science in South Kensington, where he sat in T. H. Huxley's biology class — the formative intellectual encounter of his life — and worked his way through Darwin, Lyell and Spencer.

He taught, took a London science degree, and from 1894 turned full-time to writing. The Time Machine appeared in 1895, followed in rapid sequence by The Island of Doctor Moreau, The Invisible Man, The War of the Worlds and The First Men in the Moon. With those few books he effectively invented the modern scientific romance: time travel, alien invasion, biological engineering, sound-and-light futures. He then turned to social comedy in Love and Mr Lewisham, Kipps and The History of Mr Polly, and to programmatic utopias in A Modern Utopia and Anticipations.

A long, untidy private life of liaisons accompanied a long public one. He joined the Fabian Society, quarrelled with George Bernard Shaw and the Webbs and walked out in 1908, served as a propagandist of the Allies in 1914 and the inventor of the phrase "the war that will end war," interviewed Lenin in 1920 and called him "the dreamer in the Kremlin," interviewed Stalin in 1934, and pressed for a world state through the League and after it. His Outline of History of 1920 sold over two million copies and made him for a generation the popular historian of the species. He died in London in August 1946, three months after the publication of his last book, the despairing Mind at the End of Its Tether.
