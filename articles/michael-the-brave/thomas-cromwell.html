Thomas Cromwell

Earl of Essex, chief minister of Henry VIII, c.1485–1540

Cromwell was born about 1485 at Putney, the son of Walter Cromwell, a blacksmith, brewer, and small landlord whose violence drove him as a youth across the Channel. He served as a mercenary in Italy at the battle of the Garigliano in 1503, worked in Florentine and Venetian counting-houses, and learned the law in the Netherlands and at Gray's Inn. By the early 1520s he was a successful London solicitor, a member of the Inner Temple, and the right hand of Cardinal Wolsey, for whom he managed the suppression of small monasteries to fund Wolsey's twin colleges at Ipswich and Oxford.

Wolsey's disgrace in 1529 could have ended him; instead Cromwell took a seat in the Reformation Parliament and within four years was Henry VIII's principal secretary. He drafted and steered through the legislation that broke the English church from Rome — the Act in Restraint of Appeals of 1533, the Act of Supremacy and the Treason Act of 1534 — and ran the new royal supremacy as the king's vicegerent in spirituals. He carried out the Valor Ecclesiasticus and dissolved first the lesser and then the greater monasteries between 1536 and 1540, redirecting an immense flow of land and rents to the Crown and to a new gentry class. He sponsored the printing of an English Bible, suppressed the Pilgrimage of Grace in 1536, and managed the falls of Anne Boleyn and of his old opponents the Poles.

His political base was always Henry's favour, and the king turned. The marriage to Anne of Cleves which Cromwell engineered in January 1540 proved a disaster, his religious conservatism's enemies — Norfolk, Gardiner — moved against him, and on 10 June 1540 he was arrested at the council table. He was attainted without trial, beheaded clumsily on Tower Hill on 28 July 1540, the same day Henry married Catherine Howard.

The road continues to Cardinal Wolsey.
