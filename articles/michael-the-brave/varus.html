Varus

Roman general, lost three legions at the Teutoburg Forest, c. 46 BC–AD 9

Publius Quinctilius Varus came from an old but impoverished patrician family and owed his rise to close ties with the household of Augustus, marrying into the imperial family and holding the consulship in 13 BC alongside Tiberius. As governor of Syria around the end of the first century BC, he commanded the eastern legions and, on the death of Herod the Great, marched into Judea to crush the revolts that broke out, suppressing them with such severity—crucifying some two thousand—that the episode was long remembered as the War of Varus.

Trusted and well connected, Varus was later given charge of Germania, the territory Rome was attempting to organise into a province east of the Rhine. There he governed as though the region were already pacified, imposing Roman taxation and law and holding court in the Roman manner, confident in the loyalty of the local chieftains who attended him.

That confidence was fatal. In AD 9 the Cheruscan noble Arminius, whom Varus regarded as a friend, drew the three legions into the trackless Teutoburg Forest with reports of a false uprising and ambushed them. Over several days of slaughter in rain and forest the entire army—legions XVII, XVIII, and XIX—was destroyed, and Varus fell on his sword. The disaster fixed the Rhine as Rome's permanent frontier and so shook Augustus that he was said to roam the palace crying out for Varus to give him back his legions. His name endured as the very emblem of catastrophe and misplaced trust.
