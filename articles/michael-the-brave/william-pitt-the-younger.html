William Pitt the Younger

British prime minister, 1759–1806

The second son of the great war minister William Pitt the Elder, the younger Pitt was a political prodigy who entered the House of Commons in his early twenties and, in 1783, became prime minister at the age of just twenty-four, the youngest in British history. Cool, austere, and a master of finance and debate, he set about restoring a nation shaken by the loss of its American colonies.

In his early ministry he repaired the public finances with a sinking fund and reforming, free-trading measures drawn from the new economics of Adam Smith, reorganised the government of India, and enjoyed the steady confidence of King George III. For two decades he dominated Parliament, his ascendancy challenged above all by the brilliant Whig orator Charles James Fox.

The French Revolution and the long war against France defined his later years. Pitt organised and bankrolled the European coalitions against the Republic and then Napoleon, suspended habeas corpus to suppress domestic radicalism, and pushed through the Union of Great Britain and Ireland in 1801, only to resign when the king refused to allow Catholic emancipation. He returned to office but was worn down by the strain, and died in 1806, soon after Napoleon’s victory at Austerlitz had wrecked his latest coalition, having lent his support to his friend Wilberforce’s campaign against the slave trade.
