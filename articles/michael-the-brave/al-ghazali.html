Al-Ghazali

Persian theologian and mystic, 1058–1111

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali was born about 1058 at Tus in the Persian province of Khorasan. His gifts brought him to the notice of Nizam al-Mulk, the formidable vizier of the Seljuk empire, who in 1091 installed him as head professor at the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad — at little more than thirty, he held one of the most coveted chairs in all of Islam, lecturing to hundreds and advising on the great questions of the day.

From this height he launched his assault on the philosophers. In The Incoherence of the Philosophers he turned their own logic against them, arguing that the Aristotelian system handed down by Avicenna failed to demonstrate its grandest claims, and condemning as unbelief the doctrines of an eternal world and a God ignorant of particulars. His analysis of causation — that what we call cause and effect is but God’s habitual ordering — was among the most penetrating in medieval thought.

Then, at the peak of his fame, he was seized by a spiritual crisis. Doubting that argument could yield certainty, he abandoned his post and his wealth and wandered for years as an ascetic through Damascus, Jerusalem and Mecca. Out of that ordeal came The Revival of the Religious Sciences, which wove the disciplines of law and theology together with the inner life of Sufism, winning mysticism a lasting place within Sunni orthodoxy, and the candid autobiography Deliverance from Error. Honoured as the Proof of Islam, he died at Tus in 1111. A century later Averroes would rise to answer him, but al-Ghazali had already reshaped the faith from within.
