Cyrus the Younger

Persian prince, c. 423–401 BCE

Cyrus the Younger was a Persian prince whose bid for his brother's throne gave rise to one of antiquity's most famous adventures, the march of the Ten Thousand. The younger son of King Darius II and the formidable queen Parysatis, he was sent while still young to govern the western provinces of Asia Minor.

There he proved an energetic and ambitious satrap. During the closing years of the Peloponnesian War he threw Persian money decisively behind Sparta, funding the fleets with which the admiral Lysander wore down and at last defeated Athens. His mother's favorite, he chafed under the rule of his elder brother, who had become king as Artaxerxes II.

In 401 BCE Cyrus gathered a great army, its core a force of some ten thousand Greek mercenaries, and marched east under a lesser pretext, revealing his true aim—the throne itself—only deep into the campaign. At Cunaxa, near Babylon, his army met the king's. The Greeks swept aside the troops before them, but Cyrus, seeing his brother within reach, charged recklessly at him and was killed.

His death left the victorious Greek mercenaries stranded in the heart of the Persian Empire, and their long retreat to the sea, recorded by Xenophon, who marched among them, became the Anabasis. Cyrus himself was remembered as a gifted and generous prince undone by his own daring.
