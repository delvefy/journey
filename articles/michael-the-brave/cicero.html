Cicero

Roman orator and statesman, 106–43 BC

Marcus Tullius Cicero was born at Arpinum in 106 BC, a man of equestrian family and no ancestral office—a “new man” who rose by sheer brilliance of tongue. After studying rhetoric and philosophy in Rome and Greece, he climbed the ladder of magistracies and reached the consulship in 63 BC, the first of his line to do so. In that year he exposed and crushed the conspiracy of Catiline, denouncing it in a series of speeches that became models of Latin prose; the summary execution of the conspirators, however, would later be turned against him, and he was briefly driven into exile.

Cicero stood always for the old Republic and the authority of the Senate, a stance that left him stranded as the great soldiers tore the state apart. He admired and feared Julius Caesar, who pardoned him after the civil war but whose dominance he could not accept. When Caesar was murdered in 44 BC, Cicero hoped the Republic might be restored and threw his eloquence against Mark Antony in the blistering speeches he called the Philippics. It was a fatal miscalculation: when Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus formed their triumvirate, Antony demanded his head. Cicero was hunted down and killed in 43 BC, his hands and head displayed on the Rostra where he had so often spoken.

His true monument is his writing. His letters, speeches, and philosophical works—on duty, on the state, on the nature of the gods—carried Greek thought into Latin and shaped European prose, rhetoric, and political ideals for two thousand years.
