Thomas Henry Huxley

English biologist, “Darwin’s bulldog,” 1825–1895

Largely self-taught and risen from modest circumstances, Huxley made his name as a comparative anatomist after a long voyage as assistant surgeon aboard HMS Rattlesnake, during which he sent home pioneering studies of marine invertebrates. Sharp-tongued, fearless in argument, and a brilliant lecturer, he became the most formidable scientific controversialist of Victorian Britain.

When Darwin published his theory, Huxley embraced it as a weapon against dogma and entrenched authority, declaring himself ready to go to the stake for it and earning the nickname “Darwin’s bulldog.” At the famous Oxford meeting of 1860 he clashed with Bishop Samuel Wilberforce, retorting that he would rather have an ape for an ancestor than a man who used his gifts to obscure the truth. In Evidence as to Man’s Place in Nature he argued directly for human kinship with the apes, and he coined the word “agnostic” to describe his own refusal of certainty in matters of faith.

Beyond the evolution wars, Huxley laboured to make science a respected profession and a part of ordinary education, sitting on royal commissions and reforming the teaching of biology. He founded a remarkable dynasty: his grandsons included the biologist Julian Huxley and the novelist Aldous Huxley. When he died in 1895 he had done as much as any man to win public acceptance for the scientific view of nature.
