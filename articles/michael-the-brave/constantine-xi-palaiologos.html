Constantine XI Palaiologos

Last Byzantine Emperor, 1405–1453

Constantine was born at the Blachernae palace on 8 February 1405, the fourth son of Manuel II Palaiologos and the Serbian Helena Dragaš. He grew up in a court whose treasury could no longer pay for the wax of its own candles, and whose territory by his twenties had shrunk to the capital, a few Aegean islands, and the Morea — the southern third of the Peloponnese held by his Palaiologan brothers as despots from Mistra. He governed there from 1428, retook Patras from the Latins in 1430, and led the small Byzantine effort to recover central Greece from the Latin Duchy of Athens. He married twice, both wives dying young in childbirth; he was about to marry the widow of the sultan of Trebizond when Constantinople fell.

On the death of his elder brother John VIII Palaiologos in October 1448, Constantine was elected emperor by the Mistra court and proclaimed in absence; he was crowned not in Hagia Sophia, but in the cathedral of Mistra, on 6 January 1449. The capital met him with cold respect: he was the emperor of a city of perhaps fifty thousand souls inside walls built for half a million, the ratifier of the Union of Florence which most of his clergy and people regarded as apostasy. The accession of Mehmed II in 1451 brought the threat his brother had survived to the head; the building of Rumeli Hisarı in 1452 closed the Bosphorus, and the Union liturgy celebrated in Hagia Sophia on 12 December under the legate Isidore of Kiev was greeted by the populace as further evidence that the city would die unconfessed.

The siege began on 6 April 1453. With perhaps seven thousand effective defenders — the four hundred and seventy professional troops of the Genoese condottiere Giovanni Giustiniani Longo among them — Constantine held twelve miles of land and sea wall against ninety thousand Ottomans and the great bombards of Orban for seven weeks. On the night of 28–29 May, the last Christian liturgy was sung in Hagia Sophia by Greeks and Latins together; the emperor took communion, then rode out to the Saint Romanus gate. When the wall was breached at dawn, Giustiniani was carried wounded to a ship in the harbour and the line dissolved. Constantine, said the chroniclers, stripped off the purple, drew his sword, and was last seen fighting on foot in the press at the broken wall. His body was never identified.

The road continues to John VIII Palaiologos.
