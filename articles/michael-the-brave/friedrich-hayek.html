Friedrich Hayek

Austrian-British economist and social theorist, 1899–1992

Friedrich August von Hayek was born in Vienna in 1899 into the lesser Austrian nobility, into a family of biologists and civil servants. He served as an artillery officer on the Italian front in the last year of the First World War and returned to a Vienna whose collapsed empire, sliding currency, and competing socialisms became the school in which he formed his thinking. He took doctorates in law and political science at the University of Vienna and entered the seminar of Ludwig von Mises, the foreman of the Austrian school of economics.

In 1931, at Lionel Robbins's invitation, Hayek joined the London School of Economics and spent the next decade in a celebrated theoretical duel with John Maynard Keynes at Cambridge. His Prices and Production and Monetary Theory and the Trade Cycle argued, against Keynes, that business cycles were the result of credit-induced distortions in the structure of production rather than of deficient aggregate demand, and that central planning could not in principle outperform a price system because the dispersed knowledge of millions of agents could not be aggregated by any planner. The argument was made canonical in his 1945 article "The Use of Knowledge in Society."

The Road to Serfdom, published in 1944 in the depths of the war, warned that even well-meaning collectivism slid by its own logic toward tyranny. Condensed by Reader's Digest, it sold by the million in the English-speaking world and made him a public intellectual far beyond the seminar room. In 1947 he convened a small congress of liberal economists at Mont Pèlerin in Switzerland that became the Mont Pèlerin Society, the nucleus of post-war classical liberalism.

He moved to Chicago, then to Freiburg and Salzburg, wrote the late three-volume Law, Legislation and Liberty, and won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1974 jointly with Gunnar Myrdal. The British Conservative leader who, in opposition in the 1970s, slapped his Constitution of Liberty on a Conservative Research Department table and announced "This is what we believe" was Margaret Thatcher.
