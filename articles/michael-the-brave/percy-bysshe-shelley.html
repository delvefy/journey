Percy Bysshe Shelley

English Romantic poet, 1792–1822

Born to a wealthy baronet’s family, Shelley was a rebel from boyhood. Expelled from Oxford for publishing The Necessity of Atheism, he broke with his father and devoted himself to radical politics, free love, and poetry. He eloped with the sixteen-year-old Mary Godwin, daughter of the philosopher William Godwin and the pioneering feminist Mary Wollstonecraft, and the couple joined Lord Byron by Lake Geneva in 1816 in the gathering that produced Frankenstein.

A visionary idealist, Shelley made poetry the vehicle of his dream of human liberation. Prometheus Unbound recast the rebel Titan as the hero of mankind’s deliverance from tyranny; The Mask of Anarchy answered the Peterloo massacre with a ringing call to nonviolent resistance; and the sonnet Ozymandias compressed the vanity of despots into fourteen unforgettable lines. He wrote luminous lyrics—Ode to the West Wind, To a Skylark—and, on the death of his friend Keats, the great elegy Adonais. In A Defence of Poetry he called poets “the unacknowledged legislators of the world.”

His life in Italy was shadowed by the deaths of his children and by financial strain, but his powers grew. In 1822, not yet thirty, he drowned when his small boat sank in a storm off the Tuscan coast. His body was cremated on the shore, and his ashes laid in Rome. Long underrated, he is now ranked among the supreme lyric poets of England.
