Nefertiti

Great Royal Wife of Akhenaten, c.1370–1330 BC

Nefertiti, whose name means “the beautiful one has come,” was the Great Royal Wife of the pharaoh Akhenaten and queen of Egypt during its strange religious revolution. Her origins are obscure — perhaps the daughter of the courtier Ay, perhaps of foreign birth — but at Akhenaten’s side she rose to a prominence almost without parallel for an Egyptian queen, shown in the reliefs of Amarna as her husband’s equal in the worship of the Aten and even, in the kingly posture of the conqueror, smiting Egypt’s enemies.

With Akhenaten she had six daughters, one of whom, Ankhesenamun, would become the wife of Tutankhamun. As the cult of the sun-disk reshaped the kingdom, Nefertiti stood at its devotional heart, and some scholars believe she went on to rule in her own right after her husband’s death, under another name, before the boy king restored the old order.

Her later life and death vanish from the record, and her tomb and mummy have never been certainly identified. Yet she became, in the modern world, the most recognizable face of ancient Egypt. In 1912 German excavators at Amarna uncovered a painted limestone bust of the queen, its serene and perfect features preserved across three thousand years; displayed in Berlin, it is among the most admired works of art to survive from antiquity, and it has made Nefertiti an enduring emblem of beauty and of a vanished age.
