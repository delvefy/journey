Albert Camus

French-Algerian novelist and essayist, 1913–1960

Albert Camus was born on 7 November 1913 at Mondovi, in French Algeria, the son of a farm labourer of Alsatian-French stock. His father was killed at the Battle of the Marne when Albert was eleven months old, and the boy grew up in two cramped rooms in the working-class Belcourt district of Algiers, looked after by an illiterate, half-deaf mother and a tyrannical grandmother. The Mediterranean light, his mother’s silence and the poverty of that household are the recurring backdrop of everything he wrote.

A scholarship saved him for the lycée and for the University of Algiers, where he submitted a master’s thesis on Saint Augustine and Plotinus in 1936. Tuberculosis, contracted at seventeen, closed the academic door and dogged him for the rest of his life. He drifted into journalism at Alger-Républicain, ran a small political theatre company, joined and was thrown out of the Communist Party. He moved to occupied France in 1940 and during the war edited the clandestine Resistance newspaper Combat, taking it above ground after the Liberation as one of the most respected dailies in Paris.

In the same span he wrote the two books that made him famous. The Stranger (1942), the curt first-person tale of Meursault, who kills an Arab on a beach and is condemned more for not weeping at his mother’s funeral than for the murder, was paired with the long philosophical essay The Myth of Sisyphus (1942) on what one does in the face of an indifferent universe. The Plague (1947) sealed his international reputation; The Rebel (1951) broke his friendship with Sartre. He received the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1957, at forty-four. On 4 January 1960 he was killed when Michel Gallimard’s Facel Vega, driving him from Lourmarin to Paris, struck a plane tree at Villeblevin.
