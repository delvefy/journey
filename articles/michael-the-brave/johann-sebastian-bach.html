Johann Sebastian Bach

German composer and organist, 1685–1750

Bach was born into a large family of professional musicians in Thuringia and orphaned young, raised by an elder brother. Largely self-taught as a composer, he absorbed the styles of his German predecessors and of the Italians, copying and arranging the concertos of Vivaldi to master their form. Through a career spent in the service of churches and courts he earned fame in his lifetime chiefly as a virtuoso organist, his composing admired by connoisseurs but considered by some old-fashioned.

He held a succession of posts at Weimar, then at Köthen, where he wrote much instrumental music including the Brandenburg Concertos, and finally for nearly three decades at Leipzig, where as cantor of St Thomas’s he produced an enormous body of sacred music. There he composed cantatas for the church year, the towering St Matthew Passion, and the Mass in B minor, alongside keyboard collections such as the Well-Tempered Clavier and the Goldberg Variations that became cornerstones of Western music.

Bach brought the art of counterpoint to a summit never surpassed, combining contrapuntal mastery with profound expressive depth. Late in life he visited the court of Frederick the Great, who set him a theme on which he improvised and later elaborated into The Musical Offering, and he closed his career with the unfinished Art of Fugue. After his death his music fell into relative neglect until a great revival in the nineteenth century restored him to the centre of the canon, where he has remained as one of the supreme composers of all time.
