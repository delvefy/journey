Charles Dickens

English novelist, 1812–1870

The defining novelist of the Victorian age, Dickens drew his lifelong sympathy for the poor from bitter childhood experience: when his improvident father was thrown into debtors’ prison, the twelve-year-old Charles was set to work pasting labels in a riverside blacking factory, a humiliation he never forgot. Rising through journalism, he became a sensation in 1836 with the comic Pickwick Papers, published in monthly parts, and never looked back.

Over the next three decades he poured out a stream of novels that combined unforgettable characters, broad comedy, melodrama, and fierce social criticism: Oliver Twist, A Christmas Carol, David Copperfield, Bleak House, Hard Times—dedicated to Carlyle—Great Expectations, and A Tale of Two Cities. He exposed the workhouse, the debtors’ prison, the law’s delays, and the cruelties of industrial England, reaching a readership unmatched by any author before him.

His electrifying public readings drew vast crowds on both sides of the Atlantic, though they drained his health, and his private life grew troubled after he separated from his wife. Among the admirers who sought him out was the Danish storyteller Hans Christian Andersen, whose famously protracted stay as a houseguest strained their friendship. Shaken by a railway disaster in his last years, Dickens died in 1870 and was buried, against his modest wishes, in Westminster Abbey. The visitor who outstayed his welcome was Hans Christian Andersen.
