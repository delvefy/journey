Ernest Rutherford

New Zealand-born physicist, “father of nuclear physics,” 1871–1937

The son of a flax farmer near Nelson, New Zealand, Rutherford won a scholarship that carried him to the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge, where he studied under J. J. Thomson. Moving to McGill University in Montreal, he unravelled the nature of radioactivity, showing that radioactive atoms transmute into other elements and distinguishing alpha and beta rays. For this work he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1908—to the amusement of a man who thought of himself as a physicist.

His greatest discovery came at Manchester. When his assistants Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at thin gold foil and found a few bouncing straight back, Rutherford grasped that almost all of an atom’s mass and positive charge must be concentrated in a tiny central nucleus. This nuclear model of the atom, announced in 1911, became the foundation of modern physics. In 1917 he became the first person to split the atom deliberately, transmuting nitrogen into oxygen.

As director of the Cavendish he presided over a golden age, training a remarkable school of physicists; his students James Chadwick discovered the neutron and Cockcroft and Walton built the first accelerator. Famous for his booming voice and blunt manner, he had earlier hosted the young Danish theorist Niels Bohr, who would turn his nuclear atom into a quantum model. Rutherford died in 1937 and was buried in Westminster Abbey near Newton.
