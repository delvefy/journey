Gorgias

Sicilian Sophist and rhetorician, c. 483–375 BCE

Gorgias was born at Leontini in Sicily and became one of the most celebrated of the Sophists, the travelling teachers of wisdom and persuasion who flourished in the Greek world of the fifth century BCE. By tradition he studied under the philosopher Empedocles, and from him perhaps took both his interest in nature and his gift for spellbinding speech.

In 427 BCE he came to Athens as an envoy from his native city, seeking aid against Syracuse, and astonished the Athenians with the dazzling artifice of his oratory. His balanced, rhythmic, ornament-laden style—antitheses, rhymes, and symmetries later called “Gorgianic figures”—set a fashion and made him rich and famous as a teacher of rhetoric; among his pupils was Isocrates.

He was more than a stylist. In a daring treatise, On Non-Being, he argued in three steps that nothing exists; that if anything did exist it could not be known; and that if it could be known it could not be put into words—a radical challenge to the confident metaphysics of his day. In his showpieces, the Encomium of Helen and the Defence of Palamedes, he displayed the power of speech to make the weaker case appear the stronger.

Plato made him the title figure of a famous dialogue, in which Socrates probes the moral dangers of rhetoric divorced from truth. Reputedly living past a hundred years, Gorgias embodied both the brilliance and the suspect cleverness that gave the Sophists their lasting, double-edged name.
