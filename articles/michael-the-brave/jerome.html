Jerome

Translator of the Vulgate and Father of the Church, c. 342–420 AD

Jerome was born in the Roman Balkans and educated at Rome, where he steeped himself in the classics and conceived a lifelong love of Cicero's prose. A famous story tells how he dreamed of being dragged before God's judgement seat and condemned with the rebuke, "You are a Ciceronian, not a Christian"—a vision that turned him from pagan letters to the service of Scripture. He spent years as an ascetic in the Syrian desert, mastering Greek and Hebrew, and was ordained a priest.

His learning brought him to Rome as secretary to Pope Damasus, who set him the task of revising the Latin Bible. There he also guided a circle of noble Roman women toward the ascetic life, among them the devoted Paula, who would follow him east. After Damasus died, Jerome withdrew to Bethlehem and founded monasteries, devoting the rest of his life to scholarship.

His monument is the Vulgate, a Latin Bible much of it freshly rendered from the original Hebrew and Greek, including the letters of Paul, which became the standard text of the Western Church for over a thousand years. To it he added a flood of commentaries, lives of illustrious Christian writers, and letters of brilliant, biting Latin. A fierce controversialist, he broke with his friend Rufinus over the legacy of Origen and crossed swords even with Augustine of Hippo on the meaning of Scripture. Honoured as a Doctor of the Church, the irascible, learned monk of Bethlehem left Western Christianity its very words.
