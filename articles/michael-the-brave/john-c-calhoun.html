John C. Calhoun

American statesman and political theorist, 1782–1850

Born in the South Carolina up-country to Scots-Irish settlers and educated at Yale, Calhoun entered Congress as a fiery young nationalist and “War Hawk” who helped push the United States into the War of 1812. He served capably as Secretary of War under Monroe and then as vice-president—first under John Quincy Adams and then under Andrew Jackson—a rare distinction shared by few.

But his nationalism curdled into a ferocious sectionalism. Breaking with Jackson over the tariff, he secretly authored the doctrine that a state could “nullify” a federal law it judged unconstitutional, bringing South Carolina to the brink of armed defiance in the Nullification Crisis. He resigned the vice-presidency to defend his cause from the Senate floor, where with Henry Clay and Daniel Webster he formed the “Great Triumvirate” that dominated antebellum debate.

Calhoun became the most rigorous intellectual champion of the slaveholding South, defending slavery not as a necessary evil but as a “positive good” and devising theories of the concurrent majority to protect minority sections. Dying in 1850 as the Senate wrestled with the great compromise over slavery in the territories, he warned that the Union could not endure—a prophecy his disciples would test a decade later. The president whose administration he had joined and then defied was Andrew Jackson.
