Robert the Bruce

King of Scots, 1274–1329

Robert the Bruce was born in 1274 into one of the great families of Scotland, grandson of Robert de Brus, a leading claimant to the throne in the succession dispute that Edward I of England arbitrated. As a young Earl of Carrick he shifted between English allegiance and the patriotic cause during the rising of William Wallace, never wholly committing while his family’s royal ambitions remained alive. The turning point came in February 1306, when he stabbed his rival John Comyn before the altar of the Greyfriars church at Dumfries—a sacrilege that left him excommunicate but cleared his path. Six weeks later he had himself crowned king at Scone.

His first campaigns were disasters. Defeated, outlawed and hunted, he became a fugitive in the western isles, a period that gathered to itself the famous, probably legendary, tale of the patient spider. From 1307 he waged a patient guerrilla war, retaking castles one by one and avoiding pitched battle. The reward came on 23–24 June 1314 at Bannockburn, where his smaller army shattered the much larger host of Edward II of England in the greatest Scottish victory of the age.

Victory on the field did not bring recognition. The Declaration of Arbroath of 1320, a letter from the Scottish magnates to the Pope, defiantly asserted the realm’s independence and Robert’s kingship. Only in 1328, by the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton concluded under the young Edward III, did England at last acknowledge Scotland as a free kingdom. Robert died the next year, by tradition of leprosy, asking that his heart be carried on crusade; it was borne into battle in Spain by James Douglas. He left the throne to his son David II.

The road continues to William Wallace.
