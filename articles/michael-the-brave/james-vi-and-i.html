James VI and I

King of Scotland from 1567, of England and Ireland from 1603, 1566–1625

James was born in Edinburgh Castle in June 1566, the only child of Mary, Queen of Scots and her second husband Henry, Lord Darnley. His father was murdered eight months later; in July 1567 his mother was forced to abdicate, and the thirteen-month-old baby was crowned at Stirling. The first eighteen years of his reign in Scotland passed under a sequence of regencies—Moray, Lennox, Mar, Morton—and the rigorous Calvinist tutorship of the humanist George Buchanan, who made the boy a polyglot scholar and a wary politician. He took personal control of his kingdom in his late teens.

The execution of his mother in England in 1587 he accepted, on advice, as the price of his place in the English succession. He married Anne of Denmark in 1589, after a stormy voyage that he later attributed to witchcraft and that fed his treatise Daemonologie of 1597. The Basilikon Doron of 1599, addressed to his son Prince Henry, set out his theory of kingship by divine right. On the death of Elizabeth I in March 1603 the long-anticipated union of the crowns took place: James rode south to London and added the English and Irish thrones to the Scottish one he had held since infancy.

His English reign is remembered by its high and low marks. The Gunpowder Plot of November 1605, a Catholic conspiracy to blow up the Lords with the king inside, was uncovered in time; Guy Fawkes was caught with the powder, and the day became a national festival. The Authorized Version of the Bible, the so-called King James Bible, appeared in 1611 from a project he had launched at the Hampton Court Conference of 1604. He patronised the King’s Men, the playing company of William Shakespeare. His financial improvidence, his quarrels with parliament, and his trust in unworthy favourites—Robert Carr and especially George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham—stored up the troubles that broke under his son. James died at Theobalds in March 1625 and was succeeded by Charles I.
