Leonidas I

King of Sparta, d. 480 BCE

Leonidas I, of the Agiad royal house, became a king of Sparta around 489 BCE and entered legend as the commander of the last stand at Thermopylae. He was a son of King Anaxandridas II and succeeded his half-brother Cleomenes I; he married Cleomenes's daughter Gorgo, who appears in Herodotus as a woman of notable shrewdness.

In 480 BCE, as Xerxes led his enormous army into Greece, the allied Greek states resolved to hold the narrow coastal pass of Thermopylae. Leonidas marched north with a modest force, its core the famous royal bodyguard of three hundred Spartans, supported by Thespians, Thebans, and other contingents. For two days they held the pass against vastly greater numbers, until a local man named Ephialtes guided a Persian column along a mountain track to take the defenders from behind.

Learning that he was being encircled, Leonidas dismissed most of the allied troops but remained with his Spartans and a few others to fight to the death—by tradition in fulfilment of an oracle that Sparta must lose a king or fall. He was killed in the final struggle, and Herodotus records that Xerxes, in anger, ordered his body abused. The Greeks later raised a monument on the spot bearing the famous couplet of Simonides, bidding the passer-by tell the Spartans that there the dead lay obedient to their laws.

Though Thermopylae was a defeat, it became the supreme emblem of disciplined courage and willing sacrifice for one's homeland, and Leonidas its enduring hero.
