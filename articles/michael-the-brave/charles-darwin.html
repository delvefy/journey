Charles Darwin

English naturalist, author of On the Origin of Species, 1809–1882

Born into a prosperous Shrewsbury family—grandson of the physician-poet Erasmus Darwin and of the potter Josiah Wedgwood—Darwin abandoned medicine at Edinburgh and was destined for the clergy when an invitation changed his life. In 1831 he sailed as naturalist aboard HMS Beagle under Captain FitzRoy on a five-year survey voyage. The fossils of Patagonia, the uplifted shells of the Andes, and above all the peculiar finches and tortoises of the Galápagos planted the seed of an idea that would overturn the understanding of life.

Back in England he quietly assembled evidence for evolution by natural selection—the struggle for existence weeding out the unfit and preserving favourable variations—but, fearing the storm it would raise, he withheld it for two decades. He was finally spurred to act when Alfred Russel Wallace sent him an essay sketching the same theory; their work was presented jointly in 1858, and the following year Darwin published On the Origin of Species, which sold out at once and provoked furious debate.

Plagued by chronic illness, he worked on from his home at Down House, extending his argument to humankind in The Descent of Man and studying everything from orchids to earthworms. He left the defence of his theory in public largely to bolder allies. When he died in 1882 he was buried in Westminster Abbey near Isaac Newton, his name forever joined to the central organising idea of modern biology.

The road continues to Thomas Henry Huxley.
