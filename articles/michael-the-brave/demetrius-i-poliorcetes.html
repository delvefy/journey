Demetrius I Poliorcetes

Macedonian king and Diadoch, 337–283 BCE

Demetrius was the son of Antigonus the One-Eyed, the most powerful of the generals who fought over Alexander the Great's empire after his death. Brilliant, impulsive, and given to extravagant pleasures, Demetrius joined his father's effort to reunite the whole inheritance of Alexander under their own rule.

In 307 BCE he “liberated” Athens from its Macedonian garrison and was loaded with honours there. The next year he won a great naval victory over Ptolemy at Salamis in Cyprus, after which he and his father assumed the title of king. His celebrated siege of Rhodes in 305–304, with its towering engines, broke against the Rhodians' stubborn defence but won him the surname Poliorcetes, “the Besieger”; the bronze sold from his abandoned equipment is said to have paid for the Colossus of Rhodes.

His fortunes turned at the Battle of Ipsus in 301, where the allied kings—Seleucus, Lysimachus, and others—crushed the Antigonid army and his father was killed. Demetrius survived, clung to his fleet and a few cities, and by 294 had seized the throne of Macedon itself.

But his Macedonian subjects wearied of his arrogance, and he was driven out by Pyrrhus and Lysimachus. In a last reckless campaign in Asia he was hemmed in and forced to surrender to Seleucus, who held him in comfortable captivity until he drank himself to death in 283. Plutarch paired his turbulent life with that of Mark Antony, as a study of great gifts ruined by excess.
