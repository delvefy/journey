Honoré de Balzac

French novelist, 1799–1850

Balzac was the great founder of literary realism and one of the most prodigious workers in the history of letters. After early failures in business that left him perpetually in debt, he threw himself into writing with monastic ferocity, fuelled by oceans of black coffee and labouring through the night in his dressing gown, often turning out thousands of words a day for weeks on end.

His vast life’s work he gathered under a single title, La Comédie humaine—a connected series of some ninety novels and stories meant to anatomise the whole of French society after the fall of Napoleon. Recurring characters move from book to book through every rank and corner of the nation, from misers and bankers to provincial schemers and Parisian courtesans. Masterpieces such as Père Goriot, Eugénie Grandet, and Lost Illusions dissect the corrosive power of money and ambition with unmatched density and force.

A friend and rival of the leading writers of his day, eulogised at his graveside by Victor Hugo, Balzac brought a documentary thoroughness and a teeming vitality to fiction that opened the way for the realists and naturalists who followed, from Flaubert to Zola. He married his longtime love, the Polish countess Hańska, only months before his health, broken by overwork, gave out, and he died in Paris in 1850.
