Demosthenes

Athenian statesman and orator, 384–322 BCE

Demosthenes was the greatest of the orators of ancient Athens. Orphaned young and cheated of his inheritance by his guardians, he taught himself the art of speaking, by tradition overcoming a weak voice and a stammer through fierce practice—declaiming with pebbles in his mouth and against the roar of the sea. He began as a writer of speeches for the law courts before entering public life.

His career was dominated by one great cause: resistance to the rising power of Philip II of Macedon. In a series of fiery speeches—the Philippics and the Olynthiacs—he warned his fellow citizens that Philip threatened the liberty of all Greece and urged them to rouse themselves and fight. He forged alliances and stiffened Athenian resolve, but the cause was lost on the field of Chaeronea in 338.

His bitter rivalry with the orator Aeschines reached its climax in the great courtroom duel over the Crown, in which Demosthenes defended his own career in the magnificent speech “On the Crown” and crushed his opponent so utterly that Aeschines left Athens in disgrace.

Demosthenes never abandoned the dream of Greek freedom. After Alexander's death he helped raise the Lamian War against Macedon, but when Antipater crushed the revolt in 322, the orator was hunted down. To escape capture he took poison in the temple of Poseidon on the island of Calauria. Revered ever after as the supreme model of political eloquence—studied and imitated by Cicero and countless others—he became the very emblem of the patriot who speaks truth to power.
