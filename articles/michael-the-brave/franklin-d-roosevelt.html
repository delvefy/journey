Franklin D. Roosevelt

32nd President of the United States, 1882–1945

Born to a patrician family at Hyde Park on the Hudson, Roosevelt entered politics as a Democrat, served as Assistant Secretary of the Navy, and seemed destined for high office until polio left him paralysed from the waist down in 1921. Concealing the worst of his disability, he fought his way back, won the governorship of New York, and in 1932, with the nation sunk in the Great Depression, was elected president.

His New Deal—a torrent of relief programmes, public works, banking reforms, and the new system of Social Security—reshaped the role of American government, while his reassuring “fireside chats” on the radio restored a frightened country’s confidence. He was elected an unprecedented four times. As war engulfed the world he made America the “arsenal of democracy,” and after Pearl Harbor he led the nation into the conflict.

With Churchill and Stalin he formed the Big Three that planned the defeat of Hitler and the shape of the postwar world at Tehran and Yalta. It was a letter from Albert Einstein that prompted him to launch the atomic bomb project. Worn out by the strain, he died suddenly in April 1945, just weeks before victory in Europe, and was succeeded by his vice-president, Harry Truman. He had consciously modelled his early career on his distant cousin and wife’s uncle, the earlier President Roosevelt.
