Ernest Hemingway

American writer, 1899–1961.

Born in Oak Park, Illinois, the son of a country doctor and a singer mother, Hemingway skipped college to write cub-reporter copy for the Kansas City Star, whose style sheet — short sentences, short paragraphs, vigorous English — he later credited as the best rules he ever learned. In 1918 he volunteered as a Red Cross ambulance driver in Italy, was blown up by a mortar shell at Fossalta on the Piave, and convalesced in Milan in love with the nurse Agnes von Kurowsky — a wound and a romance he would later rework into A Farewell to Arms.

In Paris through the 1920s as the Toronto Star's European correspondent, he attached himself to the modernist circle Gertrude Stein called "the lost generation": Ezra Pound and Sylvia Beach steered him, Ford Madox Ford published him, James Joyce drank with him, F. Scott Fitzgerald championed him to Scribner's. The Paris years produced In Our Time, The Sun Also Rises, and Men Without Women, and crystallized the stripped-down declarative prose — the "iceberg" of stated fact over implied feeling — that became the most imitated voice in twentieth-century English. A Farewell to Arms (1929) confirmed his fame; the Spanish Civil War, which he covered in 1937 alongside his future wife Martha Gellhorn, gave him For Whom the Bell Tolls (1940).

From the late 1930s he based himself first at his Key West house and then at the Finca Vigía outside Havana, where he wrote The Old Man and the Sea (1952), the short novel that won him the 1954 Nobel Prize in Literature. Four marriages, three sons, big-game safaris in Africa, deep-sea fishing in the Gulf Stream, two plane crashes in Uganda in 1954, and a friendship of cordial mutual suspicion with Fidel Castro after the Cuban Revolution. Depression, electroconvulsive therapy at the Mayo Clinic, and the disabling loss of his ability to write led him on the morning of 2 July 1961 in Ketchum, Idaho, to fit a Boss shotgun against his forehead and pull the trigger.

The road continues to Fidel Castro.
