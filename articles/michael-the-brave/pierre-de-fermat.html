Pierre de Fermat

French jurist and mathematician, 1607–1665

Fermat earned his living as a lawyer and magistrate in Toulouse, pursuing mathematics in his spare hours and almost never publishing, so that his discoveries survive mainly in letters and in notes scribbled in the margins of his books. Working largely through correspondence, much of it routed through the friar Marin Mersenne, he nonetheless ranks among the founders of several branches of mathematics.

He is most famous for number theory, a field he revived almost single-handedly. Beside a problem in his copy of Diophantus he claimed that no whole numbers satisfy the equation for powers above the second, adding that he had a marvellous proof the margin was too small to contain. This “last theorem” resisted every effort until it was finally proved more than three centuries later. He also stated the result now called Fermat’s little theorem and made deep conjectures about primes and sums of squares.

In an exchange of letters with Blaise Pascal over the division of stakes in an unfinished game, he helped create the theory of probability. He developed methods for finding maxima, minima, and tangents to curves that anticipated the differential calculus, and he worked out a form of analytic geometry at the same time as Descartes, with whom he quarrelled over optics. His principle that light travels the path of least time became a guiding idea in physics. Reserved and unassuming, he left a legacy out of all proportion to the modest record he bothered to publish.
