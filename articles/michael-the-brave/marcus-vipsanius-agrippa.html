Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa

Roman general and statesman, c. 63–12 BCE

Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa was the soldier and builder whose talents made possible the reign of Augustus. Born about 63 BCE to an obscure family, he was a boyhood companion of the young Octavian and stood by him from the moment of Julius Caesar's assassination, becoming the indispensable instrument of his rise.

Agrippa was the finest commander of his generation. He defeated Sextus Pompey at the naval battle of Naulochus in 36 BCE, having trained a fleet in a sheltered harbour he built for the purpose, and above all he commanded the ships at Actium in 31 BCE, where the manoeuvres he directed broke the navy of Mark Antony and Cleopatra and delivered the Roman world to Octavian. Where Augustus reaped the glory, Agrippa had won the battles.

In peace he turned the same energy to the city. As aedile he repaired and extended the aqueducts and cleansed the great sewers; he constructed baths, gardens, and the first Pantheon, and commissioned a vast map of the known world displayed in Rome. No man did more to give the capital its monumental shape.

Bound ever closer to the ruling house, he married Augustus's daughter Julia, and their sons Gaius and Lucius were marked out as heirs to the empire. Granted tribunician power and a great military command, Agrippa was in all but name co-ruler of the Roman state. He died in 12 BCE, mourned by Augustus, who delivered his funeral oration and laid him in the imperial mausoleum.
