Charles XII of Sweden

King of Sweden, 1682–1718

Charles XII was the warrior-king whose meteoric career and ruinous fall ended Sweden's age as a great power. Born in 1682, he came to the throne at fifteen, and when Russia, Denmark, and Saxony-Poland fell upon his Baltic empire in 1700, hoping to despoil a boy, they found instead a soldier of rare genius. He swiftly knocked Denmark out of the war and then, with a far smaller army, annihilated Peter the Great's host at Narva.

Turning south, he overran Poland and Saxony, defeated Augustus the Strong, and forced him from the Polish throne in favour of his own client. For half a dozen years Charles seemed invincible. Then in 1708 he launched the fatal stroke: an invasion of Russia. Peter drew him deep into the land, laying it waste, and the terrible winter and dwindling supplies wore the Swedes down. At Poltava in 1709, his army—reinforced by the Cossack hetman Mazepa—was destroyed.

Charles escaped southward into the Ottoman Empire, where he spent some five years at Bender scheming to turn the Turks against Russia, until his exasperated hosts drove him out. He made a celebrated lone ride across Europe back to a kingdom whose empire was crumbling around it.

In 1718, while besieging the fortress of Fredriksten in Norway, he was killed by a shot to the head, amid lasting argument over whether it came from the enemy or from his own side. Austere, fearless, and single-minded, he was immortalised in Voltaire's biography and in Samuel Johnson's lines on doomed ambition, and his Russian catastrophe would foreshadow Napoleon's.
