Pierre Terrail, seigneur de Bayard

The Knight without Fear and beyond Reproach, c.1473–1524

Pierre Terrail was born about 1473 at the château of Bayard, near Pontcharra in the Dauphiné, the second son of an old family of Pinerolo extraction whose men had died by tradition in their armour. He was raised by his uncle, the bishop of Grenoble, sent at thirteen as page to Duke Charles I of Savoy and then to Charles VIII, and made his first campaigns with the army that crossed the Alps in 1494. At Fornovo on 6 July 1495 he is said to have personally seized a Mantuan standard; he was eighteen and the action was reported across France. The peerless reputation he carried for the rest of his life — “sans peur et sans reproche,” the chevalier without fear or fault — was fixed under Louis XII in the Italian wars.

The exploits are uncountable but a few stand out. In April 1503 at the bridge of Garigliano, the story went, he held the narrow span single-handed against two hundred Spaniards while his men cut the planks behind him. At Genoa in 1507 he was the first man up the wall. At Padua in 1509, in the war of the League of Cambrai against Venice, he saved Emperor Maximilian’s reputation by storming the breach. At Marignano on 13–14 September 1515, in the great victory over the Swiss that opened Francis I’s reign and reopened Lombardy, he commanded the French rear and dressed the line through the night; afterwards, at his own request, Francis came to him under his banner and knelt to receive the accolade from the only man in France worthy to give it.

He was a soldier of the older chivalric mould in an age that was passing. He read Froissart, refused ransoms when his opponents had behaved well, freed nuns whose convents had been taken by his troops, and disapproved both of the arquebus, by which the wars were now to be decided, and of the courtly venality of Francis I. In the spring of 1524 he commanded the rearguard of Bonnivet’s shattered army in the retreat from Lombardy. Crossing the Sesia near Romagnano on 30 April, he was struck in the small of the back by an arquebus ball from the Italian troops of the Constable de Bourbon, the former French peer who had defected to Charles V. He died propped up against a tree, holding the cross-hilt of his sword to his lips for an altar, and refused the offer of confession to his executioner because, he said, his own life had been confession enough.
