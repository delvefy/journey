Henri Matisse

French painter and sculptor, 1869–1954

Matisse came late and by accident to art—a law clerk recovering from illness, he was given a paintbox by his mother and never looked back. By 1905 he led a group of young painters whose canvases blazed with such arbitrary, unmixed colour that a startled critic dubbed them les fauves, the wild beasts. For Matisse colour was the heart of everything: not a description of the world but a means of expression and, as he hoped, of serenity—an art like “a good armchair” in which the weary mind might rest.

Across half a century he pursued a luminous, decorative harmony in great compositions such as The Dance and The Red Studio, in odalisques painted in the warm light of Nice, where he sought out and revered the aged Renoir, and in radiant interiors. His lifelong rivalry and mutual respect with Pablo Picasso—the two regarded as the poles of modern art—spurred both men to greater daring.

In old age, bedridden after surgery and unable to stand at an easel, he reinvented himself once more, “drawing with scissors” to make the cut-paper collages of pure colour that are among his most joyous works, and designing the Chapel of the Rosary at Vence as the summation of his life. He died in 1954, the supreme colourist of his century.
