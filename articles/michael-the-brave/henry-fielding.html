Henry Fielding

English novelist and magistrate, 1707–1754

Henry Fielding was born in 1707 at Sharpham Park in Somerset, son of an army officer of impoverished gentry stock. He was educated at Eton and briefly at the University of Leiden before drifting to London in his early twenties to make his way as a playwright. From 1728 to 1737 he wrote some twenty-five comedies and farces for the London stage, scandalous and politically pointed enough that Walpole's government pushed through the 1737 Licensing Act, ending his career in the theatre.

He retrained as a barrister at the Middle Temple while turning to prose. Shamela in 1741, an anonymous parody of Samuel Richardson's recently celebrated Pamela, mocked Richardson's epistolary moralism. Joseph Andrews, published the following year, expanded the joke into a full novel "written in imitation of the manner of Cervantes" and discovered, almost by accident, a wholly new English form: the comic epic in prose. Tom Jones of 1749, his masterpiece, took a foundling hero through eighteen books of inns, highways, mistresses, and London streets, narrated by a worldly, intrusive voice that became the model for nineteenth-century English fiction. Amelia of 1751, darker and more domestic, closed his novelistic career.

From 1748 he was a magistrate at Bow Street, and there, with his blind half-brother John, he founded the Bow Street Runners, the first organised plainclothes police force in London. He worked himself to ruin on the dual labour of writing and law enforcement. Sent abroad for his health, he died at Lisbon in 1754, where his journal of the voyage was published posthumously.

His running, comic-realistic narrator and his appetite for the whole social range of the city passed directly into Walter Scott, into Thackeray, and most of all into Charles Dickens. The Cervantine inheritance he had announced in his preface remained avowed throughout his work. But the rival whose pious Pamela had provoked Tom Jones into existence, and whose epistolary realism would compete with Fielding's panoramic comedy for the soul of the English novel, was Samuel Richardson.

The road continues to Samuel Richardson.
