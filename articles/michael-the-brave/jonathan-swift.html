Jonathan Swift

Anglo-Irish satirist and clergyman, 1667–1745.

Born in Dublin to a recently deceased English steward of the Inns of Court and his Leicestershire widow, Swift was educated at Kilkenny School and Trinity College, Dublin, where he took his degree only by special grace and acquired a lifelong distrust of formal Aristotelian learning. The Glorious Revolution drove him to England in 1689, and through the next decade he served as secretary to Sir William Temple at Moor Park in Surrey — the retired diplomat who introduced him to the controversies of the Ancients and Moderns and to the eight-year-old Esther Johnson, whom Swift would call Stella and would teach, defend, and write to for the rest of her life.

Ordained in the Church of Ireland in 1695, he split his career between Irish livings and London pamphleteering. The early A Tale of a Tub (1704) and The Battle of the Books (1704) made his anonymous reputation as a savage prose satirist in the line of Lucian and Erasmus's Praise of Folly. From 1710 to 1714 he served as the chief Tory pamphleteer of the Harley-Bolingbroke ministry, editing The Examiner, drafting state papers, and recording his life and London friendships in the daily letters to Stella later collected as the Journal to Stella. The Hanoverian succession of 1714 ended his hopes of an English bishopric; he was given instead the deanery of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin, and from that post made himself the conscience of Ireland against English mercantile and currency policy — The Drapier's Letters (1724) defeated Wood's halfpence, A Modest Proposal (1729) recommended that Irish infants be served at table.

In 1726 he travelled to England carrying the manuscript of Travels into Several Remote Nations of the World, which his publisher Benjamin Motte issued anonymously that October. Gulliver's Travels was at once a children's adventure, a satire on the Royal Society and the projectors of the Walpole era, an attack on parties and pride, and the most savage indictment of the human animal anyone had yet written in English. The last decade of his life clouded with deafness, vertigo (later recognized as Ménière's disease), and what contemporaries called "fatuity"; he was found of unsound mind by an Irish commission in 1742, and died at the deanery in October 1745. The Latin epitaph he wrote for his Cathedral tomb tells the visitor that saeva indignatio — savage indignation — can no longer lacerate his heart, and asks him to imitate, if he can, a man who served liberty.
