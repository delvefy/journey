Albertus Magnus

German Dominican scholar, c.1200–1280

Albert of Lauingen, called Albertus Magnus, Albert the Great, was born in Swabia about 1200 and joined the Dominican Order, going on to teach at Cologne and Paris. His learning was so vast and so wide-ranging that his own age named him the Universal Doctor. He wrote on theology and philosophy, but also, with a naturalist’s curiosity rare in his time, on plants, animals, minerals, the heavens and the secrets of chemistry, often trusting his own observation over the authority of books.

His greatest service was to Aristotle. At a moment when the rediscovered works of the Greek philosopher, arriving through Arabic translations and the commentaries of Avicenna and Averroes, both fascinated and alarmed Christendom, Albert set out to expound the whole Aristotelian corpus and to show that this pagan science could be reconciled with the faith. In doing so he opened the path for the synthesis that his own pupil would complete.

For at Cologne and Paris he taught the young Thomas Aquinas, recognized the silent student’s genius — declaring that this “dumb ox” would one day fill the world with his bellowing — and later defended his memory. Albert served briefly as a bishop, laboured as a peacemaker, and died at Cologne in 1280. Canonized centuries afterward as a Doctor of the Church and made the patron of natural scientists, he stands as the great bridge by which Greek and Arabic learning entered the medieval schools.
