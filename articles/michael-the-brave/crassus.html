Crassus

Roman politician and the richest man in Rome, c. 115–53 BC

Marcus Licinius Crassus built the greatest private fortune of the late Republic and used it to buy a place among its rulers. He rose in the service of the dictator Sulla, and amassed his wealth shrewdly and ruthlessly—through property speculation, silver mines, slave-trading, and the proscriptions in which the goods of the condemned were sold cheap. A famous story tells of his private fire brigade, which would bargain for a burning building before agreeing to put out the flames.

His chief military achievement was the suppression of the great slave revolt led by Spartacus. In 71 BC Crassus broke the rebel army and crucified some six thousand captives along the Appian Way, a grim warning that lined the road from Capua to Rome. Yet much of the public glory was snatched by Pompey, who mopped up fugitives and claimed to have ended the war—a slight Crassus never forgot.

Wealth and rivalry drew him into the First Triumvirate of 60 BC alongside Pompey and Julius Caesar, the three men dividing power among themselves. Crassus, however, lacked the military laurels of his partners and craved a great conquest of his own. He led a Roman army east against the Parthian Empire, and in 53 BC met disaster at Carrhae, where the Parthian horse-archers annihilated his legions. Crassus himself was killed during a parley. With his death the triumvirate collapsed, leaving Pompey and Caesar to settle their quarrel in civil war.

The road continues to Spartacus.
