Pericles

Athenian statesman of the Golden Age, c. 495–429 BC

Pericles led Athens at the height of its power and brilliance, so completely that the age is named for him. Born into an aristocratic family, he embraced the democratic cause and, through repeated election to the office of general, guided the city for some three decades. He widened the democracy by introducing pay for jury service, allowing poorer citizens to take part in public life, and he made Athens the schoolroom of Greece.

The empire was the engine of his policy. Athens had built a great naval alliance, the Delian League, to resist Persia; Pericles transformed it into an empire, moving its treasury to Athens and drawing on its tribute. With that wealth he adorned the Acropolis, commissioning under the sculptor Phidias the marble temples crowned by the Parthenon—monuments meant to proclaim Athenian supremacy for all time.

His vision of the city is preserved in the Funeral Oration recorded by the historian Thucydides, a soaring tribute to Athenian democracy, openness, and the citizens who died for it. Yet his confidence helped bring on catastrophe. His hard line toward Sparta and her allies led to the long Peloponnesian War, and his strategy of withdrawing the rural population behind the city walls turned Athens into a crowded refuge. When plague swept through the packed city, it killed not only thousands of citizens but, in 429 BC, Pericles himself, leaving Athens to fight on without the leader who had defined its greatness.
