Karl Marx

German philosopher and revolutionary, 1818–1883

Born in Trier in the Rhineland, Marx studied law and then philosophy, falling in with the radical Young Hegelians who turned Hegel’s idealism toward criticism of religion and the state. A combative journalist, he was expelled from Prussia, France, and Belgium in turn, and from 1849 lived the rest of his life as an exile in London. There began his lifelong partnership with Friedrich Engels, the manufacturer’s son who supported him financially and intellectually for decades.

In 1848, as revolutions swept Europe, the two issued The Communist Manifesto, with its ringing call for the workers of the world to unite. Marx argued that all history was a history of class struggle, that capitalism produced its own gravediggers in the proletariat, and that revolution would usher in a classless society. He poured years of labour in the British Museum into his masterwork, Das Kapital, a vast critique of political economy whose first volume appeared in 1867.

He helped found the International Workingmen’s Association, quarrelling with anarchists like Bakunin over its direction, and lived much of his life in poverty, several of his children dying young. He admired the new evolutionary science of his day, sending an inscribed copy of Das Kapital to Charles Darwin. Marx died in 1883 and was buried at Highgate; within decades his ideas had inspired mass parties and revolutions that would reshape half the globe.
