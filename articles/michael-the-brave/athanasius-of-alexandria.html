Athanasius of Alexandria

Bishop of Alexandria and Doctor of the Church, c. 296–373 AD

Athanasius was born in Alexandria around 296 and rose as a deacon and secretary to Bishop Alexander, whom he accompanied to the Council of Nicaea in 325. There, though still young, he emerged as a fierce opponent of the priest Arius, who taught that the Son was a created being inferior to the Father. In 328 Athanasius succeeded Alexander as bishop of Alexandria.

For nearly half a century he defended the Nicene doctrine that the Son was “of one substance” with the Father. His unbending stance against a succession of emperors and Arian-leaning bishops cost him dearly: he was driven from his see into exile five separate times, spending some seventeen years as a fugitive, at the western court, and among the desert monks. His isolation gave rise to the phrase “Athanasius against the world.”

He was a theologian of the first rank. His youthful On the Incarnation expounded how God became man so that man might be saved, and his many polemical works pounded the Arian cause. His Festal Letter of 367 contains the earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament canon.

Athanasius also bound the rising monastic movement to the wider Church. His Life of Antony, written about the great hermit of the Egyptian desert, became one of the most widely read books of the age and carried the monastic ideal across the Christian world. He died in Alexandria in 373, his cause at last secure, and is honoured as one of the great Doctors of the Church.
