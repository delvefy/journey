John Stuart Mill

English philosopher and economist, 1806–1873

Subjected by his father and Bentham to one of the most demanding educations on record—Greek at three, political economy in his early teens—Mill was groomed to be the standard-bearer of utilitarian reform. At twenty he suffered a deep mental crisis, escaping from it through poetry and emerging with a broader, more humane philosophy that tempered the cold arithmetic of his upbringing with feeling and individuality.

He became the foremost liberal thinker of the Victorian age. In On Liberty he made the classic case that society may restrain the individual only to prevent harm to others, defending freedom of thought and speech as essential to truth. He refined utilitarianism by insisting that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity, argued the case for democracy in Considerations on Representative Government, and in The Subjection of Women made one of the first great philosophical arguments for sexual equality, shaped by his partnership with Harriet Taylor.

As a member of Parliament he championed women’s suffrage and electoral reform, and he later stood as secular godfather to the infant Bertrand Russell. His friendship with the historian Thomas Carlyle, once close, broke down over deep differences of temperament and politics, most bitterly over questions of liberty and authority. Mill died at Avignon in 1873. The friend from whom he became so estranged was Thomas Carlyle.
