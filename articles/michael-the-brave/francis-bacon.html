Francis Bacon

English philosopher and statesman, 1561–1626

Sir Francis Bacon was the herald of the scientific method and one of the great prose writers of the English language. Born in 1561 into a high political family—his father was Elizabeth I's Lord Keeper—he trained as a lawyer and entered Parliament young, beginning a long and grasping climb at court. He served the queen, befriended and then prosecuted the doomed Earl of Essex, and under James I rose at last to the summit as Lord Chancellor, with the titles Baron Verulam and Viscount St Alban.

His fall was as steep as his rise. In 1621 he was convicted of taking bribes as a judge, stripped of office, fined, and briefly imprisoned. Disgraced in public life, he turned his last years wholly to the reform of knowledge.

There his achievement was immense. Against the inherited logic of Aristotle he set a new instrument, the Novum Organum, calling for knowledge built upward from patient observation and experiment rather than spun from first principles. He exposed the "idols" that mislead the mind, proclaimed that knowledge is power, and in the New Atlantis imagined a research institute that would inspire the founding of the Royal Society.

By tradition he died in 1626 from a chill caught while stuffing a fowl with snow to test whether cold preserved flesh—a fittingly experimental end. Remembered as the father of empiricism, he set the program that Newton's science would fulfil and that thinkers from Locke to Voltaire would carry into the Enlightenment; the young Thomas Hobbes had served as his secretary.
