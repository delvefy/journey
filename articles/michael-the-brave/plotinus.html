Plotinus

Founder of Neoplatonism, c.204–270

Plotinus was born about 204 in Roman Egypt and studied philosophy for some eleven years in Alexandria under the mysterious teacher Ammonius Saccas. Hungry for the wisdom of the East, he attached himself to the emperor Gordian’s ill-fated Persian campaign; when it collapsed he made his way to Rome, where about the year 245 he opened a school and taught for the rest of his life, becoming the last great original philosopher of the ancient world.

His system, the crowning revival of Plato that we call Neoplatonism, described all reality as flowing from a single ineffable source, the One, beyond being and knowledge. From the One emanates the divine Intellect, the realm of the eternal Forms, and from Intellect the Soul, and from Soul the material world; and the human soul, fallen into matter, may turn and climb back through these levels toward mystical union with its origin — a union Plotinus himself was said to have attained. Profoundly indebted to Plato and in constant argument with Aristotle, he gave the old philosophy a new and spiritual depth.

The emperor Gallienus was his patron, and his devoted pupil Porphyry gathered his treatises after his death into the six sets of nine that are known as the Enneads. Through them his influence flowed far beyond paganism: it shaped the theology of Augustine, who found in “the books of the Platonists” a ladder toward God, and coloured Christian, Islamic and Jewish thought for a thousand years. Plotinus died in 270, ascetic and otherworldly to the last.
