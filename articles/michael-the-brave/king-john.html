King John

King of England, 1166–1216

John was born in 1166, the youngest son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, and so far down the line of succession that he was nicknamed Lackland. While his brother Richard the Lionheart was away on crusade and then in captivity, John intrigued for the throne; he won it anyway in 1199 on Richard’s death, pushing aside the claim of his young nephew Arthur of Brittany, who vanished in John’s custody and was almost certainly murdered.

His reign was a catalogue of disasters that nonetheless changed history. By 1204 he had lost Normandy, Anjou and most of the Angevin inheritance on the Continent to Philip II Augustus of France. A quarrel with Pope Innocent III over the see of Canterbury brought an interdict on England and John’s excommunication, ended only when he surrendered the kingdom in 1213 and received it back as a papal fief. His grinding taxation to fund the recovery of France collapsed when his coalition was crushed at Bouvines in 1214.

Defeated abroad and bankrupt at home, John faced open revolt from his barons. In June 1215, in the meadow of Runnymede, he was compelled to set his seal to Magna Carta, the great charter limiting royal power that would echo through every later century of English and common law. He had no intention of keeping it; war resumed at once, the rebels offered the crown to the French dauphin, and John died of dysentery in 1216 while campaigning, having lost part of his treasure to the tides of the Wash. He left a nine-year-old heir and a reputation as the worst of England’s kings.
