Tirso de Molina

Spanish dramatist and friar, c. 1579–1648

Tirso de Molina was the pen name of Gabriel Téllez, a Mercedarian friar who became one of the most gifted playwrights of the Spanish Golden Age. A devoted follower of Lope de Vega, he wrote hundreds of plays in the master’s mould, of which some eighty survive, ranging from sparkling comedies of intrigue to grave historical and religious dramas. He was admired for the wit and psychological subtlety of his female characters, unusually bold and resourceful for the stage of his day.

His towering contribution to world literature is a single figure. In El burlador de Sevilla (The Trickster of Seville), generally attributed to him, he gave dramatic life to Don Juan—the irresistible, blasphemous seducer who deceives woman after woman until the stone statue of a man he has murdered drags him down to hell. From this play the legend spread across Europe, inspiring Molière’s Dom Juan, Mozart’s opera Don Giovanni, and Byron’s great satirical poem, making Don Juan one of the enduring myths of the modern imagination.

His outspoken satire on court affairs drew the displeasure of the authorities, and a committee on public morals censured his profane comedies; his order eventually restrained his playwriting and sent him to provincial houses. He rose to be a chronicler and superior within the Mercedarians and died in 1648, his name forever bound to the seducer he set loose upon the world.
