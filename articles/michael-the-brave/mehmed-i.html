Mehmed I

Ottoman Sultan, restorer of the empire, c.1386–1421

Mehmed was the fourth son of Bayezid Yıldırım, born about 1386, and was governing the sancak of Amasya in north-central Anatolia when his father’s army was destroyed at Ankara in 1402. He alone of the princes who reached safety had been raised entirely in the Turkmen east of the empire, and his Amasya household — strengthened by his lala Bayezid Pasha and by ties to the Çandarlı vizier dynasty — became the seed from which the Ottoman state grew back. The eleven years of the Fetret Devri, the Interregnum, were a civil war of brothers under the loose suzerainty of Timur and then of his sons: Süleyman ruled in Edirne over the Balkans, İsa in Bursa, Musa over the marcher lords, while Mehmed slowly absorbed Anatolia town by town.

In 1403 he defeated and executed İsa; in 1410 Süleyman, his most dangerous rival, was abandoned by his troops outside Edirne and strangled in flight; in July 1413 Musa, the last and fiercest, was caught at Çamurlu in Serbia and put down with the help of the Serbian despot Stefan Lazarević and the Byzantine emperor Manuel II, who had backed Mehmed throughout. Reigning at last from a single capital, Mehmed pursued the policy of conciliation his nickname Çelebi — the gentleman, the educated one — captured. He restored stipends to deposed Anatolian beys, paid tribute to Byzantium for the release of his hostage brothers, and rebuilt the timar registers and the Janissary corps along the lines his father had used.

The years of restoration were not entirely peaceful. The dervish revolt of Sheikh Bedreddin in Rumelia in 1416, with its egalitarian and syncretic preaching, was put down only after hard fighting in the Deliorman; the Karamanid emirate of Konya rose three times and was three times humbled. A Venetian fleet under Pietro Loredan destroyed an Ottoman squadron off Gallipoli in May 1416, the first serious naval defeat the dynasty suffered. Mehmed died at Edirne, perhaps from a stroke, on 26 May 1421, and was buried in the Yeşil Türbe — the Green Tomb — at Bursa that he had begun for himself. He left his son Murad an empire smaller than Bayezid’s but coherent, solvent, and capable of resuming the offensive.
