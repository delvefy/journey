Christoph Willibald Gluck

Composer and operatic reformer, 1714–1787

Christoph Willibald Gluck was the composer who reformed opera and pointed it toward the modern music drama. Born in 1714 in the Upper Palatinate to a family of foresters with Bohemian roots, he trained in Prague and in Milan, and spent his early career turning out conventional Italian opera in the prevailing style, travelling as far as London, where he met an unimpressed Handel.

Settling in Vienna, he became a composer at the imperial court and music teacher to the archduchesses, among them the young Marie Antoinette. There, working with the librettist Ranieri de' Calzabigi, he launched the revolution of his maturity. Convinced that opera had decayed into a mere showcase for singers, he set out to restore drama, simplicity, and truth of feeling, declaring in the famous preface to Alceste that music must serve the poetry and the action rather than smother them. His Orfeo ed Euridice of 1762 was the first great fruit of this creed.

In the 1770s his former pupil, now Queen of France, drew him to Paris, where his French operas—Iphigénie en Aulide and Iphigénie en Tauride—won triumphs and ignited a furious public quarrel between his admirers and the partisans of his Italian rival Piccinni.

Gluck mentored the young Salieri, helped shape the operatic art of Mozart, and was revered by Berlioz and Wagner as the founder of their own ideals of dramatic music. He died in Vienna in 1787, having changed the course of opera. Among the poets whose verses he set was the reigning master of the older school, the imperial laureate Pietro Metastasio.
