Ralph Waldo Emerson

American essayist and Transcendentalist, 1803–1882

Ralph Waldo Emerson was born in Boston on 25 May 1803, the son of a Unitarian minister who died when the boy was eight, leaving the family in genteel poverty. He made his way through Harvard, taught school, and followed his father into the Unitarian pulpit at Boston’s Second Church. The death of his young first wife and growing doubts about ritual led him to resign the ministry in 1832; a journey to Europe followed, where he met Wordsworth, Coleridge and, most lastingly, Thomas Carlyle, beginning a friendship and correspondence that ran for decades.

Settling in Concord, Massachusetts, Emerson became the presiding spirit of American Transcendentalism. His little book Nature (1836) proclaimed that the divine could be known directly through the natural world and the individual soul; his oration “The American Scholar” called for an intellectual life independent of Europe; and his Essays — above all “Self-Reliance” — preached trust in one’s own genius against the pressure of conformity. He earned his living as a lecturer, carrying his gospel across the lyceum circuit of the growing nation.

In Representative Men (1850) he measured himself against the great minds he revered, devoting essays to Plato, Montaigne, Shakespeare, Napoleon and Goethe. He gathered around him a remarkable Concord circle, encouraging the young Henry David Thoreau, and famously hailed Walt Whitman’s Leaves of Grass at the start of its author’s career. Drawn into the anti-slavery cause, he defended John Brown as a martyr. His doctrine of self-trust echoed far beyond America — Friedrich Nietzsche carried his essays as a favourite companion. Emerson died in Concord on 27 April 1882, the acknowledged sage of his country’s letters.
