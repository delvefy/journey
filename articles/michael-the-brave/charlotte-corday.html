Charlotte Corday

French assassin of Marat, 1768–1793.

Marie-Anne Charlotte de Corday d'Armont was born at Les Champeaux in the bocage of Normandy, the fourth of five children of an impoverished gentry family that traced its descent from the seventeenth-century dramatist Pierre Corneille. Her mother died when she was ten and a sister soon after, and her father placed her with an aunt in Caen and then in the Abbaye-aux-Dames convent there, where she received an unusually thorough education for a girl of her class — the classics in Latin, Plutarch's Lives in translation, Rousseau's Social Contract, and her ancestor's Le Cid. When the Revolution dissolved the convents in 1791 she returned to her cousin Madame de Bretteville's house in Caen.

Caen became, after the proscription of the Girondins in Paris on 2 June 1793, the centre of the federalist resistance to the Mountain. Through the summer Corday attended the Girondin leaders' meetings — Barbaroux, Pétion, Buzot — in the Intendance and the Hôtel de l'Intendance; she came to believe, against the advice of those same leaders, that the Revolution could be saved only by killing the Cordeliers journalist whom she identified as its chief inciter, Jean-Paul Marat. She bought a six-inch kitchen knife in the Palais-Royal arcades on her arrival in Paris on 11 July, talked her way into Marat's apartment on the rue des Cordeliers on the evening of 13 July, and stabbed him once through the right lung as he sat in his medicated bath.

She was arrested at the scene, tried before the Revolutionary Tribunal on 17 July 1793, conducted herself throughout with extraordinary composure, justified the killing as the regicide of a tyrant in the line of Plutarch's Brutus, and was guillotined on the Place de la Révolution the same evening. She was twenty-four. Tradition reports that the executioner's assistant Legros lifted her severed head from the basket and slapped its cheek, and that the cheek visibly reddened. The Girondins she had hoped to save did not survive: most went to the same scaffold within the year. The Romantic nineteenth century turned her into the "Angel of the Assassination," Lamartine's epithet that fixed her as either a saint or a regicide depending on which side of the political quarrel one read her from.
