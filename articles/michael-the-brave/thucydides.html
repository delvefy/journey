Thucydides

Athenian historian, c. 460–400 BCE

Thucydides was the Athenian historian whose History of the Peloponnesian War set a new standard of rigor and remains a founding work of political and military analysis. Born around 460 BCE into a wealthy family with Thracian connections, he lived through the long struggle between Athens and Sparta that he made his subject.

He served as one of Athens' generals in 424 BCE, charged with the defense of the Thraceward coast, but arrived too late to stop the Spartan commander Brasidas from seizing the vital city of Amphipolis. For this failure he was exiled, and he spent the next twenty years gathering testimony from both sides of the war—an enforced detachment that he turned into a source of unusual impartiality.

Where Herodotus had ranged widely over peoples and marvels, Thucydides narrowed his gaze to political and military events and their causes, weighing evidence, discounting the supernatural, and composing speeches—most famously the Funeral Oration of Pericles—to lay bare the reasoning of those involved. His account of the plague of Athens, the chilling Melian Dialogue, and the catastrophe of the Sicilian Expedition are among the most studied passages in all ancient literature.

He sought to write not for the applause of the moment but as a possession for all time, confident that human nature would cause like events to recur. His history breaks off in mid-sentence in the year 411 BCE; he is thought to have died around 400, his great work left unfinished.
