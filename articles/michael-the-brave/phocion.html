Phocion

Athenian statesman and general, c. 402–318 BCE

Phocion was an Athenian general and statesman of the fourth century BCE, honored by later ages with the surname "the Good" for his austere integrity. A pupil in the circle of Plato's Academy, he joined philosophy to a long public career, and the Athenians are said to have elected him general no fewer than forty-five times, though he rarely courted the office.

In politics he was the leading voice of caution and accommodation. Against the fiery patriotism of Demosthenes, who urged resistance to Macedon at all costs, Phocion argued that Athens lacked the strength to fight Philip and later Alexander, and that peace, even on hard terms, served the city better than ruin. Alexander himself is said to have respected him and to have sent him rich gifts, which the incorruptible Phocion declined.

After Alexander's death and Athens' defeat in the Lamian War, Phocion governed a chastened, pro-Macedonian Athens under the protection of the regent Antipater. But when Antipater died and Polyperchon proclaimed the restoration of the democracy, Phocion's enemies turned upon him.

Condemned for treason in 318 BCE, he was made to drink hemlock, meeting death with the same calm that Socrates had shown in the same city long before. Within a few years the Athenians repented, gave him a public burial, and raised a statue in his honor—a belated tribute to a man whose honesty they had killed.
