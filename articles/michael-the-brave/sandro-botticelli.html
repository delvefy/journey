Sandro Botticelli

Florentine painter, 1445–1510

Alessandro di Mariano Filipepi was born at Florence in 1445, the youngest son of a tanner who lived on the Borgo Ognissanti two doors from the Vespucci. The nickname Botticelli — “little barrel,” a kitchen joke aimed first at his elder brother and then at him — replaced the family name in every signature he ever made. He was apprenticed to the painter-friar Fra Filippo Lippi, who taught him the soft Madonnas and the linear grace that would never quite leave him; after Lippi’s death he went into the Verrocchio workshop, where the young Leonardo was a fellow apprentice, and emerged about 1470 with the Fortitude for the Mercanzia tribunal that is the first picture wholly his own.

The years between 1475 and 1485 are the great period. The Adoration of the Magi for the church of Santa Maria Novella (c.1475) put Cosimo, Piero, and the young Lorenzo into the scene as kneeling kings, and the painter himself on the right edge looking out at the viewer. From Lorenzo de’ Medici’s cousin Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco came the two great mythological panels: the Primavera, with the Neoplatonic procession of Venus, the Graces, Mercury, Flora, and Zephyr in the orange grove; and, a few years later, the Birth of Venus, the first life-size female nude on a panel since antiquity, painted from a frieze of antique sources and from the Florentine convention of Simonetta Cattaneo’s pallid beauty. In 1481–82 Sixtus IV summoned him to Rome to fresco the side walls of the Sistine Chapel beside Perugino, Ghirlandaio, and Cosimo Rosselli — three scenes from the lives of Moses and Christ that established his European reputation.

In the 1490s his manner darkened under the pressure of the Savonarolan reform and of his own piety. The Mystic Nativity of 1500, signed in Greek and inscribed with a Joachimite apocalyptic message, and the late illustrations to Dante’s Comedy on parchment, drawn for Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco between 1480 and 1495, abandoned the perspectival measure of the high quattrocento for a thin, vibrating line that the next century would find archaic. He died at Florence on 17 May 1510 in poverty so common to his last years that his name had begun to slip from the working memory of the city; Vasari, fifty years later, recovered it. The Uffizi rediscovery of his great mythologies was the work of the nineteenth century.
