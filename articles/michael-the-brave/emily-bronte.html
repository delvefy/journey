Emily Brontë

1818–1848, English novelist and poet

Emily Jane Brontë was born at Thornton in Yorkshire, the fifth child of the Reverend Patrick Brontë and his wife Maria. The family removed to the parsonage at Haworth in 1820, and from the deaths of her mother and the two eldest sisters Emily formed almost her entire experience of the world from the parsonage rooms, the moors above the village, and the imaginative life she shared with her brother Branwell and her sisters Charlotte and Anne.

While Charlotte and Branwell wrote of the kingdom of Angria, Emily and Anne created the parallel saga of Gondal, an imaginary North-Atlantic island whose dynastic struggles and tragic queens supplied the matter of many of Emily's poems. She was sent at six to the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge and again, briefly, to Roe Head, and could not bear either school for long. A few months in 1842 at the Pensionnat Heger in Brussels with Charlotte was her only sustained absence from Haworth. She kept house there for her father, baked the family bread, and trained the parsonage dogs.

She read deeply in Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and the Romantic poets, and her reading of Lord Byron's eastern tales and of Manfred shaped both her own poems and her one novel. Charlotte found a manuscript of her verse by accident in 1845, and the three sisters published the next year a slim volume as Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell. In December 1847 her novel appeared under the same male pseudonym — Wuthering Heights, the story of Heathcliff and Catherine Earnshaw on the windswept Yorkshire moors. The early reviewers were repelled by its violence; later critics ranked it among the strangest and most original of English novels.

She caught a chill at Branwell's funeral in September 1848, refused medical help, and died of tuberculosis at Haworth on 19 December 1848, aged thirty. She left only the poems, Wuthering Heights, and a handful of devoirs in French.
