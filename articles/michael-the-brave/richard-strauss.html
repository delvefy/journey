Richard Strauss

German composer and conductor, 1864–1949.

Born in Munich to the principal horn of the court opera, Strauss grew up steeped in the classical canon his father policed against the Wagnerian heresy. The boy composed prodigiously, and by his twenties had already begun to subvert his father's tastes, converting to the music of the future under the influence of the violinist and composer Alexander Ritter. From Hans von Bülow he inherited the conductorship at Meiningen and a lifelong second career on the podium that took him through Munich, Weimar, Berlin, and Vienna.

His tone poems of the 1880s and 1890s — Don Juan, Tod und Verklärung, Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche, Also sprach Zarathustra, Don Quixote, Ein Heldenleben, the Symphonia Domestica — pushed the orchestral programme music inherited from Liszt to a new pitch of pictorial precision and harmonic daring. With Salome (1905) and Elektra (1909) he shocked Europe with one-act operas of extreme dissonance and psychological violence, then pivoted to the Mozartean comedy of Der Rosenkavalier (1911), the first triumph of his long partnership with the librettist Hugo von Hofmannsthal, which continued through Ariadne auf Naxos, Die Frau ohne Schatten, and Arabella.

His conduct under the Third Reich has clouded his legacy: he served, briefly and uncomfortably, as the first president of the Reichsmusikkammer, was forced out for collaborating with the Jewish writer Stefan Zweig on Die schweigsame Frau, and spent the war years trying to shield his Jewish daughter-in-law and grandsons. The late works — Metamorphosen, the Oboe Concerto, the Vier letzte Lieder — close a long career with an autumnal serenity that mourns the German culture he had outlived.
