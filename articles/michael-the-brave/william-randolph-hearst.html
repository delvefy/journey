William Randolph Hearst

1863–1951, American newspaper publisher

William Randolph Hearst was born in San Francisco, the only child of George Hearst, a Missouri-born mining engineer who struck silver in the Comstock Lode and rose to the United States Senate, and Phoebe Apperson Hearst, a former schoolteacher who poured the family fortune into universities and antiquities. He attended Harvard, where Joseph Pulitzer's New York World fascinated him and a series of pranks ended his college career. In 1887 his father turned over to him the struggling San Francisco Examiner.

He poured money into the paper, hired Ambrose Bierce among others, and within five years had made it the largest daily in the West. In 1895 he bought the New York Journal and threw it directly against Pulitzer's World. The competition produced the bold headlines, the colour comics and the sensational crusades that gave “yellow journalism” its name. When the battleship Maine exploded in Havana harbour in 1898, the Journal led the press clamour for war with Spain, and Hearst sailed to Cuba to report it himself.

Through the early twentieth century he assembled the largest newspaper empire in the world: at his peak nearly thirty dailies, the King Features syndicate, magazines, newsreels, and radio stations, reaching one American in four daily. He served two terms in Congress, sought the Democratic presidential nomination of 1904 and the mayoralty of New York in 1905, supporting himself never able to leap from publisher to officeholder. He turned violently against Franklin D. Roosevelt after 1935 and bankrolled the early career of Joseph McCarthy.

He built San Simeon on the California coast as Hearst Castle, filled it with European furnishings, and lived there openly with the actress Marion Davies. His prodigal spending and the Depression forced him in the late 1930s to liquidate art and unload papers. In 1941 Orson Welles modelled the title figure of Citizen Kane on him, and Hearst, infuriated, tried to suppress the film. He died at Marion Davies's Beverly Hills house in 1951.
