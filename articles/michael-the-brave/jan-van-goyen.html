Jan van Goyen

Dutch landscape painter, 1596–1656

Jan van Goyen was born in 1596 in Leiden, the son of a cobbler. He was apprenticed to a succession of local masters in his teens and then, around 1617, sent to Haarlem to study with Esaias van de Velde, whose lively early landscapes set him on his course. From van de Velde he learned to take the flat Dutch countryside, with its dunes, rivers, frozen canals and small wooden bridges, as the proper subject of painting in its own right.

By the mid-1620s he had developed a manner of his own—rapid, thinly painted, almost monochrome landscapes in dun browns, ochres and watery greys, with a low horizon line and a great open sky overhead. Working in parallel with Salomon van Ruysdael and Pieter de Molijn, he created what later art history would call the "tonal phase" of Dutch landscape, the foundation on which the entire seventeenth-century Dutch landscape school was built. He produced enormous numbers of these pictures, often very quickly, and at very modest prices.

He moved to The Hague in 1632 and lived there for the rest of his life, holding posts in the painters' guild. His pictures of the city's outskirts—the dunes near Scheveningen, ice-skating scenes on the canals, the great river views with small fishing boats—refined his manner further into the colour and broad space of the View of The Hague of 1651. He earned well from painting but lost his money in tulip speculation and unwise real-estate ventures, and died in 1656 leaving his family in debt.

Among his pupils were probably the young Aelbert Cuyp, who later turned his master's grey light into the warm gold of Dordrecht. His son-in-law was the painter Jan Steen, who married his daughter Margriet in 1649 and would go on to become the great comic chronicler of the Dutch domestic interior—Jan Steen.
