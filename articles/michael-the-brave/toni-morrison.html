Toni Morrison

American novelist and editor, 1931–2019

Toni Morrison was born Chloe Ardelia Wofford on 18 February 1931 in Lorain, Ohio, the second of four children of a welder and a domestic worker who had moved north from rural Alabama and Georgia. Her parents kept the house full of music and the talk of haunts; she read Tolstoy and Jane Austen and Faulkner before she was twenty. After Howard University, where she took the nickname Toni from her baptismal name Anthony, she went to Cornell for a master’s, writing on suicide in the work of Faulkner and Woolf — the two novelists who, more than any others, shaped her sense of what an English sentence could do.

She taught for a few years and in 1965 took a job at Random House in New York, becoming its first black woman editor. Over the next two decades she shepherded books by Toni Cade Bambara, Angela Davis, Henry Dumas, Gayl Jones — and one famous autobiography of Muhammad Ali — while drafting her own novels by night. The Bluest Eye (1970) and Sula (1973) earned readers; Song of Solomon (1977) won the National Book Critics Circle Award and turned her into a public figure.

Beloved appeared in 1987 — a ghost story drawn from the real case of Margaret Garner, a fugitive slave who had killed her own daughter rather than see her recaptured. It won the Pulitzer Prize and, more than any other twentieth-century American novel, recast how the long aftermath of slavery could be written. The Nobel Prize for Literature followed in 1993; she was the first African American woman to receive it. Jazz, Paradise, A Mercy, and Home continued the project. President Obama gave her the Medal of Freedom in 2012 and spoke at her memorial. She died in the Bronx on 5 August 2019, eighty-eight years old.
