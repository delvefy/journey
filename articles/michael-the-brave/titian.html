Titian

Venetian painter of the High Renaissance, c. 1488–1576

Tiziano Vecellio was born about 1488 at Pieve di Cadore, a small Alpine town in the Venetian Dolomites, the son of a soldier and town councillor. He was sent at nine or ten to a Venetian uncle and apprenticed in the workshop of Sebastiano Zuccato, then to Gentile and to Giovanni Bellini, in whose shop he ended in close partnership with Giorgione. The two of them frescoed the lost façade of the Fondaco dei Tedeschi in 1508; when Giorgione died of plague in 1510, Titian inherited the unfinished commissions and the looser, atmospheric handling that became his own. By 1516, on Bellini's death, he was the official painter of the Republic.

His Venetian altarpieces — the Assumption of the Virgin at the Frari of 1518, the Pesaro Madonna of 1526, the slightly later Death of St Peter Martyr — and the mythological "poesie" painted for Alfonso d'Este at Ferrara, including the Worship of Venus, the Bacchanal of the Andrians, and Bacchus and Ariadne, set the standard for the painting of the next century. The portrait of the doge Andrea Gritti, the so-called Man with a Blue Sleeve, and the Venus of Urbino done for Guidobaldo della Rovere extended that authority over portraiture and the female nude.

From 1530 he was the chosen painter of the Emperor Charles V, who knighted him as Count Palatine after the equestrian portrait of Mühlberg in 1548 and made his pension a charge on the customs of Milan. After 1556 he served Charles's son Philip II of Spain, for whom he painted the great series of mythological poesie — Danaë, Venus and Adonis, Diana and Actaeon, Diana and Callisto, the Rape of Europa — and a long sequence of religious works that grew increasingly broken in handling. He maintained a vast workshop in Venice, kept up an immensely productive correspondence with his friend Pietro Aretino, and died there during the plague of 1576, almost ninety, working to the last on a Pietà for his own tomb.
