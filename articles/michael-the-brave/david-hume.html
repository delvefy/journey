David Hume

Scottish philosopher and historian, 1711–1776

Hume was the most penetrating philosopher of the Scottish Enlightenment and perhaps of the whole British tradition. His youthful A Treatise of Human Nature, he later joked, fell “dead-born from the press,” but its ideas were revolutionary: a thoroughgoing empiricism that questioned whether reason could ever justify our belief in cause and effect, in the enduring self, or in miracles. We expect the future to resemble the past, he argued, only from habit, not from logic.

Finding philosophy unprofitable, he won fame and fortune instead as an essayist and as author of a multi-volume History of England that became a long-standing bestseller. Genial and witty, “le bon David” was beloved in the salons of Paris, where he spent several years attached to the British embassy among the leading thinkers of the day.

It was there that he befriended Jean-Jacques Rousseau and brought him to refuge in England, only for the friendship to collapse into a notorious public quarrel. His sceptical challenge to received certainties would, by Immanuel Kant’s own admission, rouse the German philosopher from his “dogmatic slumber” and set the course of modern philosophy. His dearest friend was the economist Adam Smith, who sat with him through his calm and cheerful final illness and recorded, against the expectations of a scandalised public, how serenely the great unbeliever met his death.
