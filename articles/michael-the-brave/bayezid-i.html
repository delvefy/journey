Bayezid I

Ottoman Sultan, c.1360–1403

Bayezid was the elder son of Murad I, born about 1360 to the Greek concubine Gülçiçek Hatun and raised in the new Ottoman style of palace and frontier. He commanded the right wing at Kosovo on 15 June 1389; when an assassin’s knife reached his father in the tent after the battle, Bayezid was acclaimed sultan on the field and had his brother Yakub strangled the same evening, the first instance of the Ottoman law of fratricide. The byname Yıldırım — Thunderbolt — was earned in the next year of campaigning, when he rode the length of his inheritance, forcing the surviving Anatolian beyliks of Aydın, Saruhan, Menteşe, and Germiyan into submission and beginning a methodical encirclement of Constantinople.

From 1394 he kept the Byzantine capital under loose blockade, building the fortress of Anadolu Hisarı on the Bosphorus to throttle traffic from the Black Sea. The crusade summoned by Pope Boniface IX and led by John, count of Nevers and future Duke of Burgundy, came down the Danube to relieve the pressure; on 25 September 1396 Bayezid destroyed it in front of Nicopolis, taking thousands of French and Hungarian knights prisoner and beheading most of them in retaliation for a massacre of his own captives a few days before. The victory secured the Balkans for a generation and brought Wallachia, Bulgaria, and northern Greece into the empire’s tributary orbit. He extended Ottoman rule over Konya and Sivas, swallowing the last fragments of the Seljuk inheritance, and styled himself sultan-i Rum.

His annexations in eastern Anatolia drew him into the path of Timur, who was campaigning out of Samarkand to reassemble the legacy of the Mongols. The two met on the plain of Çubuk, north of Ankara, on 28 July 1402; the Anatolian contingents in Bayezid’s army defected at the critical hour, his Janissaries were overrun, and the sultan himself was taken prisoner with one of his sons. Timur carried him through the winter campaign in a covered litter and, by some accounts, in an iron cage. Bayezid died in captivity at Akşehir on 8 March 1403, by his own hand according to one chronicle. His four surviving sons fought eleven years for the throne in the Ottoman Interregnum, and the empire he had built almost vanished.

The road continues to Timur.
