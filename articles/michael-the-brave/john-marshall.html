John Marshall

Chief Justice of the United States, 1755–1835

Born on the Virginia frontier and hardened by service with Washington’s army through the winter at Valley Forge, Marshall became a leading Federalist lawyer and, briefly, Secretary of State before John Adams named him Chief Justice in 1801. He would hold the office for thirty-four years, longer than anyone since, and would transform a weak and uncertain Supreme Court into a coequal branch of government.

In Marbury v. Madison he established the Court’s power to strike down laws that violated the Constitution—judicial review—the cornerstone of American constitutional law. In a succession of bold opinions he affirmed the supremacy of federal over state authority, upheld a broad reading of Congress’s implied powers, and protected contracts and interstate commerce, binding the young republic into a true nation.

A genial man of plain manners but formidable mind, he dominated his colleagues by force of reasoning and turned the Court into a single authoritative voice. He clashed repeatedly with his distant cousin and bitter rival Thomas Jefferson, and in 1807 presided over a sensational trial, refusing to bend the law of treason to the administration’s wishes and securing the defendant’s acquittal. He also found time to write a multi-volume life of George Washington. Marshall died in 1835. The defendant he had acquitted of treason was Aaron Burr.
