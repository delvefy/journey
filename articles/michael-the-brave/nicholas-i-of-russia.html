Nicholas I of Russia

Autocrat and “gendarme of Europe,” 1796–1855

Nicholas I was the most rigidly autocratic of the Russian emperors, a soldier-tsar who made the defence of order his life's creed. Born in 1796, a younger son never expected to reign, he came to the throne in 1825 amid the chaos of a disputed succession and was met at once by the Decembrist revolt, a rising of liberal officers that he put down without mercy. The experience fixed his rule for thirty years.

He governed by the trinity of Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality, upheld by a vast secret police and a heavy censorship—he served as personal censor even to the poet Pushkin. He crushed a Polish uprising, and abroad he earned the title of “gendarme of Europe” by sending his armies to extinguish the Hungarian revolution of 1849 and so rescue the young Habsburg throne.

Beneath the parade-ground discipline, his reign coincided with a brilliant flowering of Russian literature, even as the autocracy fenced it in. But his foreign ambitions, especially against the decaying Ottoman Empire, at last drew Britain and France into the field against him.

The Crimean War exposed the backwardness of his system, and Nicholas died in 1855 with his armies faltering before Sevastopol, broken, it was whispered, as much by humiliation as by illness. He left to his son the task of reform. The young emperor whose crown his soldiers had saved in 1849 was Franz Joseph I of Austria.
