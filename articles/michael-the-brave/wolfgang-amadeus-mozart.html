Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Austrian composer, 1756–1791

Mozart was the supreme prodigy of music. Born in Salzburg, he was composing and performing across the courts of Europe as a small child, paraded by his father Leopold before kings and emperors. He absorbed every style he encountered and, by his teens, had outgrown the role of marvel to become a master of opera, symphony, concerto, and chamber music alike, with a fluency and apparent ease that astonished his contemporaries.

Chafing under the service of the Archbishop of Salzburg, he broke away to make an independent career in Vienna, a risky course for a musician of the age. There he produced an extraordinary stream of masterpieces: the operas The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, and The Magic Flute, written with his librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte and others; a series of great piano concertos he performed himself; and his final symphonies, crowned by the “Jupiter.” He befriended the older Haydn, who declared him the greatest composer he knew, and studied the counterpoint of Bach.

Despite his fame his finances were precarious, and his health gave way as he laboured on a Requiem mass commissioned in mysterious circumstances. He died in 1791 at only thirty-five, the work unfinished, and was buried without ceremony in a common grave. He left behind more than six hundred compositions of unmatched grace and depth, and his name became, for posterity, almost a synonym for musical genius itself.
