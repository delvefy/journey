Joan of Arc

French peasant girl, military leader, and saint, c.1412–1431

Joan was born about 1412 in the village of Domrémy on the Meuse, on the still-Armagnac fringe of Lorraine, the youngest daughter of Jacques d'Arc, a small farmer, and Isabelle Romée. From the age of thirteen she heard voices she identified as Michael, Catherine of Alexandria, and Margaret of Antioch, who told her she must drive the English from France and have the Dauphin Charles crowned at Reims. In May 1428 she travelled to the garrison of Vaucouleurs and finally persuaded its captain Robert de Baudricourt to send her to the Dauphin at Chinon, where she arrived in February 1429 after an eleven-day ride in male dress. Charles received her, then sent her to Poitiers for a theological examination which ended in cautious approval.

Joan was given armour, a household, and a banner. She rode with a relief column to Orléans on 29 April 1429, broke the English siege within nine days, and on 18 June helped destroy John Talbot's army at the battle of Patay. The road to Reims opened; on 17 July 1429 Charles was anointed king in the cathedral with Joan beside the altar. An assault on Paris in September failed, she was wounded, and the dauphin's court began to lose interest. Captured by Burgundian troops at Compiègne on 23 May 1430, she was sold to the English for ten thousand livres.

The trial began at Rouen in February 1431 under Bishop Pierre Cauchon. Seventy charges shrank to twelve; she defended herself with peasant directness against the doctors of theology; her wearing of male clothing was the substantive issue at the end. Condemned as a relapsed heretic, she was burned in the Vieux-Marché on 30 May 1431, aged about nineteen. A rehabilitation trial in 1456 quashed the verdict; she was canonised by Pope Benedict XV in 1920.

The road continues to Charles VII of France.
