Harry Truman

33rd President of the United States, 1884–1972

A Missouri farmer’s son who commanded an artillery battery in the First World War and failed as a haberdasher before entering politics through the Kansas City machine, Truman rose to the United States Senate and, in 1945, to the vice-presidency. Barely three months later the death of Franklin Roosevelt thrust him into the White House, where he learned for the first time of the atomic bomb. Within months he took the decision to use it against Hiroshima and Nagasaki, ending the Second World War.

At Potsdam he met Churchill and Stalin to shape the postwar world, and as the wartime alliance curdled into the Cold War he set the course of American policy for a generation. The Truman Doctrine pledged support to nations resisting communism, the Marshall Plan rebuilt Western Europe, and his administration founded NATO, airlifted supplies into blockaded Berlin, and recognised the new state of Israel.

Plain-spoken and combative—“the buck stops here,” read the sign on his desk—he won a stunning upset re-election in 1948 against all predictions. His second term was consumed by the Korean War, during which he dismissed the immensely popular General MacArthur for defying civilian authority. He left office in 1953 with low ratings that history has steadily revised upward. He died in 1972, his reputation as a decisive everyman secure. The towering predecessor whose office he inherited was Franklin D. Roosevelt.
