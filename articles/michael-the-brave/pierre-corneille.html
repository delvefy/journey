Pierre Corneille

French dramatist, 1606–1684.

Born in Rouen to a well-to-do Norman family of lawyers, Corneille was educated by the Jesuits, took his licence in law, and bought a modest royal office in his native city — a clerkship at the Table de Marbre — which he held for the next twenty years and which set the steady provincial bourgeois beneath the heroic poet. His first comedy Mélite (1629), a Parisian success, drew him to the attention of Cardinal Richelieu, who placed him among the "Five Authors" he commissioned to dramatize his own outlines.

In December 1636 (or January 1637) the Théâtre du Marais produced Le Cid, drawn from the Spanish play of Guillén de Castro, and Paris was overturned. The drama of the young Rodrigue, torn between honour and love for Chimène whose father he must kill, ran for months to overflowing houses; the line "Nous partîmes cinq cents; mais par un prompt renfort..." passed at once into common French. The success provoked the "Quarrel of the Cid," in which Georges de Scudéry, Jean Chapelain, and the newly founded Académie française — under Richelieu's prompting — accused him of breaching the classical unities. He answered with the four great Roman tragedies that would set the form for the next half-century: Horace (1640), Cinna (1641), Polyeucte (1642), and Pompée (1643), built around the Stoic hero who masters passion by an act of will and grounded on the lives in Plutarch and the moral writings of the elder Romans.

Through the 1640s and 1650s he wrote tragedies, machine plays, and one masterpiece of comedy, Le Menteur. The career declined as the taste of Louis XIV's court moved towards the more passionate and pathetic manner of the rising Jean Racine, whose Andromaque (1667) and Britannicus (1669) directly contested Corneille's elder authority; the public preferred Racine, and Corneille's last play, Suréna (1674), was a quiet failure. He died in Paris in 1684, much honoured but poor; Louis XIV had to grant his widow a small pension. Voltaire's 1764 Commentaire on his works, published with a benefit edition for the destitute great-grand-niece Marie-Françoise Corneille whom he had taken into his Ferney household, restored him to the literary canon he has occupied ever since alongside Molière and Racine.
