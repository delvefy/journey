Frederick the Great

King of Prussia, 1712–1786 (reigned 1740–1786)

Frederick II of Prussia endured a brutal upbringing under his soldier-father, Frederick William I, who despised the boy’s love of music and French letters; an attempt to flee ended with Frederick forced to watch the execution of his friend. Yet on inheriting the throne in 1740 he proved a ruthless and brilliant ruler. Within months he seized the rich province of Silesia from the young Maria Theresa of Austria, igniting decades of conflict and announcing Prussia as a great power.

His military genius was tested to the utmost in the Seven Years’ War, when Prussia faced Austria, France, and Russia at once. Through victories such as Rossbach and Leuthen, and a stubborn refusal to surrender even near total ruin, he survived against overwhelming odds, his reputation as a commander secured. He later joined Austria and Russia in the partition of Poland, enlarging his kingdom further.

At home Frederick styled himself the first servant of the state, an enlightened ruler who reformed law and administration, promoted religious toleration, encouraged agriculture, and corresponded with the philosophers of the age, hosting Voltaire at his court before their friendship collapsed. A gifted flautist and composer, he built the elegant palace of Sanssouci as a retreat for music and conversation, and it was there that an aging Johann Sebastian Bach, summoned to play, received the royal theme that he wove into one of his last masterworks. Frederick left Prussia a disciplined, formidable state at the centre of German affairs.
