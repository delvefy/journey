Edmund Burke

Anglo-Irish statesman and political philosopher, 1729–1797

Born in Dublin and drawn to London as a young man of letters, Burke entered Parliament as a Whig and became one of the most celebrated orators of his age. He believed in measured, organic reform rather than abstract revolution, and brought that conviction to every great question of his time.

He won lasting fame as a friend of liberty within the established order. When George III’s ministers pressed taxation on the American colonies, Burke argued eloquently for conciliation, warning that empire could not be held by force against the consent of its people. He spent years prosecuting the impeachment of Warren Hastings for the abuses of British rule in India, and pressed for relief of Irish Catholics and reform of royal patronage.

Yet it was his response to events across the Channel that secured his place in the history of ideas. His Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) condemned the upheaval as a reckless assault on the slow-grown fabric of society; he mourned the humiliation of Marie Antoinette and prophesied that the chaos would end in tyranny and the rule of a military strongman. The book provoked a storm of replies, above all Thomas Paine’s Rights of Man, and shattered Burke’s long friendship with the radical Whig leader Charles James Fox. In defending tradition, prescription, and gradual change against revolutionary zeal, Burke became the founding voice of modern conservatism, his arguments echoing long after his death.
