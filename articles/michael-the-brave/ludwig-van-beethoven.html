Ludwig van Beethoven

German composer, 1770–1827

Beethoven was born in Bonn to a musical family and showed early gifts that his harsh father tried to exploit. As a young man he moved to Vienna, the musical capital of Europe, where he studied for a time with Joseph Haydn and quickly won fame as a brilliant pianist and improviser. Building on the classical style he inherited from Haydn and Mozart, he began to expand it with unprecedented power, scale, and emotional force.

From his late twenties he was afflicted by a growing deafness that drove him to despair and near-suicide, a crisis he recorded in an anguished letter to his brothers. Yet he resolved to live for his art, and his deafness, which eventually became total, did not stop him from composing his greatest works entirely in his imagination. He carried music from the classical era into the romantic, making it a vehicle for personal struggle and heroic affirmation.

His nine symphonies redefined the form: the Eroica, first conceived in honour of Napoleon before Beethoven angrily struck out the dedication on hearing he had made himself emperor; the fate-driven Fifth; and the choral Ninth, which set Schiller’s Ode to Joy in a vision of universal brotherhood. Alongside them stand his piano sonatas, string quartets, the opera Fidelio, and the Missa Solemnis. Revered in his own lifetime, he was mourned by a vast crowd at his funeral in Vienna, and he remains the very image of the artist as a heroic, suffering creator.
