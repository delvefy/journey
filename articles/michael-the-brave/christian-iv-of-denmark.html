Christian IV of Denmark

King of Denmark and Norway, 1577–1648

The son of Frederick II, Christian IV reigned for nearly sixty years, one of the longest reigns in European history, and his energetic personality stamped itself on his age. He took up the government of Denmark and Norway determined to make his kingdom a great power, and for a time he succeeded, drawing on the wealth of the Sound tolls and the silver of Norway to fund an ambitious programme of building, trade, and war.

He is remembered above all as a builder. He founded new towns—Kristiansand and Christianshavn among them, and the city of Christiania that grew into modern Oslo—and raised the great monuments that still define Copenhagen, including the Rosenborg Castle, the Round Tower, and the old stock exchange with its dragon-tail spire. He expanded the navy and chartered trading companies to reach toward Asia, giving Denmark a brief moment of commercial ambition.

His wars were less happy. As a Lutheran prince he entered the Thirty Years’ War to defend the Protestant cause and his own influence in northern Germany, but his army was beaten by the imperial forces and he was driven back, his prestige badly dented. Later, war with a rising Sweden cost Denmark territory and confirmed its slow decline before its neighbour. His sister Anne had married James VI and I, linking him to the British crown. When he died in 1648, he left a kingdom diminished in power but adorned with works that keep his memory green.
