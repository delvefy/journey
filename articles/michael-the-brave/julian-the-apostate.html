Julian the Apostate

Roman emperor, 331–363 AD

Julian was a nephew of Constantine the Great, born about 331. As a boy he survived the dynastic massacre of 337 that destroyed most of his family, and he was raised in watchful seclusion, steeped in Greek literature and philosophy. Drawn to Neoplatonism and the old gods, he secretly abandoned the Christianity in which he had been reared—earning him the name “the Apostate.”

In 355 his cousin the emperor Constantius II made him Caesar and sent him to defend Gaul. There Julian proved a brilliant and energetic commander: in 357 he routed a far larger Alemannic army at the Battle of Strasbourg and restored the Rhine frontier. His popularity with the troops alarmed Constantius, and when ordered to give up his best units, his soldiers proclaimed him Augustus at Paris in 360.

Civil war was averted only by the sudden death of Constantius in 361, which left Julian sole emperor. He threw off all concealment and launched a sweeping effort to restore paganism: reopening temples, reorganising pagan priesthoods on a Christian model, stripping privileges from the Church, barring Christians from teaching the classics, and even ordering the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem rebuilt to embarrass them. A prolific author, he poured out philosophical, satirical, and polemical works, among them the treatise Against the Galileans.

In 363 he invaded the Persian empire of Shapur II, marching deep into Mesopotamia to the walls of Ctesiphon. Unable to take the capital, he began a grinding retreat and was mortally wounded in a skirmish, dying at thirty-one. With him perished both the Constantinian dynasty and the last serious attempt to turn the empire back from Christianity.
