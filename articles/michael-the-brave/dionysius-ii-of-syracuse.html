Dionysius II of Syracuse

Tyrant of Syracuse, c. 397–343 BCE

Dionysius II was tyrant of Syracuse, the son and successor of the formidable Dionysius I, and a ruler chiefly remembered for the great opportunity he wasted. He inherited his father's vast power in 367 BCE while still young and untried, and at first allowed his uncle Dion and the philosopher Plato to attempt to mold him into a just and philosophic king.

The experiment collapsed. Pleasure-loving and suspicious, Dionysius grew jealous of Dion, exiled him, and let the chance of reform slip away. In 357 BCE Dion returned with an army and drove him from Syracuse; though Dionysius later clawed back a measure of power, his rule was now feared and hated.

His final fall came in 344 BCE, when the Corinthian general Timoleon arrived to free the Sicilian Greeks from tyranny and from Carthaginian pressure. Dionysius surrendered his citadel and gave himself up.

He was sent to live out his days in Corinth, where, by a famous and much-moralized tradition, the former master of the richest city in the Greek world was reduced to keeping a school—teaching boys their letters, men said, so that he might still lord it over someone. His decline became a stock example of the instability of fortune.
