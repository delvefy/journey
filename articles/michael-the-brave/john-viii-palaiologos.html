John VIII Palaiologos

Byzantine Emperor, last to seek Western union, 1392–1448

John was born at Constantinople on 18 December 1392, the eldest surviving son of Manuel II Palaiologos and Helena Dragaš. He was associated as co-emperor in 1408 and, from about 1421, was the effective ruler of an empire reduced to the city and its suburbs. He accompanied his father on the long Western tour of 1399–1403 that sought military aid against Bayezid I and watched the rescue come, in the end, from Timur and not from the courts of London and Paris. The years of his reign were a parallel exercise. He was crowned in his own right in 1425 on his father’s death, and almost his first act was to begin negotiations with the papal curia for a council that would heal the schism of 1054 in exchange for an army to lift the Ottoman siege from the city walls.

The Council of Ferrara-Florence convened in 1438 under Pope Eugenius IV, the Venetians and Florentines paying the bills. John brought with him the Patriarch Joseph II, twenty-two metropolitans, the philosopher Gemistos Plethon, the future cardinals Bessarion of Trebizond and Isidore of Kiev, and a court whose Eastern dignity and costume were captured for posterity by Pisanello in medals and by Benozzo Gozzoli, who put the emperor on horseback as the middle of the three Magi in the Medici chapel at Florence. On 6 July 1439 the bull Laetentur coeli proclaimed union; the dogmatic compromises — Filioque, purgatory, papal primacy — were signed by every Greek bishop except Mark of Ephesus, who turned and walked out, and who would be the hero of the anti-unionist majority back in Constantinople.

The crusade John’s subscription was meant to buy reached the Black Sea in 1444 and was destroyed at Varna. The Union of Florence was not received in the capital; the patriarch Joseph II had died at Florence and was succeeded by a series of anti-unionist Greeks; the people of Constantinople muttered that they preferred the sultan’s turban to the cardinal’s hat. John spent the four remaining years of his reign in his books and his grief — his beloved second wife Maria of Trebizond had died of plague in 1439 — and saw few foreigners. He died at Constantinople on 31 October 1448, leaving the empire and the broken union to his brother Constantine.
