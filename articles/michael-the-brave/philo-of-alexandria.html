Philo of Alexandria

Hellenistic Jewish philosopher, c.20 BC–c.50 AD

Philo was born about 20 BC into one of the wealthiest and most prominent Jewish families of Alexandria, the great Greek-speaking city of Egypt, and became the foremost philosopher of Hellenistic Judaism. Steeped in both the Hebrew scriptures and Greek learning, he devoted his life to showing that the two were one truth in different dress, reading the books of Moses through the lens of Plato and the Stoics by means of elaborate allegory in which the literal stories concealed deeper philosophical meanings.

At the centre of his thought stood the Logos, the divine Word or Reason that mediates between the transcendent God and the created world — an idea that would echo powerfully through later religious thought. So Greek was his temper that admirers later said either Plato philonizes or Philo platonizes.

His one securely dated public act came around the year 40, when violence against the Jews of Alexandria and the emperor Caligula’s demand to be honoured as a god drove the community to send Philo at the head of an embassy to Rome. He recorded the perilous mission, and the emperor’s contempt, in his Embassy to Gaius, and the persecution itself in another treatise; the historian Josephus also noted his leadership of the delegation. Though later Judaism set him aside, the Christians did not: his allegorical method and his Logos passed into the Alexandrian church, shaping Clement and Origen and, through them, the whole tradition of Christian Platonism. He died about the year 50.
