Jean-Paul Sartre

French philosopher, novelist, and playwright, 1905–1980

Jean-Paul Sartre was born in Paris on 21 June 1905. His father, a naval officer, died before he was two, and the boy grew up in the apartment of his maternal grandfather Charles Schweitzer, an Alsatian Germanist and an uncle of Albert Schweitzer. At the École Normale Supérieure he stood out among an exceptional generation that included Raymond Aron, Paul Nizan, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty, and there in 1929 he met Simone de Beauvoir, who would be his lifelong intellectual partner. He passed the agrégation that year at the head of the list and went off to teach philosophy in provincial lycées.

A year of study in Berlin, 1933–34, introduced him to the phenomenology of Husserl and to Heidegger’s Being and Time, the catalysts of his own thought. The novel La Nausée (1938) and the volume of stories Le Mur (1939) made his name. Mobilized at the outbreak of war, he was captured in June 1940 and spent nine months as a prisoner before release; back in Paris under the Occupation he taught, wrote, and operated on the fringes of the Resistance. In 1943, with German censors looking the other way, he published L’Être et le Néant — Being and Nothingness — an 800-page existentialist ontology that would define a postwar generation.

After the Liberation he founded the review Les Temps Modernes with Beauvoir and Merleau-Ponty and took up the public role he never relinquished. His plays — No Exit, The Flies, Dirty Hands — and his lecture “Existentialism is a Humanism” turned him into the most visible French intellectual of the century. He embraced Marxism in the 1950s, condemned Soviet repression but never quite the Soviet Union; quarrelled publicly with Camus, then with Merleau-Ponty; opposed the wars in Algeria and Vietnam; and in 1964 declined the Nobel Prize in Literature. Half-blind and frail, he died in Paris on 15 April 1980; the funeral cortège through Montparnasse drew fifty thousand mourners.
