John Maynard Keynes

English economist, 1883–1946

Keynes was born in Cambridge, the son of an academic, and educated at Eton and King’s College, where he was elected to the secretive Apostles and drawn into the artistic circle that became the Bloomsbury Group. Brilliant and many-sided, he combined a fellowship in economics with a career in the Treasury, shrewd speculation that made him rich, and lifelong patronage of the arts.

He first reached a wide public with The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), written after he resigned in protest from the British delegation at the Paris peace conference. Its warning that the crushing reparations imposed on Germany would destabilize all of Europe proved grimly prophetic and made him internationally famous. Through the 1920s he argued against the deflationary orthodoxy of the gold standard and a balanced budget.

His masterpiece, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), overturned the classical economics descended from Adam Smith. Keynes argued that economies could become stuck in prolonged slumps because total demand was too low, and that governments should counteract this through deliberate spending and public works rather than waiting for markets to right themselves. The doctrine reshaped policy across the democratic world and underlay the long postwar boom. Near the end of his life he helped design the Bretton Woods system of international finance. More than any other figure, Keynes founded modern macroeconomics.
