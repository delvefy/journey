John III Sobieski

King of Poland, victor of Vienna, 1629–1696

John III Sobieski was the soldier-king of Poland whose charge at Vienna turned back the last great Ottoman advance into the heart of Europe. Born in 1629 into a noble family with a tradition of arms, he learned war in the brutal mid-century struggles of the Commonwealth—against rebel Cossacks, against the Swedish “Deluge,” and above all against the Tatars and Turks of the south.

His skill raised him to grand hetman, the realm's chief commander, and his victories over the Ottomans, crowned by the great triumph at Khotyn in 1673, won him such fame that the nobles elected him king the following year.

His supreme hour came in 1683. The Ottoman grand vizier had laid siege to Vienna, the Habsburg capital, and its fall seemed near. Sobieski led the Polish army to join a Christian relief force, took overall command, and on the twelfth of September launched from the heights above the city the largest cavalry charge ever seen, his winged hussars at its head. The Ottoman army broke and fled; the siege was lifted. “We came, we saw, God conquered,” he wrote.

The victory made him the hero of Christendom, though the emperor whose city he had saved received him with cold ingratitude. At home his later years were frustrated by the quarrels of a nobility jealous of royal power. A cultivated man who adored his French wife and patronized the astronomer who named a constellation for him, he died in 1696. The emperor he had rescued at Vienna was Leopold I.
