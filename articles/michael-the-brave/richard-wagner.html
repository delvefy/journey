Richard Wagner

German composer and opera reformer, 1813–1883

Born in Leipzig, Wagner set out to remake opera into what he called the “total work of art,” a fusion of music, poetry, and drama in which every element served a single overwhelming vision. Writing his own librettos and binding his scores together with recurring musical motifs, he composed works of unprecedented scale and ambition—The Flying Dutchman, Tannhäuser, and Lohengrin—before the chromatic harmonies of Tristan und Isolde pushed Western music to the edge of a new age.

His involvement in the failed Dresden uprising of 1849 sent him into years of exile, during which he absorbed the philosophy of Schopenhauer and planned his vast four-opera cycle, Der Ring des Nibelungen. Rescued from his debts by the devoted young King Ludwig II of Bavaria, he eventually built his own theatre at Bayreuth to stage his works as sacred festivals, crowning his career with the cycle and the solemn Parsifal.

His private life was as outsized as his art: he married Cosima, the daughter of Franz Liszt, after a scandalous affair, and dazzled then alienated the young Nietzsche. His venomous antisemitic writings cast a long shadow and made him, decades after his death, a favourite of Hitler. Revolutionary and repellent by turns, he transformed music as few have done. He died in Venice in 1883. His father-in-law, and one of his earliest champions, was the pianist Franz Liszt.
