Herodian

Greek historian of Rome, c.170–240

Herodian was a Greek-speaking subject of the empire, probably of Syrian origin and a minor official, who in the early third century composed a History of the Empire from the Death of Marcus in eight books. It carried the story of Rome from the accession of Commodus down to the year 238, and for much of that span Herodian wrote as a contemporary, recording events he had seen or heard of at first hand.

His pages bring the turbulent age of the Severans to vivid life. He told of the murder of Commodus and the brief reign of Pertinax, of the auction of the empire and the wars by which Septimius Severus mastered it, of the fratricidal Caracalla, and of the strange, scandalous reign of the boy-priest Elagabalus. Writing in a dramatic, rhetorical Greek modelled on the classical historians, he aimed more at colour and moral effect than at precise accuracy, and where he can be checked against the sterner Cassius Dio he is often found wanting.

Yet for the events of his own later life — above all the overthrow of the last Severan and the rise of the towering soldier Maximinus Thrax, and the chaos of the year 238 — Herodian is a precious and sometimes the only witness. His history, rediscovered and translated by Renaissance humanists, remains one of the chief sources for the empire’s slide toward the great crisis of the third century.
