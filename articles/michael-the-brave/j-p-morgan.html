J.P. Morgan

American banker and financier, 1837–1913

John Pierpont Morgan was born in 1837 at Hartford, Connecticut, into a Yankee merchant family. His father Junius Spencer Morgan was a partner in the leading Anglo-American banking house of George Peabody in London, and Pierpont was educated to follow him—at the English High School in Boston, at the University of Göttingen for mathematics. He spent the Civil War in private banking on Wall Street, kept clear of front-line service through a paid substitute, and gradually built up the New York firm that became J. Pierpont Morgan and Company in 1895.

From the 1870s he was the leading American conduit for British capital into the post-bellum United States. He reorganised the broken Civil War-era railroads through what became known as "Morganisation": consolidating competitors, scaling rates, placing his own directors on the board, and disciplining managers to long-term profitability. He financed the merger of Edison's electric companies and Thomson-Houston into the General Electric Company in 1892, the merger of Carnegie Steel and other producers into the United States Steel Corporation in 1901—the first billion-dollar corporation in the world—and a long series of agricultural, shipping, and communications combinations.

In 1895 he personally led a syndicate that lent gold to the United States Treasury to save it from suspending specie payments, an action for which Grover Cleveland's administration was widely accused of selling out to him. In the Panic of 1907, with no Federal Reserve to act as lender of last resort, Morgan locked the heads of the major New York banks into his library on East 36th Street and refused to let them out until they had agreed a collective bailout that ended the crisis. The episode is generally credited with creating the political will that produced the Federal Reserve System six years later.

Theodore Roosevelt's Northern Securities suit of 1902 broke Morgan's railroad combination and signalled the limits of what the Republic would tolerate. He collected obsessively—books, manuscripts, Old Master drawings, Renaissance paintings, Egyptian antiquities—and his collections form the core of the Pierpont Morgan Library and substantial sections of the Metropolitan Museum of Art. He died in Rome in 1913. The earlier American transport magnate whose railroad consolidations Morgan inherited and reorganised, the founder of the Vanderbilt fortune, was Cornelius Vanderbilt.
