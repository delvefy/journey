Mary Wollstonecraft

English feminist philosopher and writer, 1759–1797

Self-educated and fiercely independent, Wollstonecraft escaped a brutal home to earn her own living as a governess, teacher, and finally a professional writer for the radical London publisher Joseph Johnson, whose circle drew together reformers, artists, and freethinkers, among them Thomas Paine and the young William Blake. There she forged a career almost without precedent for a woman of her time.

When Edmund Burke denounced the French Revolution, she answered at once with A Vindication of the Rights of Men (1790), defending the revolutionary ideals. Two years later came the book that made her name immortal, A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792). In it she argued that women appeared inferior to men only because they were denied education, and demanded that they be treated as rational beings and full citizens. It became the founding text of modern feminism.

Her private life defied convention as boldly as her writing. She lived in revolutionary Paris, bore a daughter out of wedlock, and twice attempted suicide over a faithless lover, before finding happiness with the philosopher William Godwin, whom she married while pregnant. In 1797 she gave birth to a second daughter, the future Mary Shelley, and died days later of childbed fever at thirty-eight. Her reputation suffered from Godwin’s frank memoir, but she is now honoured as a foundational thinker of women’s rights.
