Benjamin Franklin

American statesman, scientist, and printer, 1706–1790

Born in Boston, the fifteenth of seventeen children, Franklin was apprenticed to a printer and ran away to Philadelphia as a teenager. There he built a printing business, published the popular Poor Richard’s Almanack, and grew wealthy enough to retire early to a life of public service and inquiry. Largely self-taught, he embodied the practical optimism of the Enlightenment in the New World, founding a lending library, a fire company, a hospital, the American Philosophical Society, and the academy that became the University of Pennsylvania.

His experiments with electricity made him famous across Europe: he proposed that lightning was electrical, demonstrated it in his celebrated kite experiment, and invented the lightning rod, along with bifocal spectacles and an efficient stove. The honours that followed turned a colonial printer into an international celebrity of science.

From the 1750s Franklin spent long years in London as agent for several colonies, seeking reconciliation until he was publicly humiliated before the Privy Council and turned to the cause of independence. He helped Thomas Jefferson and others draft the Declaration of Independence, then sailed to France as the new nation’s envoy. His charm and reputation secured the decisive alliance of 1778 at the court of Louis XVI, where he was lionised and embraced the aged Voltaire before a Paris crowd. He helped negotiate the 1783 peace with Britain and, in his last public act, lent his prestige to the Constitutional Convention of 1787, the oldest delegate present.
