Claude Lorrain

French painter of ideal landscape, c.1604–1682

Claude Gellée, called Claude Lorrain after his native duchy, was born around 1604 in the village of Chamagne in Lorraine. Orphaned young, he made his way south to Rome as a teenaged kitchen boy or studio apprentice and never afterwards lived anywhere else. He studied with the landscape painter Agostino Tassi and absorbed the small atmospheric pictures of the German Adam Elsheimer, and he learned to draw out of doors in the Roman Campagna, sketching trees and ruins at dawn and at evening.

From the 1630s his Roman patrons included Pope Urban VIII and Philip IV of Spain, and a wide international clientele besides. To deter forgers he began the famous Liber Veritatis, a notebook of small drawn copies of every picture he sent out, which survived intact as a record of his entire career. His subjects were a few quiet ones repeated again and again: a port at sunrise or sunset with ships at anchor and small classical figures on the quay, a pastoral landscape with shepherds, a Biblical or Ovidian scene set in a luminous southern countryside.

Above all he was the painter of light. He composed his pictures so that the sun itself, low and golden, sits at or near the centre of the canvas, drawing the viewer's eye through deep, calibrated recessions of trees and water. The Embarkation of the Queen of Sheba and the Landscape with the Marriage of Isaac and Rebecca became models of the picturesque for every century after his own.

He died in Rome in 1682 a wealthy man, alongside his friend Nicolas Poussin. His ideal landscape passed through eighteenth-century connoisseurship to the English country house and to English painting, where his most passionate disciple was the painter who instructed in his will that two of his canvases be hung forever between two of Claude's at the National Gallery—J.M.W. Turner.
