Meister Eckhart

German Dominican mystic, c.1260–1328

Eckhart von Hochheim, known to posterity as Meister Eckhart, was born around 1260 in Thuringia and entered the Dominican Order as a young man. He studied and taught at Paris, where he twice held the Dominican chair of theology — the office once occupied by Thomas Aquinas — and rose to high rank in his order as provincial of Saxony. But it was as a preacher in the German tongue, to nuns and ordinary believers, that he found his true voice.

His sermons and treatises taught a daring mysticism of the soul’s union with God. He spoke of a divine “spark” in the depths of the soul, of the eternal birth of the Word within the believer, of a Godhead hidden beyond the named God, and of the detachment by which a person empties the self to make room for the divine. Drawing on Augustine and on the negative theology of Dionysius, he pressed language to its limits in paradox after paradox.

Such boldness drew suspicion. Late in his life his propositions were examined for heresy, and after his death Pope John XXII condemned a number of them, though Eckhart had already submitted himself to the Church’s judgment and professed his orthodoxy. Condemnation could not silence him: his thought lived on through his Dominican disciples and the Rhineland mystics, and centuries later philosophers from Hegel to Heidegger would return to him. He died about 1328, the boldest of the medieval mystics.
