Hippocrates

Greek physician, father of medicine, c. 460–c. 370 BC

Hippocrates was born on the Greek island of Kos and belonged, by tradition, to a guild of healers who traced their craft to the legendary Asclepius. Few firm facts of his life survive—much that is told of him is later legend—but he was a real and celebrated teacher in his own day, named with respect by Plato. To posterity he became simply the Father of Medicine.

His great achievement was to free the study of disease from superstition. Where others saw illness as the work of angry gods, the Hippocratic physicians insisted that maladies have natural causes and natural courses; a famous treatise argued that even epilepsy, the so-called sacred disease, was no more divine than any other ailment but a disorder of the brain. From this followed a method built on careful observation at the bedside, the recording of symptoms, attention to diet and regimen, and a trust in the healing power of nature.

The body of writings gathered under his name, the Hippocratic Corpus, became the foundation of Western medical learning, and with it the doctrine of the four humours whose balance was thought to govern health. His tradition also gave medicine its enduring ethical voice in the Hippocratic Oath, with its charge to help the sick and to do no harm—words still invoked by physicians today. Centuries later the great Galen would take up and systematise the Hippocratic legacy, carrying it down to the modern age.
