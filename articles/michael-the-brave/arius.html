Arius

Alexandrian priest and heresiarch, c. 256–336 AD

Arius was born around 256, probably in Libya, and became a popular and austere presbyter at the church of Baucalis in Alexandria. Around 318 he fell into open conflict with his bishop, Alexander, over the nature of Christ. Arius taught that the Son was not co-eternal with the Father but was the first and greatest of God's creatures, brought into being out of nothing—“there was a time when he was not.”

His teaching, easily summarised and even set to popular songs, spread rapidly and split the Eastern Church. Alexander condemned and excommunicated him, but Arius found powerful protectors among the bishops, above all Eusebius of Nicomedia. The dispute grew so bitter that the emperor Constantine, fearing for the unity of his realm, summoned the first ecumenical Council of Nicaea in 325 to settle it.

At Nicaea the assembled bishops, led against him by the young deacon Athanasius, rejected Arius's doctrine and adopted the Creed declaring the Son “of one substance” with the Father. Arius was condemned, his books burned, and he was driven into exile. Yet the controversy was far from finished.

Within a few years the political tide turned; Arius was partially rehabilitated, and Constantine himself ordered his readmission to communion. According to hostile accounts he died suddenly and grotesquely in Constantinople in 336, on the eve of his restoration. Though branded the arch-heretic, Arianism long outlived him, dominating the court for decades and, through missionaries such as Ulfilas, becoming the faith of many of the Germanic peoples who entered the empire.

The road continues to Eusebius of Nicomedia.
