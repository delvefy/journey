Antigonus I Monophthalmus

Macedonian general and Diadoch, 382–301 BCE

Antigonus, nicknamed the One-Eyed for an old battle wound, was one of Alexander the Great's senior officers, left by the king as satrap of Phrygia in Asia Minor during the great eastern campaign. When Alexander died in 323 BCE without a capable heir, Antigonus emerged, over the following years, as the most powerful of the warlords contending for the inheritance.

From his Asian base he sought to reunite the whole of Alexander's empire under his own hand. The aged regent Antipater had named him royal general in Asia to crush the loyalist Eumenes, a brilliant commander whom Antigonus duly defeated and put to death; he also drove Seleucus out of Babylon. By 306, after his son Demetrius won a great sea-battle over Ptolemy, Antigonus became the first of the successors to claim the title of king, and the others soon followed.

His very success united his rivals against him. Ptolemy of Egypt, Seleucus, Lysimachus of Thrace, and Cassander of Macedon formed a grand coalition to bring him down.

The reckoning came in 301 BCE at the Battle of Ipsus in Phrygia, where the allied armies, strengthened by Seleucus's Indian war elephants, overwhelmed the Antigonid forces. Antigonus, more than eighty years old, fell fighting on the field. His death shattered the last real hope of preserving Alexander's empire as a single realm and fixed its permanent division among the rival dynasties. Yet his line endured: through his son Demetrius, the Antigonids would rule Macedon for more than a century.
