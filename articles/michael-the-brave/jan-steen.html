Jan Steen

Dutch genre painter, 1626–1679

Jan Havickszoon Steen was born in 1626 in Leiden, the son of a brewer, and grew up among the city's prosperous burgher families and its university wits. He may have trained briefly under Nicolaes Knüpfer in Utrecht and afterwards in Haarlem; he entered the Leiden painters' guild in 1648 and the same year married Margriet van Goyen, daughter of the landscape painter Jan van Goyen. He lived by turns in The Hague, Warmond, Haarlem and finally back in Leiden, where in 1672 he took over a tavern as well as continuing to paint.

His subject was the Dutch household at its most disorderly. The Merry Family, The Drawing Lesson, As the Old Sing So Pipe the Young, The Doctor's Visit, the dozens of "easy household" pictures—a Dutch proverb still gives "a Jan Steen household" as the name for a chaotic one—crowd the canvas with eating, drinking, courting, laughing, fighting, drunken, and ill-behaved children. He painted himself, grinning, into many of them, glass in hand, looking out at the viewer.

Behind the comedy lay a steady Catholic moralism: the upset cup, the broken egg, the lurking dog were emblems of vanity, and the scenes had a sermon under them. Steen handled paint with a quick, witty touch, set figures in deep illusionistic spaces with an almost theatrical care, and had a feeling for facial character that few in his century matched.

He died in Leiden in 1679, having married twice, fathered eight children, and produced several hundred pictures. His Dutch contemporaries, above all the great quiet master of Delft, Johannes Vermeer, treated the bourgeois interior with utterly different gravity, but no painter inherited Steen's comic-moralising tradition more directly than the English satirist who turned it, a generation later, into a savage running commentary on London life—William Hogarth.
