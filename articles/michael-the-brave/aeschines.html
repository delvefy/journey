Aeschines

Athenian orator, 389–314 BCE

Aeschines rose from humble beginnings in Athens, working as a clerk and a stage actor before he found his true gift in public speaking and entered politics. He became one of the ten orators later canonised as masters of Attic eloquence, and the great rival of Demosthenes.

Where Demosthenes urged unyielding war against Philip II of Macedon, Aeschines argued for accommodation and peace. He took part in the embassy that negotiated the Peace of Philocrates with Philip in 346 BCE, and his conduct there became the subject of a furious prosecution by Demosthenes, which Aeschines narrowly survived.

Their feud reached its height in 330 BCE in the trial known as “On the Crown.” Aeschines challenged a proposal to award Demosthenes a public crown of honour, delivering a polished speech against the proposer Ctesiphon; Demosthenes answered with the most celebrated oration of antiquity. Aeschines was crushed, failing to win even a fifth of the jury's votes, and under the weight of the penalty he left Athens for good.

He withdrew to the island of Rhodes, where by tradition he founded a school of rhetoric and taught the art that had been his life. Three of his speeches survive, prized as models of clarity and force, and the contrast between his measured style and Demosthenes's fire long served teachers as the classic study in the art of persuasion.
