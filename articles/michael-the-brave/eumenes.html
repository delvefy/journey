Eumenes

Greek general of the Diadochi, c. 362–316 BCE

Eumenes of Cardia was the most remarkable of Alexander the Great's successors—a Greek among Macedonians, a former secretary who proved himself one of the finest generals of the age. Born around 362 BCE in the Greek town of Cardia, he served as chief secretary first to Philip II and then to Alexander, keeping the royal records through the long campaign in Asia.

After Alexander's death his administrative skill gave way to unexpected military genius. Loyal to the regent Perdiccas and to the ideal of a united empire under Alexander's heirs, he was given charge of Asia Minor. There in 321 BCE he won a stunning victory over the invading marshals Craterus and Neoptolemus; both fell in the battle, Craterus—one of the most admired of all Macedonian commanders—trampled in the rout.

But the death of Perdiccas left Eumenes isolated, condemned as a public enemy by the other successors. For years he waged a brilliant defensive war across the East against the most powerful of them, Antigonus the One-Eyed, holding together a fractious army that included the formidable veteran corps known as the Silver Shields.

His undoing came not in battle but through treachery: after an indecisive clash at Gabiene in 316 BCE, the Silver Shields, to recover their captured baggage and families, surrendered Eumenes to Antigonus, who reluctantly put him to death. Plutarch held him up as proof that ability and loyalty could not always prevail against birth and brute power.
