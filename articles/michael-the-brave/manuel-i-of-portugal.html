Manuel I of Portugal

“The Fortunate,” king of Portugal, 1469–1521

Manuel was born at Alcochete on 31 May 1469, the ninth child of Ferdinand, Duke of Viseu, a cadet of the House of Aviz. Nothing in his birth suggested kingship. His brother-in-law and cousin John II reigned with a hand so sure that successive heirs vanished—the king’s only legitimate son Afonso killed in a fall from his horse in 1491, his bastard Jorge passed over—until, on John II’s sudden death in October 1495, the crown fell to the gentle, scholarly Duke of Beja. He took the throne at twenty-six and reigned for almost twenty-six years.

Two decisions in the first eighteen months of the reign defined it. In 1497, as the price of his marriage to Isabella of Aragon—daughter of the Catholic Monarchs and widow of Prince Afonso—he agreed to expel the Jews and Muslims from Portugal. The mass forced baptism that followed in Lisbon, designed to keep the artisans without keeping the religion, produced the New Christian community whose persecution would shadow the country for centuries. The same year he commissioned Vasco da Gama’s voyage to India. The next twenty years brought Bartolomeu Dias’s Cape, Cabral’s Brazil and India, Tristão da Cunha’s island, and the conquests of Afonso de Albuquerque from Hormuz to Goa to Malacca. Manuel called himself, with no exaggeration, Senhor da Conquista, Navegação e Comércio da Etiópia, Arábia, Pérsia e Índia.

The wealth came home in galleons. He spent it on the late Gothic stonework that took his name—the Jerónimos Monastery at Belém, the Torre de Belém, the windows of Tomar—on embassies to the Pope bearing white elephants, and on a tightening grip over Portuguese law and administration. He was a poor judge of subordinates: he quarrelled with Albuquerque on his deathbed, refused to raise the small pay of the obscure soldier Fernão de Magalhães, and watched him leave for Spain in 1517 to propose the westward circumnavigation. After two Spanish marriages produced ten children, his third, to Eleanor of Austria in 1518, gave him only two. He died at Lisbon on 13 December 1521, ten months before Magellan’s surviving ship returned to Seville.
