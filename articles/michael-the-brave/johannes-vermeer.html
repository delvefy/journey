Johannes Vermeer

Dutch Golden Age painter, 1632–1675

Vermeer spent his whole quiet life in the city of Delft, where he was born, married into a Catholic family, and worked as both a painter and a dealer in art. He produced very slowly—only some three dozen pictures survive—and he was little known beyond his town, yet within that small output he achieved a perfection of light, colour, and stillness unmatched in Western painting.

His subjects were the calm interiors of Dutch homes: a woman reading a letter at a window, a girl pouring milk, a lacemaker bent over her work, all bathed in a soft, even daylight rendered with miraculous delicacy. The luminous Girl with a Pearl Earring and the radiant cityscape View of Delft are among the best-loved images in art. His uncanny optical precision has led many to believe he studied his scenes with a camera obscura.

The French invasion of 1672 wrecked the Dutch art market and Vermeer’s finances with it. He died suddenly at forty-three, deep in debt and leaving a widow and many children; the scientist Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, his fellow citizen of Delft, was appointed to administer the bankrupt estate. For two centuries Vermeer was all but forgotten, until nineteenth-century critics rediscovered him and raised him to his present place among the supreme masters.
