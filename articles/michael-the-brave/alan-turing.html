Alan Turing

English mathematician and founder of computer science, 1912–1954

Alan Mathison Turing was born in 1912 in Maida Vale, London, the second son of a colonial civil servant in India. A solitary child of unusual mathematical gift, he was sent to Sherborne School in Dorset and went up to King's College, Cambridge in 1931. He took a Smith's Prize in 1936 with the paper that founded theoretical computer science—"On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem"—which gave a negative answer to Hilbert's decision problem by defining a Universal Turing Machine and proving that there exist mathematical problems no such machine can solve.

He took a doctorate under Alonzo Church at Princeton, then returned to Britain on the eve of war. From September 1939 he was at the Government Code and Cypher School at Bletchley Park, charged with breaking the German Enigma cipher. He designed the electromechanical Bombe that mechanised the breaking, and led the cracking of the more difficult naval Enigma whose intercepts probably shortened the Atlantic war by years. His work, classified for decades, was unknown to the public in his lifetime; Winston Churchill afterwards called his cryptanalysts "the geese that laid the golden eggs and never cackled."

After the war Turing helped to design the ACE computer at the National Physical Laboratory and then, at Manchester, the first true stored-program machines. His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed the imitation game now called the Turing test, opening the field of artificial intelligence. His last papers introduced the mathematical theory of morphogenesis, explaining how patterns emerge in biological growth.

In 1952 he was prosecuted for "gross indecency" with another man and accepted chemical castration in place of prison. He died in 1954 at Wilmslow of cyanide poisoning, almost certainly self-inflicted, an apparent half-eaten apple beside his bed. A formal British government apology and posthumous royal pardon followed only decades later. The Austrian logician whose incompleteness theorems of 1931 had set the stage for Turing's "On Computable Numbers" and rewritten the foundations of mathematics was Kurt Gödel.
