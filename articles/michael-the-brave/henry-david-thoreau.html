Henry David Thoreau

American naturalist and essayist, 1817–1862

Henry David Thoreau was born on 12 July 1817 in Concord, Massachusetts, the town he would scarcely leave and never tire of. After Harvard he tried schoolteaching, worked in his family’s pencil-making business, and took up land-surveying, but his real vocation was observation — of nature, of conscience, of himself. Drawn into the orbit of Ralph Waldo Emerson, who became mentor, employer and friend, he absorbed the Transcendentalist conviction that truth is found through direct experience rather than received authority.

In 1845 he built a small cabin on Emerson’s woodlot at Walden Pond and lived there for two years, “to front only the essential facts of life.” The record of that experiment, Walden (1854), became a classic meditation on simplicity, self-sufficiency and the natural world. While at the pond he spent a night in the Concord jail for refusing to pay a poll tax to a government that upheld slavery and waged war on Mexico; from that protest grew his essay “Civil Disobedience,” arguing that the individual must not lend himself to injustice.

A fierce abolitionist, Thoreau lectured in defence of John Brown when others recoiled. He filled thousands of journal pages with precise natural observation that later generations would recognize as pioneering ecology, and he visited the poet Walt Whitman in Brooklyn, admiring the raw new voice of Leaves of Grass. Tuberculosis cut his life short on 6 May 1862, at forty-four. Yet his small essay on conscience travelled the world: Leo Tolstoy praised it, Mahatma Gandhi made it a cornerstone of nonviolent resistance, and Martin Luther King Jr. traced his own philosophy of protest back to it.
