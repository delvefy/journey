Johannes Kepler

German astronomer and mathematician, 1571–1630

Kepler was born in 1571 in Weil der Stadt, a small free city in Württemberg, into a downwardly mobile Lutheran family. A scholarship took him to the University of Tübingen, where his teacher Michael Maestlin quietly introduced him to the heliocentric theory of Copernicus. After graduation he was sent, against his own preference for the ministry, to teach mathematics at the Protestant school in Graz. There, in 1596, he published the Mysterium Cosmographicum, a youthful attempt to derive the spacing of the planets from the nested Platonic solids.

Religious persecution in Habsburg-ruled Styria drove him from Graz, and in 1600 he joined Tycho Brahe at the imperial court in Prague. Tycho, jealous of his data, died unexpectedly in 1601, and Kepler inherited both the post of imperial mathematician under Rudolf II and the most accurate planetary observations in Europe. Working chiefly on Mars, he discovered that the planet’s orbit was not a circle but an ellipse with the Sun at one focus, and that a line joining the planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. These first two laws appeared in the Astronomia Nova of 1609. The third—that the square of a planet’s period is proportional to the cube of its mean distance—was published in the Harmonices Mundi of 1619.

His later years were harried. He defended his mother in a long witchcraft trial in Württemberg, lost his patron when Rudolf was deposed, and saw his Linz home overrun by Counter-Reformation troops. He completed the Rudolphine Tables in 1627 under the protection of the warlord Albrecht von Wallenstein. In November 1630, travelling to collect arrears of pay, he died of a fever in Regensburg. His three laws became the bridge between Copernicus and Newton.
