Walt Whitman

American poet, 1819–1892

Walt Whitman was born on 31 May 1819 on a farm at West Hills, Long Island, and grew up in working-class Brooklyn. With little formal schooling, he learned the printer’s trade and drifted through journalism, editing the Brooklyn Eagle and writing conventional verse and fiction that gave no hint of what was coming. In 1855 he published, at his own expense, a thin volume called Leaves of Grass — twelve untitled poems in a sprawling, unrhymed free verse that sang of the self, the body, democracy and the teeming life of America.

The book bewildered most readers, but Ralph Waldo Emerson recognized its power at once, writing to greet its author “at the beginning of a great career.” Whitman spent the rest of his life revising and enlarging it through edition after edition. When civil war came he went to Washington and served as a volunteer nurse, sitting with thousands of wounded and dying soldiers, an experience that produced the war poems of Drum-Taps.

He revered Abraham Lincoln, and the President’s assassination drew from him two of the most enduring elegies in the language, “O Captain! My Captain!” and “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d.” A stroke left him partly disabled, and he settled in Camden, New Jersey, as the “Good Gray Poet,” receiving pilgrims who came to honour him — among them Oscar Wilde, who called on him in 1882, and Henry David Thoreau, who had visited the younger poet years before. Whitman died in Camden on 26 March 1892, having reshaped what poetry in English could sound like and do.
