John von Neumann

Hungarian-American mathematician and polymath, 1903–1957

János von Neumann was born in 1903 in Budapest into a wealthy banking family, one of the cohort of brilliant Hungarian Jews—Wigner, Szilárd, Teller, Erdös—sometimes called the Martians. His prodigy was famous from boyhood: he was said to have memorised pages of the phone book on sight and to have done calculus at six. He took simultaneous degrees in chemical engineering at Zürich and in mathematics at Budapest, with a doctoral thesis on set theory whose axiom of regularity is still used.

His twenties produced a torrent. He gave the first rigorous mathematical foundation of quantum mechanics in Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics, using Hilbert spaces and operator algebras; he founded the field of operator rings (now von Neumann algebras); and with Oskar Morgenstern he founded the modern theory of games and economic behaviour. He invented the minimax theorem and the technical machinery of cooperative bargaining. He was appointed to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton in 1933, alongside Einstein and Gödel, and remained there for the rest of his life.

From 1943 he was indispensable to the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos. He worked out the implosion lens geometry of the plutonium bomb, designed shock-wave computations, and ran the numerical simulations that the chemistry and the physics could not solve. Robert Oppenheimer relied on him constantly. After the war he became the central architect of the modern electronic computer: his 1945 "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC" laid out the stored-program von Neumann architecture that almost every computer still uses, and his Institute machine at Princeton became the model for a generation of post-war computers.

He worked on the hydrogen bomb under Edward Teller, served on the Atomic Energy Commission, and pioneered cellular automata as a theory of self-reproducing machines. He died of cancer in 1957, having been a Catholic convert in his last years. Of his rivals and friends in the founding of computing, the British logician who had asked the same question of mechanical computation from a different angle, defining the universal machine in 1936, was Alan Turing.
