Albert Einstein

German-born theoretical physicist, 1879–1955

Born in Ulm and raised in Munich, Einstein was an unremarkable student by reputation and, unable to find an academic post, took work as a clerk in the Swiss patent office. From that obscurity, in his “miracle year” of 1905, he published papers that transformed physics: explaining the photoelectric effect in terms of light quanta, accounting for Brownian motion, and setting out the special theory of relativity, with its famous equivalence of mass and energy, E = mc².

A decade later he completed the general theory of relativity, recasting gravity as the curvature of spacetime and overturning the universe of Isaac Newton. When a 1919 eclipse expedition confirmed that starlight bent around the sun as he had predicted, he became overnight the most famous scientist in the world. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1921, though he never accepted the randomness of the new quantum mechanics, insisting in long debates with Niels Bohr that God “does not play dice.”

A Jew and a pacifist, he fled Nazi Germany in 1933 for Princeton. In 1939 he signed a letter warning President Roosevelt that an atomic bomb might be built, helping to spur the Manhattan Project he would later regret. He devoted his last years to a fruitless search for a unified theory and to the cause of peace, joining Bertrand Russell in warning against nuclear war. He died in 1955, his name a synonym for genius. Among his admired contemporaries was the pioneer of radioactivity, Marie Curie.
