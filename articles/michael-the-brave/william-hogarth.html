William Hogarth

English painter and satirical engraver, 1697–1764

William Hogarth was born in 1697 in Smithfield, London, the son of an unsuccessful Latin teacher whose debts landed the family in the Fleet prison. The early experience of debtors' London marked him for life. Apprenticed to a silver engraver, he taught himself painting and history-piece composition and, in 1729, eloped with Jane, the daughter of the court painter Sir James Thornhill.

His ambition was a wholly new kind of picture, what he called "modern moral subjects." Painted and then engraved as serial prints sold by subscription, A Harlot's Progress in 1732 and A Rake's Progress in 1735 followed the downfall of an ordinary protagonist through six or eight tableaux of contemporary London with the comic-tragic detail of a novel. Marriage A-la-Mode of 1745 turned the same form on aristocratic adultery; Industry and Idleness and the savage Beer Street and Gin Lane in 1751 carried it into civic argument about cheap spirits and the poor.

He kept up at the same time a vigorous career as portraitist—The Shrimp Girl, Captain Coram, the great group portrait of his servants, and the Garrick as Richard III commemorating his actor friend David Garrick. His Analysis of Beauty of 1753, a theoretical treatise built around the serpentine "line of beauty," is the first major work of English art theory. He was Sergeant Painter to the King and a founder of the Foundling Hospital, where he hung his pictures to draw the public into a new sort of charitable exhibition.

He worked tirelessly to pass the 1735 Engravers' Copyright Act, the so-called "Hogarth's Act," to protect printmakers from piracy. He died at Leicester Fields in 1764. His comic-moral seriality fed the cartoonists Gillray, Rowlandson and Cruikshank, and the great London novelist whose own running portraits of vice, charity, and chaos run closest in spirit to Hogarth's prints—and who counted him a personal friend—was Henry Fielding.
