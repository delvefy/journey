John Wycliffe

English theologian and reformer, c.1320–1384

Wycliffe was born about 1320 in the Yorkshire parish of Hipswell, in lands held by his family in the lordship of Richmond. He was at Oxford from about 1345, moving among the secular colleges — Merton, Balliol, and Queen's — and took his Master of Arts in 1361, the same year he was elected Master of Balliol. He held a string of livings (Fillingham, Ludgershall, finally Lutterworth in Leicestershire) almost entirely through royal patronage, and proceeded slowly to his doctorate in theology in 1372. By then he was already the most formidable schoolman in England and a publicly favoured client of the king's son John of Gaunt, who had use for an Oxford theologian willing to argue for royal authority over a worldly church.

In 1374 the Crown sent him to a conference at Bruges on papal provisions and taxation, and from there he turned increasingly to public controversy. His Latin treatises De dominio divino and De civili dominio of 1375–76 argued that all lordship is forfeit by mortal sin and that wicked clergy could lawfully be deprived by the secular power — explosive doctrine in a kingdom whose church owned a third of the land. Pope Gregory XI issued five bulls against him in May 1377; in February 1378 Gaunt and Princess Joan of Kent protected him in person at the trial at Lambeth. The Great Schism that broke later that year — two rival popes — wrecked his already shaky belief in the papacy itself.

From 1379 he denied transubstantiation in his De eucharistia, an attack the universities and the king's uncle could not protect him from. Archbishop Courtenay condemned twenty-four of his propositions at the Blackfriars Earthquake Council in May 1382, and Wycliffe retired to Lutterworth. There he and his Oxford disciples, with John Purvey and Nicholas of Hereford, organised the first full translation of the Vulgate Bible into English. A stroke struck him at the altar on 28 December 1384 and he died three days later. The Council of Constance condemned him in 1415; in 1428 his bones were exhumed at Lutterworth, burned, and the ashes thrown into the river Swift.

The road continues to John of Gaunt.
