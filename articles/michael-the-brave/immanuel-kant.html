Immanuel Kant

German philosopher, 1724–1804

Kant spent his entire life in the Prussian city of Königsberg, where he taught at the university and lived by habits so regular that his neighbours were said to set their clocks by his afternoon walk. From this quiet existence came one of the most far-reaching revolutions in the history of philosophy. Roused, as he put it, from his “dogmatic slumber” by the sceptical arguments of David Hume, and inspired in his view of human dignity by Rousseau, he set out to determine the powers and the limits of human reason itself.

In his great Critique of Pure Reason he argued that the mind does not merely receive the world but actively shapes experience through its own forms of space, time, and understanding; we can know things only as they appear to us, not as they are in themselves. This “Copernican revolution” sought to secure the certainty of science, including the physics of Newton, while marking the boundaries beyond which knowledge cannot reach.

His moral philosophy was equally influential. He held that morality rests on reason alone, expressed in the categorical imperative, the command to act only on principles one could will to become universal laws, treating humanity always as an end and never merely as a means. In further works he examined beauty, purpose, religion, and the prospects for perpetual peace among nations, and in a famous essay he defined enlightenment as the courage to use one’s own understanding. Nearly all later Western philosophy has had to reckon with his thought.
