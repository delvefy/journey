Jane Austen

1775–1817, English novelist

Jane Austen was born at Steventon rectory in Hampshire, the seventh of eight children of a parish clergyman who took in boys as boarders to supplement his living. Her education was largely from her father's substantial library and her own omnivorous reading; the Juvenilia, written in her teens, parody the sentimental novels of the day with a mature comic ear. Her closest companion through life was her elder sister Cassandra, with whom she lived almost without separation.

By her early twenties she had drafted the books later issued as Sense and Sensibility, Pride and Prejudice and Northanger Abbey. A first publisher accepted Northanger Abbey for ten pounds in 1803 and then shelved it. The family's removal to Bath in 1801, her father's death in 1805, and unsettled years in Southampton interrupted her writing entirely. Only the move in 1809 to Chawton, a cottage on her brother Edward's estate, restored the conditions under which she could work.

From Chawton she published Sense and Sensibility in 1811, Pride and Prejudice in 1813, Mansfield Park in 1814, and Emma in 1815, each anonymously, as “by a Lady” or “by the author of Pride and Prejudice.” Walter Scott, reviewing Emma in the Quarterly Review, praised her “exquisite touch” with the everyday surface of provincial life. She admired Samuel Johnson and the earlier novels of Samuel Richardson, but matched neither for compression of dialogue or sharpness of free indirect style.

From 1816 she suffered the symptoms now usually identified with Addison's disease. She finished Persuasion in the autumn of that year, began a new novel later called Sanditon, and died at Winchester in July 1817 at forty-one. Persuasion and Northanger Abbey appeared together posthumously. Her novels passed in the next century into the central canon of English fiction; Virginia Woolf, Leo Tolstoy and a long line of readers since have written about her with the kind of attention given to few of her contemporaries.
