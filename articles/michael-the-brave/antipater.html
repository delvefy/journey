Antipater

Macedonian regent, c. 397–319 BCE

Antipater was a veteran general and statesman who had served Philip II of Macedon and won his deep trust. When Philip's son Alexander set out to conquer the Persian Empire in 334 BCE, he left Antipater behind as regent of Macedon and overseer of Greece, charged with holding the home kingdom and keeping the restless Greek cities in check.

He discharged the duty with iron firmness. In 331 he crushed a revolt led by the Spartan king Agis III at the Battle of Megalopolis. Throughout Alexander's long absence he managed the affairs of Macedon, though he quarrelled bitterly with the king's mother, Olympias, whose interference he resented.

When news came of Alexander's death in 323, the southern Greeks, led by Athens, rose against Macedonian domination in the Lamian War. Hard pressed at first, Antipater rallied, won the decisive Battle of Crannon in 322, and broke the revolt. He occupied Athens, ended its democracy, and demanded the surrender of the anti-Macedonian orators; rather than be taken, the great Demosthenes drank poison.

In the scramble among Alexander's successors, Antipater rose to become regent of the entire empire after the fall of Perdiccas in 321. But he was old, and on his death in 319 he passed the regency not to his ambitious son Cassander but to the officer Polyperchon, a choice that plunged the empire into fresh civil war. A cultured man, he was a lifelong friend of Aristotle, who named him executor of his will.
