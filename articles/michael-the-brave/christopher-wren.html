Christopher Wren

English architect and scientist, 1632–1723

Wren first made his name not in architecture but in science. A prodigy in mathematics and astronomy at Oxford, he became a founding member and later president of the Royal Society, a friend and colleague of Robert Hooke, and an investigator whose geometrical work even Isaac Newton praised. It was a coffee-house wager among Wren, Hooke, and Edmond Halley about the laws of planetary motion that helped set Newton on the path to his Principia.

The catastrophe of the Great Fire of London in 1666 turned Wren to building. Charged with raising the gutted city from its ashes, he designed more than fifty parish churches and, above them all, the new St Paul’s Cathedral, crowned by one of the greatest domes in Europe and built over more than three decades. His genius also produced the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, the Royal Hospital at Chelsea, the library of Trinity College, Cambridge, and much else.

As Surveyor of the King’s Works he served Charles II and his successors, shaping the very face of London and defining the English Baroque. Knighted and long-lived, he was buried in his own cathedral beneath a famous epitaph: Lector, si monumentum requiris, circumspice—Reader, if you seek his monument, look around you.
