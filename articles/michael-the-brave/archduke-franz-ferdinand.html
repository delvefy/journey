Archduke Franz Ferdinand

Heir to Austria-Hungary, 1863–1914

Archduke Franz Ferdinand was the heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary whose murder set off the First World War. Born in 1863, he became heir presumptive after the suicide of Crown Prince Rudolf and the death of his own father, standing next in line to his aged uncle, the Emperor Franz Joseph.

His private life defied the court. He fell in love with Sophie Chotek, a noblewoman of rank too low for a Habsburg bride, and to marry her in 1900 he had to accept a morganatic union: she could share neither his titles nor his throne, and their children were excluded from the succession. The slight rankled for the rest of his life.

In politics he was a difficult, reform-minded man who saw that the creaking empire of Germans and Hungarians could not endure as it was. He toyed with schemes to raise the empire's Slavs to equal standing—ideas that frightened Hungarian grandees and enraged Serbian nationalists, who dreamed instead of gathering those Slavs into a Greater Serbia.

On the twenty-eighth of June 1914, visiting the Bosnian capital of Sarajevo, he and Sophie were shot dead in their open car. The assassination triggered the July Crisis: an ultimatum to Serbia, the locking of alliance against alliance, and within weeks a general war that would consume four empires, his own among them. The youth whose shots began it all was the Bosnian Serb Gavrilo Princip.
