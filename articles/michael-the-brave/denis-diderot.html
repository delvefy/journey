Denis Diderot

French philosophe and editor of the Encyclopédie, 1713–1784

Denis Diderot was born in 1713 at Langres in Champagne, the son of a master cutler. Schooled by the Jesuits and at the University of Paris, he disappointed his father by refusing the priesthood and the law, and lived for years in the bohemian poverty of the Paris quartiers, scraping by as a tutor, a translator, and a pseudonymous compiler of erotic books. He was imprisoned briefly at the Château de Vincennes in 1749 for atheist materialism in his Lettre sur les aveugles.

In the same year a Paris bookseller invited him to edit a French translation of Ephraim Chambers's English Cyclopaedia. The project grew, under Diderot and his friend the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert, into the Encyclopédie—twenty-eight folio volumes of text and plates published from 1751 to 1772, hounded by the censor, abandoned by d'Alembert at the height of the persecution, and finished by Diderot virtually single-handed. It collected the science, industry, philosophy, and crafts of the age into the manifesto of the Enlightenment.

Beyond the Encyclopédie he wrote tirelessly across forms—the bourgeois drama Le Fils naturel, the philosophical novels Jacques the Fatalist and The Nun, the dialogue Rameau's Nephew (which Goethe translated and Hegel made famous), Salon reviews that founded modern art criticism, and after Richardson's death the rapturous Éloge that fixed Clarissa in European letters. He carried on a thirty-year quarrel with Rousseau and a long collegial debate with Voltaire.

In 1765 Catherine the Great of Russia bought his library to settle his debts, leaving him its librarian for life, and in 1773 brought him to St Petersburg for a celebrated five-month visit of philosophical conversation. He died in Paris in 1784, his most personal writings unpublished. The mathematician and philosophe who had begun the Encyclopédie at his side, the supreme synthetic mind of the early Enlightenment, was Jean le Rond d'Alembert.
