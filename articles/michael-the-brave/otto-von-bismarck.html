Otto von Bismarck

Prussian statesman, first Chancellor of Germany, 1815–1898

A conservative Junker landowner of iron will and supple cunning, Bismarck was appointed minister-president of Prussia in 1862 by King Wilhelm I amid a constitutional deadlock. He declared that the great questions of the day would be settled not by speeches and majorities but by “blood and iron,” and over the next decade he made good the boast, uniting the German states under Prussian leadership through three deliberately engineered wars.

He defeated Denmark, then shattered Austria’s claim to German leadership at Königgrätz in 1866, and finally provoked and crushed the France of Napoleon III in 1870–71, capturing the emperor at Sedan. In the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles he had Wilhelm I proclaimed German Emperor, the new Reich born from his statecraft. As its first chancellor he then turned to preserving what he had won, weaving an intricate system of alliances to keep France isolated and Europe at peace, while at home he battled the Catholic Church and the socialists, even introducing pioneering social-insurance laws to undercut them.

For twenty years the “Iron Chancellor” dominated European diplomacy. But the accession of the headstrong young Kaiser Wilhelm II brought conflict, and in 1890 the old statesman was forced to resign. He retired to his estates embittered, warning of the dangers ahead, and died in 1898, the founder of modern Germany.
