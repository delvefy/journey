Ronald Reagan

1911–2004, fortieth President of the United States

Ronald Wilson Reagan was born above a shop in Tampico, Illinois, the younger son of a shoe salesman of Irish Catholic descent and a devout Disciples of Christ mother. He worked his way through Eureka College, broadcast Chicago Cubs games over WHO Des Moines from a teletype ticker, and in 1937 signed a Hollywood contract with Warner Brothers. Over twenty years he appeared in some fifty films, served stateside in the Army Air Forces, and from 1947 served six terms as president of the Screen Actors Guild, where his anti-Communism hardened and his New Deal Democratic politics began to migrate.

From 1954 he hosted General Electric Theater on CBS and gave the company's plant tours that became the dry run for his political style. A late televised address for Barry Goldwater in October 1964 brought him to national notice. He was elected governor of California in 1966, served eight years, and after twice falling short of the Republican nomination defeated President Jimmy Carter in November 1980.

His administration cut marginal income-tax rates, accelerated military spending, and in his first term saw the worst recession since the 1930s give way to a long expansion. He survived the assassination attempt of John Hinckley Jr. in March 1981 with his style of self-deprecation intact. The Strategic Defense Initiative provoked a Soviet response neither side could afford, and after summits at Geneva, Reykjavik, Washington and Moscow he signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty in 1987 with Mikhail Gorbachev, the first treaty to abolish a class of nuclear weapons. He worked closely with Margaret Thatcher and corresponded discreetly with Pope John Paul II on the Polish struggle.

The Iran–Contra affair of 1986–87 nearly broke the second term; congressional hearings established that arms had been sold to Iran and the proceeds diverted to the Nicaraguan Contras. He left office in January 1989 with high approval ratings, retired to his Bel Air home and his ranch above Santa Barbara, and in 1994 announced in a handwritten letter to the American people that he had been diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease. He died of pneumonia ten years later at ninety-three.

The road continues to Pope John Paul II.
