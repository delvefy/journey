Edgar Degas

French painter and sculptor, 1834–1917

Edgar Degas was born in Paris in 1834 into a banking family and trained first in law before turning to the École des Beaux-Arts and a long apprenticeship in classical draughtsmanship. He travelled through Italy in his twenties, copied tirelessly in the Louvre and the great Italian collections, and announced himself with severe portraits and historical pictures in the manner of Ingres, whom he revered above all other masters.

In the 1860s he came under the orbit of Édouard Manet at the Café Guerbois and turned to modern subjects: the racecourse, the orchestra pit, the laundress at her iron, the milliner at her counter. He was one of the founders of the Impressionist exhibitions of the 1870s, though he disliked the label and refused plein-air painting on principle. His true subject became the body in motion under artificial light, above all the dancer—the ballerina at the barre, behind the curtain, exhausted between rehearsals. His pastels and his small wax sculptures of dancers and bathers redefined what the medium could carry.

He was a difficult, conservative, and increasingly reclusive man. He quarrelled spectacularly with Manet, broke off most of his friendships during the Dreyfus Affair, on which he took a virulent anti-Dreyfusard line, and ended his life nearly blind, modelling in wax in a darkened studio. He died in 1917.

His severe drawing and his cut, photographic framing of urban subjects shaped Toulouse-Lautrec, Mary Cassatt and a generation of moderns; Picasso, in old age, returned obsessively to his brothel monotypes. But beyond his idol Ingres there stood, for Degas, one further Romantic master whose colour and notebooks he collected and copied with a kind of awe—Eugène Delacroix.
