Tutankhamun

Pharaoh of Egypt, c.1341–1323 BC

Tutankhamun, the boy king of Egypt’s Eighteenth Dynasty, came to the throne around the age of nine and reigned for only about a decade before dying in his late teens. He was a son of the revolutionary pharaoh Akhenaten, born into the strange new world of his father’s religion and given the name Tutankhaten, “living image of the Aten,” in honour of the single sun-god his father had exalted above all others.

His brief reign was dominated by the undoing of that revolution. Guided by powerful officials, the young king abandoned his father’s heresy, restored the worship of Amun and the old gods, returned the court from the new city of Akhetaten to the traditional centres, and changed his name to Tutankhamun, “living image of Amun.” He married his half-sister Ankhesenamun, but the couple left no surviving children, and when he died young — of causes still debated — he was buried hastily in a small tomb, his line at an end.

In his own age he was a minor figure, soon all but forgotten and omitted from the official king-lists. His immortality came by accident: in 1922 the archaeologist Howard Carter found his tomb in the Valley of the Kings almost untouched by robbers, its chambers crammed with golden treasure and crowned by the exquisite funerary mask that has become the very emblem of ancient Egypt. The discovery made the obscure boy king the most famous pharaoh of all, and gave the modern world its most vivid glimpse of a vanished civilization.
