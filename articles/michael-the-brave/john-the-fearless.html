John the Fearless

Duke of Burgundy, 1371–1419

John was born at Dijon on 28 May 1371, the eldest surviving son of Philip the Bold of Burgundy and Margaret of Male, heiress of Flanders, Artois, and Franche-Comté. The marriage in 1385 to Margaret of Bavaria, sister of Charles VI's queen Isabeau, was part of the great Wittelsbach-Burgundian double wedding at Cambrai that bound the Low Countries to the French royal house. In 1396 the young Count of Nevers led the cream of French chivalry on the crusade against Bayezid I; the army was destroyed at Nicopolis on 25 September. John fought conspicuously in the front line, was captured, and held for nearly a year until ransomed for 200,000 gold florins. He came home with the sobriquet sans Peur and a hard, unsentimental view of politics.

The death of his father in 1404 made him Duke of Burgundy and the dominant figure of the regency that ran the kingdom of the mad Charles VI. His rivalry with the king's brother, Louis, Duke of Orléans, over policy, patronage, and the queen turned murderous: on the night of 23 November 1407 Orléans was hacked to death in the rue Vieille-du-Temple by a band of John's hired men. John fled Paris, then returned to defend the killing in the Hôtel Saint-Pol through the Norman theologian Jean Petit, whose Justification turned tyrannicide into legal doctrine. France divided into Burgundians and Armagnacs and never recombined.

He held Paris through the Cabochien rising of 1413, was driven out by the Armagnacs, and stood aside in 1415 when Henry V landed in Normandy; the disaster of Agincourt destroyed his rivals and left him master of half the kingdom. He retook Paris in 1418 and ran a regime of bloody reprisal there. In September 1419 he agreed to meet the Dauphin Charles on the bridge of Montereau to settle their factions; partway across the bridge, Tanguy du Châtel and the dauphin's other men cut him down on 10 September 1419. The murder threw his son Philip the Good into the English alliance and led directly to the Treaty of Troyes the following year.

The road continues to Bayezid I.
