Marquis de Lafayette

French aristocrat and general of two revolutions, 1757–1834

Born to wealth and an old military family, Lafayette was barely nineteen when, fired by the American cause, he sailed for the New World against the express orders of his king. George Washington took to the eager young Frenchman at once, and the two formed a bond like that of father and son. Lafayette was wounded at Brandywine, wintered with the army at Valley Forge, and shared in the decisive victory at Yorktown, all the while helping to draw France into open alliance with the rebels.

He returned to France a celebrated hero, and when revolution broke out in 1789 he stood among its liberal leaders. As commander of the National Guard he tried to steer a middle course, presenting a Declaration of the Rights of Man drafted with the help of his friend Thomas Jefferson and devising the tricolour cockade. But he was caught between the court of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette, whom he sought to protect, and the radicals led by Robespierre. After his guardsmen fired on a crowd at the Champ de Mars he lost favour, and as the Revolution turned violent he fled, only to spend five years in Austrian and Prussian prisons.

He kept his distance from Napoleon, declined his offers, and lived to champion liberty again—making a triumphal tour of the United States in 1824 and helping install a constitutional monarchy in the revolution of 1830. He died revered on both sides of the Atlantic.
