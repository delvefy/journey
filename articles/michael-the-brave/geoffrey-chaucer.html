Geoffrey Chaucer

English poet, c.1340–1400

Chaucer was born about 1340 in London, probably in Thames Street, the son of John Chaucer, a prosperous wine merchant, and his wife Agnes. The family supplied the royal household, and in 1357 the boy entered as a page in the household of Elizabeth de Burgh, wife of Edward III's son Lionel of Antwerp. In 1359 he served on Edward III's last great French campaign and was captured outside Reims; the king himself contributed sixteen pounds to his ransom. He married Philippa Roet about 1366, the sister of Katherine Swynford who became John of Gaunt's mistress and finally wife — the connection placed Chaucer within Gaunt's affinity for the rest of his life.

From 1366 he held a stream of royal offices that paid the bills and shaped his reading. He travelled on diplomatic missions to Spain, France, Flanders, and especially to Italy — Genoa and Florence in 1372–73, Lombardy in 1378 — where he absorbed Dante, Petrarch, and above all Boccaccio. In 1374 he was made comptroller of the wool custom at the port of London and given the free lease of a house above Aldgate; in 1389 the new king Richard II appointed him clerk of the king's works, overseeing the upkeep of Westminster Palace, the Tower, and royal manors. His patrons changed with the political wind — Gaunt, Richard II, then in 1399 the new Henry IV, whose accession he greeted with the Complaint to his Purse.

Around this working life he wrote the body of English poetry that founded the literary language. The Book of the Duchess elegised Gaunt's first wife. The Parliament of Fowls and the House of Fame followed. Troilus and Criseyde, of about 1385, transmuted Boccaccio's Filostrato into a five-book psychological tragedy. The Canterbury Tales, planned around 1387 and left unfinished at his death, gave the English vernacular its first encyclopaedic survey of voices, classes, and genres. He died on 25 October 1400 and was buried in Westminster Abbey, beginning what became Poets' Corner.

The road continues to Petrarch.
