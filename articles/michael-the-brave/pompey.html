Pompey

Roman general and statesman, 106–48 BC

Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus—Pompey the Great—was the most admired soldier of his generation before Caesar eclipsed him. He first won fame as a young commander raising private legions for the dictator Sulla, who hailed him with the title “the Great.” In a string of campaigns he crushed the last Marians in Spain, helped finish the slave war of Spartacus, and in 67 BC swept the pirates from the Mediterranean in a single brilliant season. He then reorganized the entire Roman East, defeating King Mithridates of Pontus and annexing Syria.

Returning in triumph, Pompey found the Senate reluctant to ratify his settlements, and so he joined Crassus and Julius Caesar in the informal alliance known as the First Triumvirate in 60 BC. To bind the pact he married Caesar’s daughter Julia, whom he loved deeply. But Julia died in childbirth, Crassus fell in Parthia, and the two surviving giants drifted into rivalry. As Caesar’s power grew, Pompey aligned himself with the senatorial conservatives who feared a tyrant.

Civil war broke out in 49 BC when Caesar crossed the Rubicon. Pompey withdrew to Greece to gather the resources of the East, but at the battle of Pharsalus in 48 BC Caesar’s veteran army shattered his larger force. Pompey fled by sea to Egypt, hoping for refuge at the court of the boy-king Ptolemy XIII. Instead, as he stepped ashore, he was stabbed to death and beheaded—a squalid end for a man who had once held the Roman world in his hand.
