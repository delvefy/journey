Jeremy Bentham

English philosopher and reformer, founder of utilitarianism, 1748–1832

A child prodigy who entered Oxford at twelve, Bentham trained for the law but was disgusted by its cruelty, mystery, and waste, and devoted his life instead to reforming it on rational principles. He founded the doctrine of utilitarianism, holding that the rightness of any law or action is measured by a single standard—“the greatest happiness of the greatest number”—and proposed a calculus of pleasures and pains to guide the legislator.

From this principle he derived a vast programme of reform astonishingly ahead of his age: he argued for prison reform and designed the “Panopticon,” called for the welfare of animals, the rights of women, the decriminalization of homosexuality, and the separation of church and state, and dismissed the doctrine of natural rights as “nonsense upon stilts.” His ideas inspired a band of “Philosophical Radicals” who pressed for democratic and legal change in Britain.

His closest collaborator was the economist James Mill, and Bentham took a guiding interest in the rigorous education of Mill’s son. He helped inspire the founding of a great secular university in London, to which he bequeathed his own preserved body as an “auto-icon,” still on display. He died in 1832, the year Britain passed the Reform Act his followers had championed. The brilliant young man raised in his philosophy was John Stuart Mill.
