Mark Twain

American writer and humorist, 1835–1910

Samuel Langhorne Clemens took his pen name from the riverboatman’s cry that sounded a safe depth on the Mississippi, the river of his Missouri boyhood. After stints as a printer, steamboat pilot, and silver prospector, he made his name with comic sketches and the travel book The Innocents Abroad, and became the most beloved humorist and lecturer in America, famous for his drawl, his white suit, and his mordant wit.

His enduring fame rests on the books drawn from his youth along the great river. The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and, above all, Adventures of Huckleberry Finn—the tale of a runaway boy and an escaped slave drifting downriver on a raft—captured the American vernacular and the moral contradictions of slavery so powerfully that the second has been called the source from which all modern American literature flows.

Twain was also a shrewd, ill-starred businessman. His publishing house issued the acclaimed memoirs of the dying Ulysses S. Grant, but bad investments ruined him, and he toured the world lecturing to pay off his debts. A friend of the inventor Nikola Tesla, in whose laboratory he loved to tinker, and of the young Helen Keller, he grew darker and more pessimistic in his later years, scarred by the deaths of his wife and daughters. He died in 1910, as Halley’s Comet returned, just as he had predicted.
