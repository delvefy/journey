Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba

El Gran Capitán, Spanish general, 1453–1515

Gonzalo was born at Montilla, in the Córdoba countryside, on 1 September 1453, the second son of the Count of Aguilar. He was sent at fifteen to the court of the Infante Alfonso of Castile and was on the staff of Isabella when she made her case against her brother Henry IV at Guisando in 1468. He fought for her in the war of succession that followed the king’s death in 1474, was knighted on the field of Toro in March 1476, and then, in the long Granada war, learnt his trade in the only school in Christendom that taught it well: ten years of Andalusian sieges, mountain ambushes, and Moorish light-cavalry encounters at Tájara, Loja, Íllora, and Granada itself, where his Arabic and his courtesy were among the things that brought Boabdil to surrender the Alhambra in January 1492.

Ferdinand of Aragon sent him to Calabria in 1495 with five thousand Castilian troops to oppose the French invasion of Naples. The first campaign was a hard education: at Seminara on 28 June 1495 the Swiss pikes broke his cavalry, and he learned the lesson, retreated into the hills, and refused open battle. By the time the second Italian campaign came in 1500 he had reorganised the Spanish infantry into the tercio in embryo — sword-and-buckler, pike, and the new arquebus combined — and at Cerignola, on 28 April 1503, behind a hastily dug field-work, he killed the French commander Louis d’Armagnac, Duke of Nemours, and broke the army of Naples with arquebus volleys at twenty paces. On 28 December he repeated the trick at the Garigliano, crossing the river by a pontoon bridge in fog at dawn and rolling up the French line.

Ferdinand made him viceroy of Naples; he held the post for three years and was so popular with the Italians and so visibly competent that the king began to suspect him. He was recalled in 1507 on a pretext of consultation; on his arrival at Spain his expense claims were audited line by line, and he is said to have answered the auditors with the “account of the Great Captain,” an itemised invoice for the picks and shovels he had used to dig the king’s graves with — a story Quevedo retold a century later. He retired to his estates at Loja, refused further command, and died at Granada on 2 December 1515 of quartan fever, the foremost soldier of his time. He was buried in the Convento de San Jerónimo at Granada, his banner laid across the tomb.
