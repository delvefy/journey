Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Genevan philosopher and writer, 1712–1778

Born in Geneva and largely self-taught after a restless, wandering youth, Rousseau made his way to Paris, where he befriended Diderot and contributed to the great Encyclopédie. He burst into fame with an essay arguing that the progress of the arts and sciences had corrupted rather than improved humanity, and he developed this provocative theme in a celebrated discourse on the origins of inequality, contrasting the supposed innocence of natural man with the injustices of civilised society.

His most influential work, The Social Contract, opened with the ringing words that man is born free yet is everywhere in chains, and argued that legitimate authority rests on the sovereignty of the people expressed through the general will. In Emile he set out a new philosophy of education according to nature, and in his novel Julie and his astonishingly candid Confessions he gave powerful voice to feeling and the inner self, helping to inspire the Romantic movement.

Quarrelsome, sensitive, and increasingly persecuted, he fell out spectacularly with his fellow philosophes, above all his great rival Voltaire, and even with the Scottish thinker David Hume, who had given him refuge in England. Condemnation of his books drove him into exile and a wandering, fearful old age. Yet his ideas about freedom, equality, and popular sovereignty proved explosive: a generation later the revolutionaries of France, Robespierre foremost among them, would hail him as their prophet, and his thought reshaped politics, education, and literature alike.
