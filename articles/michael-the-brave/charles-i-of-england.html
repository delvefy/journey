Charles I of England

King of England, Scotland, and Ireland, 1600–1649

The second son of James VI and I, Charles became heir on his elder brother’s death and king in 1625. Shy, stubborn, and devoted to the divine right of kings, he married the Catholic French princess Henrietta Maria and at once fell into conflict with his Parliaments over money, religion, and prerogative. From 1629 he dispensed with Parliament altogether, ruling for eleven years of “Personal Rule” financed by contested levies such as Ship Money.

His was the most cultivated court England had known. He assembled one of the greatest royal art collections in Europe, brought Peter Paul Rubens to paint the ceiling of the Banqueting House, and made Anthony van Dyck his painter, whose portraits fixed forever the melancholy elegance of the doomed king. Yet his attempt to impose an English prayer book on Scotland provoked rebellion, forcing him to recall Parliament and igniting a constitutional crisis.

From 1642 king and Parliament went to war. Defeated in the field by the New Model Army and its rising star Oliver Cromwell, Charles negotiated in bad faith and reignited the conflict, hardening his enemies against him. Tried by a special court for treason against his own people, he met his death with dignity outside the Banqueting House in January 1649, the only English king ever executed. The monarchy was abolished, to be restored in 1660 under his son Charles II.

The road continues to Henrietta Maria.
