Judas Maccabeus

Jewish priest and rebel commander, died 160 BC

Judah, surnamed Maccabeus—"the Hammer"—was the third son of the priest Mattathias of Modein. When the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes outlawed the practice of Judaism, plundered and defiled the Temple in Jerusalem, and persecuted those who kept the Law, Mattathias raised the banner of revolt in 167 BC. On his father's death the following year, Judas took command of the rebel forces and became the soul of the resistance.

He proved one of the great guerrilla generals of antiquity. Leading small, mobile bands of the devout, he routed a succession of larger and better-equipped Seleucid armies at Beth Horon, Emmaus, and Beth Zur, defeating in turn the royal generals sent against him. In 164 BC he recovered Jerusalem and cleansed the Temple of its pagan altar, rededicating it to the God of Israel—the event commemorated ever since in the festival of Hanukkah, the Feast of Dedication.

Judas understood that a small nation needed allies, and he opened relations with the rising power of Rome, securing a treaty of friendship with the Senate. But the Seleucids returned in force, and in 160 BC, abandoned by most of his men against overwhelming numbers, he fell fighting at the battle of Elasa. His brothers Jonathan and Simon carried the struggle to its end, and from Simon's line sprang the Hasmonean dynasty that ruled Judea for a century. The deeds of Judas, recorded in the Books of the Maccabees, made him an enduring symbol of faith defended by the sword.
