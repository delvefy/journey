Simón Bolívar

Venezuelan revolutionary, "El Libertador," 1783–1830.

Born to one of the richest creole families in colonial Caracas, Simón José Antonio de la Santísima Trinidad Bolívar y Palacios was orphaned at nine and brought up by his uncle Esteban and the eccentric tutor Simón Rodríguez, who fed him on Rousseau, Voltaire, Locke, and Montesquieu. Sent to Spain at fifteen, he married María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro y Alaysa in Madrid in 1802; she died of yellow fever within months of their arrival in Caracas, and he swore on her grave never to remarry. The widower wandered Europe, witnessed Napoleon's coronation in Paris in 1804 with disgust, and on Monte Sacro in Rome the next year vowed to free Spanish America.

The Napoleonic invasion of Spain in 1808 set off the wars of independence across the empire. Bolívar joined the cause in Caracas under Francisco de Miranda, took refuge in Cartagena after the First Republic's collapse, and from there launched in 1813 the "Admirable Campaign" that gave him Caracas and the title El Libertador. The struggle was brutal and reversible — the Second Republic fell, the royalists reconquered the country, Bolívar spent years in exile in Jamaica and Haiti — but from 1817 onwards, supplied by President Alexandre Pétion and with the llanero cavalry of José Antonio Páez at his disposal, he turned the war. The crossing of the Andes and the victory at Boyacá in August 1819 liberated New Granada; Carabobo in 1821 freed Venezuela; his general Antonio José de Sucre's victory at Ayacucho in 1824 ended Spanish rule in South America.

He was, by the mid-1820s, president of Gran Colombia (modern Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Panama), dictator of Peru, and founder-father of Bolivia, which took his name. He met José de San Martín at Guayaquil in 1822, after which the southern Liberator quietly withdrew to Europe and left him the field. Yet his vision of a federated Spanish-American republic fractured: separatist conspiracies, an assassination attempt in 1828 from which his mistress Manuela Sáenz saved him, the secession of Venezuela under Páez and of Ecuador under Flores, and finally the murder of Sucre in 1830 broke him. He died of tuberculosis on his way to exile, forty-seven years old, near Santa Marta in December 1830, lamenting that he had ploughed the sea. Lafayette wrote his eulogy.
