Baruch Spinoza

Dutch rationalist philosopher, 1632–1677

Baruch Spinoza was born on 24 November 1632 in Amsterdam, into a community of Portuguese Jews whose families had fled the Iberian Inquisition. Schooled in Hebrew scripture and Talmud, he also drank deeply of Latin learning and the new philosophy of René Descartes. His conclusions soon outran his community’s tolerance, and in 1656, at twenty-three, he was pronounced under a ban of cherem — excommunicated and cursed — for opinions held to deny the soul’s immortality and a providential God. He took the Latin name Benedictus and never looked back.

Refusing patronage and even a professorship at Heidelberg that might have curbed his freedom, he supported himself by grinding optical lenses and lived in quiet lodgings near Leiden and The Hague. In 1670 he published, anonymously, the Theological-Political Treatise, a daring plea for freedom of thought and a critical reading of scripture that scandalized orthodox readers across Europe. His masterwork, the Ethics, written in the austere form of geometric proofs, he withheld until after his death.

In it he identified God with Nature itself — a single infinite substance of which all things are modes — and argued that human freedom lies not in escaping necessity but in understanding it, culminating in an “intellectual love of God.” Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz sought him out in 1676 to discuss these ideas. Spinoza died of a lung disease, aggravated by glass dust, on 21 February 1677, only forty-four. Reviled in his own age as an atheist, he was later embraced as a prophet of the Enlightenment: Goethe carried the Ethics as a talisman, and Albert Einstein, asked whether he believed in God, answered that he believed in the God of Spinoza.
