Charlie Chaplin

1889–1977, English comic actor and filmmaker

Charles Spencer Chaplin was born in south London, the son of music-hall performers whose drink and illness drove him in childhood into the Lambeth workhouse and his mother into the asylum. He went on the boards at five, toured England with the Eight Lancashire Lads, and at seventeen joined Fred Karno's comedy troupe, in which Stan Laurel was a fellow understudy. On Karno's second American tour in 1913 Mack Sennett signed him to Keystone for a hundred and fifty dollars a week.

In a single year at Keystone, Essanay and Mutual he assembled the costume of the Tramp — bowler, cane, baggy trousers, oversized shoes, the small moustache — and worked out a style that married broad slapstick with sentiment and social observation. By the end of the First World War he was the most recognized human being on the planet. In 1919, with Mary Pickford, Douglas Fairbanks and D.W. Griffith, he co-founded United Artists so that the great stars might own their own pictures.

The features he then directed under that banner — The Kid, The Gold Rush, The Circus, City Lights, Modern Times, The Great Dictator — defined the silent feature comedy and outlasted the medium itself. The Great Dictator (1940) put two Chaplin performances against each other, a Jewish barber and the tyrant Adenoid Hynkel, and ended with a direct address to camera attacking Hitler. Friends ranged from Albert Einstein, with whom he attended the Hollywood premiere of City Lights, to Winston Churchill and Mahatma Gandhi, whom he met in London in 1931.

Paternity suits, his marriage to Eugene O'Neill's daughter Oona, and his refusal to take American citizenship made him a target of J. Edgar Hoover; in 1952, sailing for the London premiere of Limelight, he was barred from re-entry. He settled in Switzerland, made A King in New York and A Countess from Hong Kong, accepted an honorary Oscar in 1972 to a record-long ovation, and died at Vevey on Christmas Day 1977.

The road continues to Buster Keaton.
