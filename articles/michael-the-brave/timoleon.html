Timoleon

Corinthian general and liberator of Sicily, c. 411–337 BCE

Timoleon was a Corinthian general remembered as one of the noblest figures of the Greek fourth century, the liberator of Sicily from tyranny and from the Carthaginian threat. His career was marked at its outset by a terrible private act: when his own brother Timophanes seized tyrannical power at Corinth, Timoleon, after vainly pleading with him, consented to his killing for the sake of the city's freedom—a deed that won him admiration and lasting grief in equal measure, and after which he long withdrew from public life.

In 344 BCE the Corinthians, asked by their Sicilian colonists for help against the tyrant Dionysius II and the encroaching Carthaginians, gave the command to Timoleon. With a tiny force he crossed to Sicily, took Syracuse, and sent Dionysius off into exile at Corinth.

His greatest victory came in 341 at the river Crimisus, where, against enormous odds and in a driving storm, he routed a vast Carthaginian army and checked Carthage's advance in Sicily for a generation. He then deposed the lesser tyrants of the island, resettled its ravaged cities with fresh colonists, and restored law and self-government to Syracuse.

His work done, Timoleon laid down his power and lived out his days as an honored private citizen, blind in old age but revered as a father of his people. He died around 337 BCE, and the Syracusans mourned him as their second founder.
