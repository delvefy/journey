Plutarch

Greek biographer and essayist, c. AD 46–120

Plutarch was born at Chaeronea in Boeotia and spent much of his life in that small Greek town, content to remain where his family had deep roots. He studied at Athens, travelled to Rome and Egypt, held local magistracies, and for the last decades of his life served as a priest at the oracle of Delphi. A Roman citizen who lived under the emperors Trajan and Hadrian, he embodied the cultured fusion of Greek learning and Roman power that marked his age.

His fame rests on two great bodies of work. The first is the Parallel Lives, in which he paired a famous Greek with a comparable Roman—Alexander with Caesar, Demosthenes with Cicero—and then compared their characters. Plutarch was frank that he wrote biography, not history: a small gesture or a chance remark, he believed, could reveal a soul more truly than the bloodiest battle. The second is the Moralia, a sprawling collection of essays and dialogues on ethics, religion, education, and curiosities of every kind.

Through these works Plutarch became, for later centuries, the great storyteller of the ancient world. His Lives shaped how Europe imagined classical heroes; Shakespeare drew the plots of his Roman plays almost directly from a translation of Plutarch, and the leaders of the Enlightenment and the revolutions that followed read him as a school of civic virtue. More than a chronicler of the famous, he taught generations how to think about greatness itself.
