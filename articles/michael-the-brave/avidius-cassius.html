Avidius Cassius

Roman general and usurper, c.130–175

Avidius Cassius was born around 130 in Syria, into a family that boasted descent from the old Seleucid kings, and rose to become the ablest soldier of his generation. When war with Parthia broke out at the start of the joint reign of Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, it was Cassius who carried the Roman armies to victory in the East, storming the great cities of Seleucia and the Parthian capital Ctesiphon. In reward he was given an extraordinary command over the whole Roman East, a virtual viceroy of the eastern provinces.

In 175 a rumour reached him that Marcus Aurelius, campaigning far away on the Danube, had died. Cassius let himself be proclaimed emperor, and Egypt and Syria rallied to him; later writers whispered that the empress Faustina, fearing for the succession of her young son, had encouraged the move. But the rumour was false: Marcus lived, and when the truth spread, Cassius’s support evaporated.

Within about three months his revolt was over. He was struck down by his own officers, who sent his head to the emperor — a gift Marcus is said to have received with sorrow rather than triumph, for he had hoped to spare and pardon his rebel. Marcus then travelled through the eastern provinces, dealing with the aftermath in the spirit of clemency for which his philosophy was famed. Cassius remained a byword for the overreaching general undone by a false report.
