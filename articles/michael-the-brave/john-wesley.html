John Wesley

English founder of Methodism, 1703–1791

Wesley was born in 1703 at Epworth in Lincolnshire, the son of a rector and of the formidable Susanna Wesley, who shaped his disciplined piety. At Oxford he, his brother Charles, and George Whitefield gathered a band of earnest students whose methodical devotions earned them the mocking nickname "Methodists." An early missionary venture to the colony of Georgia ended in disappointment and self-doubt.

The turning point came in 1738 at a religious meeting in Aldersgate Street in London, where, as a reading of Luther’s preface to the Epistle to the Romans was given, Wesley felt his heart "strangely warmed" and knew an assurance of personal salvation. Taking up the open-air preaching pioneered by Whitefield, he carried his message to the labouring multitudes the established Church had neglected, riding, it was reckoned, a quarter of a million miles and preaching tens of thousands of sermons across the British Isles.

His genius was as much organiser as evangelist. He gathered his converts into societies, classes, and circuits served by lay preachers, building the durable framework of the Methodist movement. His Arminian insistence on grace freely offered to all set him against the Calvinist predestination of Whitefield. Though he lived and died a clergyman of the Church of England, the movement he founded became one of the great forces of world Protestantism after his death in 1791.
