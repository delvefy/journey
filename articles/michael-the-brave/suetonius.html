Suetonius

Roman biographer, c. 69–after 122 CE

Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus was the Roman biographer whose Lives of the Twelve Caesars fixed forever the popular image of the emperors from Julius Caesar to Domitian. Born about 69 CE into the equestrian order, he made his way in the literary and administrative world of the early second century under the wing of his friend and correspondent Pliny the Younger, who secured posts and favours for him.

His scholarship eventually carried him into the imperial palace, where he served as secretary in charge of correspondence to the emperor Hadrian. Access to the archives gave his writing an authority no rival could match, but his court career ended abruptly around 122 CE when he was dismissed, reportedly for some breach of decorum involving the empress.

The work that made him immortal arranged each emperor's life not as a flowing narrative but by categories—ancestry, career, person, habits, virtues, and vices, down to omens and the manner of death. Into this frame he packed a wealth of anecdote, rumour, and intimate detail, preserving stories of Caligula, Claudius, and Nero that no sober annalist would have recorded, and balancing them against documents he alone could quote.

He wrote also On Illustrious Men, brief lives of poets, orators, and grammarians, of which only fragments survive. His vivid, gossipy, category-by-category method became the enduring template of imperial biography, imitated across the centuries—most famously by Einhard in his life of Charlemagne—and remains a chief source for everything we know of the first Caesars.
