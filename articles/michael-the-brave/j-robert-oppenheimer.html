J. Robert Oppenheimer

American physicist, director of Los Alamos, 1904–1967

Born to a cultured and wealthy New York family, Oppenheimer was a dazzling, restless intellect who studied at Harvard and took his doctorate at Göttingen under Max Born. Returning to America, he built at Berkeley and Caltech the first great American school of theoretical physics, making contributions to quantum theory and predicting, in work long overlooked, the gravitational collapse of stars into what would later be called black holes.

In 1942 General Groves chose this seemingly improbable administrator to lead the secret weapons laboratory at Los Alamos. There Oppenheimer marshalled a brilliant and fractious community of scientists toward a single goal, and on 16 July 1945 watched the first atomic explosion light the New Mexico desert, recalling a line from the Bhagavad Gita: “Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds.” Weeks later the bombs fell on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Haunted by what he had made, he told President Truman he felt he had blood on his hands, and afterward opposed the development of the far more destructive hydrogen bomb. In the Red Scare of 1954 his enemies engineered a humiliating security hearing that stripped him of his clearance and his influence. He spent his last years directing the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, the colleague of Einstein, before dying of cancer in 1967, a tragic emblem of science in the nuclear age.
