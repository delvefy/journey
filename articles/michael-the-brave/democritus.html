Democritus

Greek atomist philosopher, c. 460–c. 370 BC

Democritus was born at Abdera in Thrace and, by tradition, spent a large inheritance travelling through Egypt, the Near East, and perhaps beyond in pursuit of knowledge. Working with his teacher Leucippus, he developed the boldest idea of early Greek thought: that all things are composed of countless indivisible, indestructible particles—atoma, the "uncuttable"—moving eternally through empty space. The endless variety of the world, he held, arises only from the size, shape, and arrangement of these atoms as they collide and combine.

From this he built a thoroughly materialist and mechanistic picture of nature, in which even the soul is made of atoms and all events unfold by necessity rather than design. He wrote prodigiously on physics, ethics, mathematics, and much else, but nearly all of it is lost, surviving only in fragments and in the reports of later writers, above all Aristotle, who set out his doctrines in order to dispute them.

In ethics he taught that the goal of life was a cheerful contentment of mind, and his own famously good humour earned him the name of the "laughing philosopher," set against the gloom of Heraclitus. A celebrated tale tells how the alarmed people of Abdera summoned the physician Hippocrates to cure his ceaseless laughter, only for Hippocrates to declare him the sanest of men. Though Plato is said to have wished his books burned, the atomism of Democritus passed to Epicurus and, two thousand years later, helped inspire the atomic theory of modern science.
