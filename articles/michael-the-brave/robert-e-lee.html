Robert E. Lee

Confederate general, 1807–1870

Son of a Revolutionary War cavalryman and married to a great-granddaughter of Martha Washington, Lee was steeped in the legacy of the founding generation; his home at Arlington overlooked the capital named for George Washington. A brilliant West Point cadet and engineer, he served with distinction in the Mexican War and led the marines who captured John Brown at Harpers Ferry. When civil war came in 1861 he was offered command of the Union armies, but could not bring himself to fight against his native Virginia and resigned to serve the Confederacy.

As commander of the Army of Northern Virginia he became the most feared general of the war, repeatedly defeating larger Union forces through audacity and maneuver. At Chancellorsville his daring division of his army, executed by his trusted lieutenant, produced his masterpiece—though at terrible cost. Twice he carried the war north, and twice was turned back, decisively at Gettysburg in 1863.

Worn down at last by Grant’s remorseless campaigns, he surrendered at Appomattox in 1865. In defeat he counselled reconciliation and submission to the Union, spending his final years as president of a small Virginia college that now bears his name alongside Washington’s. He died in 1870, later mythologized by the “Lost Cause” that romanticized the Confederacy. The lieutenant whose loss at Chancellorsville he never replaced was Stonewall Jackson.
