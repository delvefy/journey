Jean Froissart

Chronicler of the Hundred Years’ War, c. 1337–c. 1405

Jean Froissart was born about 1337 at Valenciennes in the county of Hainault, on the borders of France and the Empire, and trained as a clerk with a gift for verse. Around 1361 he found his way to the English court and into the service of Queen Philippa of Hainault, Edward III’s consort and a daughter of his own homeland, who became his patroness and set him to write the deeds of his time. Her favour launched a career of restless travel.

To gather his material Froissart rode across England and Scotland, through France and the Low Countries, down to the glittering court of Gaston Fébus of Foix in the Pyrenees, and as far as Italy, where he mingled with the great at a famous Milanese wedding. Everywhere he sought out knights and captains who had been present at the great events and coaxed their stories from them.

The result was the Chronicles, his vast narrative of the Hundred Years’ War from the 1320s to the century’s end — Crécy and Poitiers, the Black Prince in his glory, the Jacquerie, the Peasants’ Revolt in England. Dazzled by chivalry and not always reliable, it is nonetheless the most vivid and indispensable portrait of the age, the lens through which later centuries saw it. He was a fine poet besides, and in 1395 he came back to England to present a richly illuminated copy of his work to King Richard II. He passed his last years as a canon at Chimay and died around 1405.

The road continues to Philippa of Hainault.
