Louis XVI of France

King of France, 1754–1793 (reigned 1774–1792)

Louis XVI succeeded his grandfather Louis XV in 1774, inheriting a kingdom burdened by debt and a court resistant to change. Well-meaning, pious, and fond of hunting and lock-making, he lacked the decisiveness his troubled reign demanded. With his queen, Marie Antoinette, he presided over a monarchy whose prestige was ebbing, and his early ministers’ attempts at financial reform foundered on the opposition of the privileged orders.

His government’s decision to support the American rebels against Britain, welcoming their envoy Benjamin Franklin and committing France to the war that helped secure George Washington’s victory, won prestige abroad but deepened the ruin of the treasury at home. The mounting crisis at last forced Louis to summon the Estates-General in 1789, for the first time in 175 years, hoping to find new revenue. Instead he unleashed the French Revolution.

As the old order collapsed around him, Louis was made a constitutional monarch, his powers curtailed. His half-hearted acceptance of the new regime, and above all the royal family’s failed attempt to flee the country in 1791, captured at Varennes, convinced many that the king was a traitor in league with France’s enemies. The monarchy was abolished in 1792, and the former king was put on trial before the National Convention. Condemned for treason, with Robespierre among those demanding his death, he was guillotined in January 1793, his execution sending shock waves across the courts of Europe.
