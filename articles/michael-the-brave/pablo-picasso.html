Pablo Picasso

Spanish painter and sculptor, 1881–1973

A child prodigy from Málaga, son of a drawing master, Picasso could draw like a master before he was a man and spent the rest of his long life unlearning it to reinvent art again and again. After early phases of melancholy blue and tender rose, he and Georges Braque, building on the lessons of Cézanne, shattered the conventions of perspective to create Cubism around 1907—announced by the savage, mask-faced Les Demoiselles d’Avignon—breaking objects into facets seen from many angles at once. It was the most radical revolution in painting since the Renaissance.

Endlessly inventive and ferociously productive across painting, sculpture, ceramics, and printmaking, he never stopped transforming his style. Deeply rooted in the Spanish tradition of Velázquez and Goya, he paid them open homage—most famously in his many variations on Velázquez’s Las Meninas. In 1937, in fury at the bombing of a Basque town during the Spanish Civil War, he painted Guernica, a vast black-and-white outcry against the horror of modern war that became the most famous political painting of the century.

Living in France through two world wars, a celebrity of unmatched fame and wealth, Picasso worked tirelessly into his nineties. He died in 1973, the towering and inexhaustible genius of twentieth-century art, rivalled only by his friend and counterpart Henri Matisse.
