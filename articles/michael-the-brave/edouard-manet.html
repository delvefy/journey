Édouard Manet

French painter, 1832–1883

The son of a Paris magistrate, Manet was the pivotal figure between the old painting and the new, the man who dragged art into the modern world. Steeped in the museum tradition—he idolised the Spaniards Velázquez and Goya and travelled to Madrid to study them—he used their lessons to startling, contemporary ends. His Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe, with a nude woman calmly picnicking among clothed men, and his Olympia, a frank courtesan staring boldly out at the viewer, scandalised the public and critics of the 1860s by transposing the poses of revered masterpieces into modern Parisian life.

Though he craved official recognition and the approval of the Salon, Manet became the reluctant figurehead of the avant-garde. The younger Impressionists—Monet, Renoir, and his friend and sister-in-law Berthe Morisot—gathered around him, and under their influence he lightened his palette and took up painting outdoors, even as he refused to join their independent exhibitions. His friend the poet Charles Baudelaire urged him to become the painter of modern life, and he did.

His brushwork grew ever freer and more brilliant, culminating in the haunting late masterpiece A Bar at the Folies-Bergère. Struck down by illness in his early fifties, he died in 1883, honoured at last, the indispensable bridge to modern art.
