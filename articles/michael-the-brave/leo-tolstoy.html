Leo Tolstoy

Russian novelist and moral philosopher, 1828–1910

Born a count on the family estate of Yasnaya Polyana, orphaned young, Tolstoy wasted his youth in gambling and dissipation before army service in the Caucasus and the siege of Sevastopol turned him to writing. From his maturity came two of the supreme achievements of the novel: War and Peace, an immense panorama of Russian society swept up in the Napoleonic wars, and Anna Karenina, a tragedy of love and society whose moral seriousness and psychological depth have rarely been equalled.

At the height of his fame a profound spiritual crisis overtook him. He renounced the Orthodox Church, which excommunicated him, and worked out a radical Christianity of his own—rejecting the state, private property, and all violence, and preaching nonresistance to evil. He gave away his copyrights, dressed as a peasant, and laboured in the fields, to the despair of his wife.

His later tracts, above all The Kingdom of God Is Within You, carried his gospel of nonviolence around the world. Among the young men who read them was a Indian lawyer in South Africa, with whom the old prophet exchanged letters in his final year and who named a settlement “Tolstoy Farm” in his honour. In 1910, at eighty-two, Tolstoy fled his home to live as a wanderer and died days later at a remote railway station. The disciple who would carry his doctrine of nonviolence furthest was Mahatma Gandhi.
