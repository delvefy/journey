Pythagoras

Greek philosopher and mathematician, c. 570–495 BC

Pythagoras was born on the Aegean island of Samos and, according to tradition, travelled widely in search of wisdom before settling at Croton, a Greek colony in southern Italy. There he founded a community that was at once a school, a religious brotherhood, and a political force. Its members lived by strict rules, shared their property, kept their teachings secret, and revered their master so deeply that later writers could scarcely separate the man from the legend.

At the heart of his thought lay the conviction that number is the key to reality. The Pythagoreans discovered that musical harmonies correspond to simple ratios of string lengths, and from this they leapt to the grand idea that the whole cosmos is ordered by number and proportion—a “harmony of the spheres.” The theorem relating the sides of a right-angled triangle bears his name to this day, though much of the mathematics was likely the work of his followers. He is also remembered for the doctrine of the transmigration of souls, the belief that the soul is reborn into new bodies after death.

The brotherhood’s influence in Croton eventually provoked a violent backlash, and the Pythagoreans were scattered. Yet the master’s ideas endured and spread. His fusion of mathematics, music, and mysticism deeply shaped later thinkers—above all Plato, whose vision of an eternal order behind the visible world owes much to the Pythagorean spirit—and helped set Western philosophy and science on their long search for the hidden mathematical structure of things.
