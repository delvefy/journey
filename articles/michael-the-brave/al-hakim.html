Al-Hakim

Fatimid caliph, 985–1021

Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah was the sixth Fatimid caliph, ruling the great Shia empire of Egypt and North Africa from Cairo. He came to the throne in 996 as a boy of eleven and grew into one of the most enigmatic and erratic sovereigns of the Middle Ages, remembered by later ages as the “Mad Caliph” for the violent swings of his decrees.

He was, at his best, a great patron of learning. He founded in Cairo the House of Knowledge, a vast library and academy, and gathered scholars to his court — among them the scientist Ibn al-Haytham, whom he summoned to master the flooding of the Nile. Yet the same ruler issued bewildering and cruel edicts, persecuted Christians and Jews by turns, and in 1009 ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem — an outrage that shocked Christendom and is counted among the distant causes of the Crusades.

Stranger still was the cult that grew around his person: certain of his followers proclaimed him a manifestation of the divine, and from that belief sprang the Druze faith, which reveres him to this day. In 1021 al-Hakim rode out by night into the hills outside Cairo and never returned; whether murdered in a palace intrigue or vanished by his own will, no one knew, and his devotees held that he had withdrawn into hiding to return at the end of days. He left behind a memory poised between madness, mysticism and legend.
