W. B. Yeats

1865–1939, Irish poet and dramatist, Nobel laureate

William Butler Yeats was born in Sandymount, Dublin, the eldest child of the painter John Butler Yeats and Susan Pollexfen of the Sligo shipping family. Long childhood summers among his Pollexfen grandparents fixed Sligo and the legendary west of Ireland as the landscape of his imagination. He studied art briefly in Dublin and London, then turned to poetry; his first volume, The Wanderings of Oisin, appeared in 1889, the year he met Maud Gonne, the beautiful Anglo-Irish nationalist who became his unattainable obsession and the subject of much of his early verse.

From 1899, with Lady Augusta Gregory at Coole Park and the playwright John Millington Synge, he founded what became the Abbey Theatre in Dublin in 1904, the cradle of modern Irish drama. He wrote propaganda plays for the cause (Cathleen ni Houlihan) and defended Synge's Playboy of the Western World from the rioting audiences of 1907. He was president of the Irish National Theatre Society, hunted occult societies through the Order of the Golden Dawn, and chiselled his early misty Celticism into something stonier and more public in Responsibilities (1914).

The Easter Rising of 1916 — the deaths of MacDonagh, MacBride, Pearse and Connolly — gave him the matter of "A terrible beauty is born," and the Anglo-Irish War and Civil War of his middle age produced The Tower (1928) and The Winding Stair (1933), volumes that contain "Sailing to Byzantium," "Among School Children" and "Leda and the Swan." He married Georgie Hyde-Lees in 1917, whose automatic writing on their honeymoon furnished the symbolic system of A Vision. He served as a senator of the new Irish Free State from 1922 and was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923, having earlier midwifed Rabindranath Tagore's by writing the introduction to Gitanjali. He died at Roquebrune on the French Riviera in January 1939; his body was reinterred at Drumcliffe under the slopes of Ben Bulben in 1948.
