Pope Boniface VIII

Benedetto Caetani, pope 1294–1303

Benedetto Caetani was born around 1230 at Anagni and rose as a sharp canon lawyer and papal diplomat. He came to the throne of Saint Peter in 1294 in extraordinary circumstances, after the saintly hermit Celestine V resigned the papacy — a withdrawal Caetani was thought to have encouraged, and which Dante would scorn as “the great refusal.” Once elected as Boniface VIII, he kept his predecessor in close confinement and set about asserting the rights of the Church with a pride that brooked no rival.

He pushed the medieval claim of papal supremacy to its furthest extreme. His bull Clericis Laicos forbade kings to tax the clergy without Rome’s consent, setting him against both Philip IV of France and Edward I of England, who needed church money for their wars. In 1300 he proclaimed the first Jubilee, a Holy Year that drew immense crowds of pilgrims to Rome in a blaze of prestige. Then in 1302 came Unam Sanctam, the most absolute declaration ever issued of the pope’s authority over every earthly power, holding that submission to the Roman pontiff was necessary for salvation itself.

Against Philip IV the words proved hollow. The king answered force with force: in September 1303 his minister Nogaret and the pope’s Roman enemies the Colonna seized the aged Boniface at Anagni and abused him for days. Rescued by the townspeople but broken in body and spirit, he died in Rome within weeks. His defeat marked the eclipse of the high medieval papacy before the rising power of kings. Dante, whose ruin Boniface’s meddling in Florence had helped to seal, took his own revenge, consigning the proud pope to the flames of Hell in the Inferno.
