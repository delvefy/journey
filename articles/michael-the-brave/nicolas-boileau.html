Nicolas Boileau

French poet and critic, 1636–1711.

Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux was born in the rue de Jerusalem on the Île de la Cité in Paris, the fifteenth child of a clerk of the Grande Chambre of the Paris Parlement. Educated at the Collège de Beauvais and then in theology at the Sorbonne, he abandoned both Church and law on his father's death in 1657, when a modest inheritance gave him just enough to live on and the leisure to write. His early Satires, circulated in manuscript from 1660 and printed in 1666, set the lines of a literary persona — honest, sharp-tongued, principled, intolerant of pretentious bad writing — that he kept all his life.

From the 1660s he was at the centre of the young Parisian literary world. With Molière, Racine, and La Fontaine he made up "le groupe des quatre amis," meeting at a tavern called the Mouton Blanc; he defended his three friends in print whenever the journals attacked them, and they him. His verse Epistle XII to the king and the Art poétique of 1674 — a 1,100-line manual of poetic doctrine modelled on Horace's Ars Poetica and Aristotle's Poetics — codified French classicism for the next century: rules of unity, simplicity, decorum, the primacy of reason over the merely brilliant. The same year he published the mock-epic Le Lutrin, a quarrel of clergy over a lectern that Alexander Pope would soon imitate in The Rape of the Lock, and his French version of Longinus's On the Sublime, which gave the eighteenth century its category of the sublime.

Louis XIV pensioned him in 1669 and in 1677 appointed him, with Racine, royal historiographer; the two of them attended the king on his campaigns to write up the official annals of the reign. He was elected to the Académie française in 1684 over the king's initial objection, took up the cause of the Ancients against Charles Perrault and the Moderns in the famous quarrel of the 1690s, and outlived almost all of the friends of his youth. He died in Paris in 1711, having become for European letters the model of the unmoved, principled critic, and the school examiner of every poet who came after him in French.
