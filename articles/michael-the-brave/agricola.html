Agricola

Roman general and governor of Britain, 40–93 AD

Gnaeus Julius Agricola was born in southern Gaul and first saw service as a young military tribune in Britain, where he witnessed the great revolt of Boudica and its bloody suppression. In the civil wars of 69 he threw in his lot with Vespasian, whose victory opened the way to a steady rise: command of a legion back in Britain, the governorship of Aquitania, the consulship, and at last, around 78, appointment as governor of Britain itself.

There he won lasting fame. Over some seven years—the longest and most successful of all Roman governorships in the province—he subdued the tribes of Wales and the north, pushed deep into Caledonia, and in 83 crushed a great confederation of northern Britons under the chieftain Calgacus at the battle of Mons Graupius. He sent his fleet around the island, proving Britain to be an island, and encouraged the spread of Roman towns, schooling, and the Latin tongue among the conquered.

His very success undid him. The emperor Domitian, jealous of a subject's military glory, recalled Agricola and gave him no further command, so that he spent his last years in retirement and was never again employed. He died in 93, amid rumours—recorded by his son-in-law—that he had been poisoned. That son-in-law was the historian Tacitus, whose admiring biography preserved Agricola's deeds and, in the famous speech he gave to Calgacus, the bitter verdict on Rome that "they make a desert and call it peace."

The road continues to Boudica.
