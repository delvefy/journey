Charles VII of France

King of France, "le Victorieux", 1403–1461

Charles was born at the Hôtel Saint-Pol in Paris on 22 February 1403, fifth son of the mad king Charles VI and Isabeau of Bavaria. The deaths of his elder brothers between 1415 and 1417 made him dauphin during the worst of the Armagnac–Burgundian civil war. His complicity, real or imputed, in the assassination of John the Fearless of Burgundy at the bridge of Montereau in 1419 pushed Burgundy into the English camp; the Treaty of Troyes in 1420 disinherited him and recognised Henry V of England as heir to France. After the deaths of Henry V and Charles VI in 1422 he held only the south of the kingdom, ran a hand-to-mouth court at Bourges, and was mocked as "the King of Bourges".

The turning point came in 1429. Joan of Arc reached him at Chinon, the siege of Orléans was broken, and on 17 July 1429 he was anointed and crowned in the cathedral of Reims. He failed to support Joan at Compiègne the following year, but the political momentum was now his. The Treaty of Arras in 1435 detached Burgundy from England; Paris reopened her gates in 1436. The truce of Tours in 1444 gave him breathing space to reform his army: the Ordonnance of 1445 created the compagnies d'ordonnance, the first European standing army, and the franc-archers added an infantry reserve in 1448. His Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges of 1438 placed the Gallican church under royal supervision.

The reformed army reconquered Normandy at Formigny in 1450 and Guyenne at Castillon in 1453, where John Talbot was killed and English France reduced to Calais. The Hundred Years' War was effectively over. Charles's later years were embittered by the trial of his financier Jacques Cœur in 1451 and the open hostility of his son the dauphin, the future Louis XI, who fled to Burgundy. He died at the Château de Mehun-sur-Yèvre on 22 July 1461, having transformed a defeated kingdom into a centralising monarchy.

The road continues to Henry V of England.
