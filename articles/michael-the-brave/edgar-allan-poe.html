Edgar Allan Poe

American writer and poet, 1809–1849

Orphaned young and raised by a Richmond merchant with whom he bitterly quarrelled, Poe lived a life of poverty, drink, and grief, scraping a living as a magazine editor and critic while producing some of the most influential writing in the American canon. His was a genius of the strange and the terrifying. In tales such as The Fall of the House of Usher, The Tell-Tale Heart, and The Masque of the Red Death he perfected the Gothic horror story, plumbing madness, dread, and obsession with hypnotic intensity.

He was a restless inventor of forms. In The Murders in the Rue Morgue and its sequels, featuring the brilliant amateur sleuth C. Auguste Dupin, he created the modern detective story, the direct ancestor of Sherlock Holmes. He wrote pioneering science fiction and tales of cryptography and ratiocination, and in essays argued for the unity of effect in the short tale and the lyric poem. His poem The Raven, with its tolling refrain “Nevermore,” made him briefly famous across America.

His final years were haunted by the death of his young wife and by his own demons; in 1849 he was found delirious on the streets of Baltimore and died days later in circumstances still unexplained. Long undervalued at home, he was exalted in Europe, above all by Baudelaire, and is now honoured as a founder of whole branches of modern literature.
