Basil of Caesarea

Cappadocian Father and Doctor of the Church, c. 330–379 AD

Basil was born around 330 into a wealthy and devout Cappadocian family that produced an extraordinary number of saints, among them his grandmother Macrina, his sister Macrina the Younger, and his brother Gregory of Nyssa. He studied rhetoric at Caesarea, Constantinople, and Athens, where he and his friend Gregory of Nazianzus were fellow students. Persuaded by his sister to give up worldly ambition, he toured the monasteries of Egypt and Syria and embraced the ascetic life.

Returning to Cappadocia, he gathered communities of monks and drew up rules of common life, prayer, work, and charity that became the foundation of Eastern monasticism and still bear his name. He insisted that monks serve the wider world, and outside Caesarea he built the Basiliad, a vast complex of hospital, hostel, and almshouse that astonished his contemporaries.

Made bishop of Caesarea in 370, Basil became the great champion of Nicene orthodoxy in the East during the reign of the Arian emperor Valens. When the emperor's prefect threatened him with confiscation, exile, and death, Basil's fearless reply so impressed his persecutors that they left him in peace. His treatise On the Holy Spirit was decisive in establishing the full divinity of the third person of the Trinity.

A tireless pastor, theologian, and administrator, he wore himself out in the Church's service and died in 379, not yet fifty, mourned by his whole city. Honoured as Basil the Great and numbered among the Three Holy Hierarchs of the Eastern Church, he shaped its monasticism, its liturgy, and its doctrine alike.
