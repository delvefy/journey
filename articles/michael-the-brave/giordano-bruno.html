Giordano Bruno

Italian philosopher and cosmologist, 1548–1600

Born at Nola near Naples in 1548, Filippo Bruno took the name Giordano when he entered the Dominican order as a young man. His restless brilliance and heterodox reading soon drew suspicion of heresy, and around 1576 he fled the cloister to begin a wandering life across Europe. He lectured in Paris under the protection of King Henry III, dazzling audiences with his prodigious art of memory; crossed to England, where he disputed at Oxford and dedicated philosophical dialogues to courtiers of Elizabeth’s circle; and moved on through Germany, quarrelling with Calvinists and Lutherans as readily as with Catholics.

Bruno seized upon the heliocentric astronomy of Nicolaus Copernicus and pushed it far beyond anything its author had intended. Drawing also on Nicholas of Cusa and the ancient atomists, he proclaimed an infinite universe filled with countless suns and worlds, with no centre and no outer wall, and a God immanent within all nature rather than enthroned above it. It was a vision of breathtaking audacity, and a dangerous one.

Lured back to Italy in 1591, he was denounced to the Inquisition in Venice and handed to Rome, where he was held and interrogated for years. Refusing to renounce his central convictions, he was condemned as an obstinate heretic and burned at the stake in the Campo de’ Fiori on 17 February 1600. A generation later Galileo Galilei would face the same tribunal over the motion of the Earth. Bruno left no school, but his defiant death made him a lasting emblem of free thought, and his pantheist cosmos foreshadowed the philosophy of Baruch Spinoza.
