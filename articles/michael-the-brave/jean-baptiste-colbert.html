Jean-Baptiste Colbert

French statesman and finance minister, 1619–1683

The son of a Reims cloth merchant, Colbert rose through the patronage of Cardinal Mazarin, who on his deathbed commended the diligent administrator to the young Louis XIV. Colbert repaid the trust at once by helping to destroy the over-mighty superintendent of finances, Nicolas Fouquet, and stepping into effective control of the kingdom’s economy, which he would direct for two decades.

As controller-general he became the supreme exponent of mercantilism—so much so that the doctrine is often called “Colbertism.” He overhauled the tax system and royal accounts, slashed corruption, and pursued a relentless policy of building national wealth: protective tariffs, state-sponsored luxury manufactures such as the Gobelins tapestries and Saint-Gobain glass, new roads and the Canal du Midi, and a vast expansion of the navy and merchant marine. He chartered trading companies for the East and West Indies and promoted colonies in Canada and the Caribbean.

A patron as well as an accountant, he founded the Académie des sciences and academies of inscriptions, architecture, and the arts, drawing scholars like Christiaan Huygens to Paris, and employed men of letters such as Charles Perrault to glorify the reign. His ceaseless labour funded the splendour of Versailles, yet the king’s wars steadily drained the treasury he had so carefully filled. He died exhausted and unpopular in 1683, the architect of French economic power under the Sun King.
