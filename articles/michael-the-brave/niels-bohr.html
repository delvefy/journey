Niels Bohr

Danish physicist, founder of quantum atomic theory, 1885–1962

A Copenhagen professor’s son and a powerful, brooding thinker, Bohr went to Manchester to work with Rutherford and there solved a deep problem in the new nuclear atom. In 1913 he proposed that electrons could occupy only certain fixed orbits and emit or absorb energy in discrete quanta when they jumped between them, a bold marriage of classical and quantum ideas that explained the spectrum of hydrogen. The Bohr model won him the Nobel Prize in 1922 and made quantum theory respectable.

From his Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen he presided over the creation of modern quantum mechanics, drawing the brilliant young men of a generation—Heisenberg, Pauli, Dirac—into endless argument. His principle of complementarity, that an electron could be described as both wave and particle depending on the experiment, became the heart of the “Copenhagen interpretation,” and led to his long, friendly, and unresolved philosophical duel with Einstein over whether nature was fundamentally random.

With Wheeler he explained nuclear fission just as the Second World War began. Half-Jewish, he escaped Nazi-occupied Denmark in 1943 in a dramatic flight to Sweden and on to the Manhattan Project, where he argued—in vain—that the coming bomb should be shared openly to avoid an arms race. After the war he campaigned for an “open world” and the peaceful use of atomic energy. He died in 1962, having shaped both the science and the conscience of the atomic age.
