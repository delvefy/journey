Maecenas

Roman statesman and patron of the arts, c. 70–8 BCE

Gaius Maecenas was the great cultural minister of the age of Augustus, a man who never held high public office yet stood for decades among the most powerful figures in Rome. Born about 70 BCE into a wealthy Etruscan family of equestrian rank that claimed descent from the old kings of Etruria, he attached himself early to the young Octavian and became his most trusted political confidant.

His value lay in diplomacy and discretion. He helped negotiate the marriage of Octavian to Scribonia and the delicate settlements with Mark Antony, including the Treaty of Brundisium in 40 BCE. During the campaigns that ended the civil wars—above all the war against Antony and Cleopatra that climaxed at Actium in 31 BCE—Maecenas was repeatedly left in charge of Rome and Italy, governing without formal title and suppressing conspiracies on Octavian's behalf.

He is remembered most of all as a patron. Around him gathered the finest poets of the day: he drew Virgil and Horace into his circle, gave Horace the Sabine farm that secured his independence, and encouraged the writing of the Georgics and the Odes. So complete was his sponsorship that his very name became, in every later European language, the word for a generous patron of letters and the arts.

Famous too for his luxurious gardens on the Esquiline, his refined and faintly affected taste, and his own mannered prose, Maecenas gradually withdrew from public affairs in his later years. He died in 8 BCE, leaving his fortune to Augustus, and was mourned as the man who had made Roman poetry flourish.
