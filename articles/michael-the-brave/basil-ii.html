Basil II

Byzantine emperor, r. 976–1025

Basil II, of the Macedonian dynasty, was the longest-reigning of all Byzantine emperors and the ruler under whom the medieval empire reached its zenith. He came to the throne as a youth overshadowed by powerful soldier-regents, and in his early reign he barely survived two great revolts of the military aristocracy. To crush the rebel Bardas Phokas, he struck a fateful bargain with Vladimir, prince of the Kievan Rus: Vladimir sent six thousand fierce warriors — the origin of the emperor’s famed Varangian Guard — and in return received the hand of Basil’s sister Anna and accepted baptism, drawing his whole people into the Orthodox Church.

His name is bound above all to his long war with Bulgaria. For decades he campaigned against Tsar Samuel, and after a crushing victory in 1014 he is said to have blinded thousands of captured Bulgarian soldiers, sending them home in long files led by the one-eyed — a cruelty that earned him the grim title Boulgaroktonos, the Bulgar-Slayer. Bulgaria was annexed, and the Balkans returned to Roman rule.

Austere, unmarried and wholly devoted to the state, Basil curbed the great landed families, filled the treasury, and pushed the frontiers outward in the Balkans, Armenia and the East to their widest extent in centuries. When he died in 1025 he left the empire stronger and larger than at any time since Justinian — though his successors would soon squander the inheritance.
