Herod the Great

King of Judea, c. 72–4 BC

Herod was of Idumean descent, the son of Antipater, a powerful minister to the Hasmonean ruler Hyrcanus and a trusted ally of Rome under Julius Caesar. Made governor of Galilee while still young, Herod rose amid the upheavals of the Roman civil wars. He won the favour of Mark Antony, who in 40 BC procured for him the title King of the Jews from the Roman Senate, and with Roman arms he took Jerusalem in 37 BC, extinguishing Hasmonean rule. To shore up his legitimacy he married the Hasmonean princess Mariamne.

When Antony fell at Actium, Herod adroitly transferred his loyalty to the victorious Octavian, soon to be Augustus, who confirmed him in power and enlarged his kingdom. For more than thirty years he ruled as Rome's client, bringing prosperity and a flood of grand construction. He rebuilt the Second Temple in Jerusalem on a magnificent scale, raised the great harbour city of Caesarea, and fortified the desert strongholds of Masada and Herodium.

Yet his reign curdled into suspicion and bloodshed. Consumed by jealousy and fear of conspiracy, he had his wife Mariamne executed, along with her kin and several of his own sons—cruelty that prompted the quip that it was safer to be Herod's pig than his son. The Gospel of Matthew remembers him as the king who, learning of a newborn rival, ordered the massacre of the infants of Bethlehem, driving the Holy Family to flee into Egypt. He died in 4 BC, and Augustus divided his kingdom among his surviving sons.

The road continues to Mariamne.
