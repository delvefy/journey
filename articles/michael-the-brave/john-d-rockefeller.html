John D. Rockefeller

American oil magnate and philanthropist, 1839–1937

John Davison Rockefeller was born on 8 July 1839 at Richford in upstate New York, the second of six children. His father was a travelling con-man and bigamist; his Baptist mother, who held the household together, drilled into him a fierce sense of thrift and tithing that he kept all his life. The family moved to Cleveland in 1853, and at sixteen, after a short business course, he became a bookkeeper for a produce commission firm at fifty cents a day.

The American oil rush of 1859 in western Pennsylvania caught his attention. In 1863 he and a partner built a Cleveland refinery, and from then on his focus was on the refining and distribution end of the industry rather than the drilling. The Standard Oil Company, founded in 1870, used secret rebate arrangements with the railroads to undercut competing refiners, then absorbed them. By the late 1870s Standard Oil controlled some ninety per cent of the refining capacity of the United States. The Standard Oil Trust of 1882 was the model for the great industrial combinations that came after.

The muckraking journalism of Ida Tarbell exposed his methods to a national readership; the federal Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 and Theodore Roosevelt's trust-busting administrations brought the case that, in 1911, dissolved Standard Oil into thirty-four successor companies, including the ancestors of Exxon, Mobil, and Chevron. The breakup made Rockefeller, who held shares in all of them, vastly richer still.

From the late 1890s he devoted himself increasingly to giving the money away on Baptist and scientific principles, founding the University of Chicago, the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research (later Rockefeller University), the General Education Board, and the Rockefeller Foundation. The Foundation's eradication campaigns against hookworm and yellow fever in the Americas and its support for modern medical research established the template of large American philanthropy. He died at Ormond Beach, Florida, in 1937, aged 97, having given away the equivalent of many billions of modern dollars. The Scottish-born steel magnate whose own fortune had grown alongside his in the same Gilded Age, and whose Gospel of Wealth was the matching theory to Rockefeller's own giving, was Andrew Carnegie.
