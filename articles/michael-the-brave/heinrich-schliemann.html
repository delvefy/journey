Heinrich Schliemann

German businessman and pioneer of Aegean archaeology, 1822–1890

Born poor in Mecklenburg, Schliemann made an immense fortune as a merchant—trading in indigo, then profiteering during the Crimean War and the California gold rush—and mastered more than a dozen languages along the way. Financially independent in middle age, he devoted his wealth and ferocious energy to a boyhood dream: to prove that the Troy of Homer’s Iliad had truly existed.

Guided to the mound of Hisarlik in Turkey by the diplomat Frank Calvert, he began digging in 1870 and, hacking impatiently through the layers of many superimposed cities, uncovered fortifications and a hoard of gold ornaments he triumphantly proclaimed the “Treasure of Priam.” He moved on to mainland Greece and at Mycenae unearthed the shaft graves and their golden death-masks, one of which he declared—wrongly—the face of Agamemnon. His sensational finds electrified the world.

His methods were crude and his claims often self-aggrandising and mistaken; later scholars showed he had destroyed much and misdated what he found. Yet he had revealed an entire lost Bronze Age civilisation behind the Greek myths and founded the modern archaeology of prehistoric Greece. His discoveries were hailed by enthusiasts of Homer such as the British statesman William Ewart Gladstone, and his trained collaborators carried the work on after his death in 1890.
