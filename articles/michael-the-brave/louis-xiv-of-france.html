Louis XIV

King of France, the “Sun King,” 1638–1715

Grandson of Henri IV, Louis came to the throne as a child of four, and his long minority was governed by the wily Cardinal Mazarin. The noble revolts of the Fronde, which drove the boy-king from his capital, left him with a lifelong distrust of the aristocracy. When Mazarin died in 1661, the young king astonished his court by announcing that he would rule alone, taking the sun as his emblem and concentrating all power in his own person.

For more than half a century he made France the dominant state of Europe and the model of royal absolutism. He built the colossal palace of Versailles and drew the great nobles there, taming them in a gilded cage of etiquette and spectacle. His minister Colbert reorganised the economy, and his reign became a golden age of French culture, patronising the playwright Molière, the dramatist Racine, and the composer Lully. Yet his revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 expelled the Protestant Huguenots and stained his record.

Ambition drew him into ceaseless wars. Early victories enlarged France, but the ruinous War of the Spanish Succession, against a coalition led by William III and the Duke of Marlborough, drained the treasury and the people. Reigning seventy-two years—the longest in European history—he outlived his son and grandson and left the throne to his young great-grandson Louis XV.
