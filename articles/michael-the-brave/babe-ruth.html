Babe Ruth

American baseball player, 1895–1948.

George Herman Ruth was born in Pigtown, a Baltimore waterfront slum, to a saloon-keeper father and a barmaid mother who together had eight children of whom only two survived infancy. Declared "incorrigible" at seven, he was sent to St. Mary's Industrial School for Boys, where the Xaverian Brother Matthias Boutlier taught him to throw, catch, hit, and read. The minor-league Baltimore Orioles signed him in 1914; teammates dubbed the muscular nineteen-year-old "Jack's babe," shortened it, and the name stuck.

Sold that summer to the Boston Red Sox, Ruth was at first one of the best left-handed pitchers in the American League, winning eighteen games in 1915 and twenty-three in 1916 with a sub-two ERA, helping pitch Boston to two World Series championships. Yet his hitting was so prodigious that the team began moving him to the outfield to keep his bat in the lineup every day. In 1919, the year he hit a then-record twenty-nine home runs, the cash-strapped Boston owner Harry Frazee sold him to the New York Yankees for $100,000, an event Boston fans came to call the Curse of the Bambino.

In pinstripes Ruth remade the sport. In 1920 he hit fifty-four home runs, more than any other team in the league; in 1921, fifty-nine; in 1927, his sixty home runs stood as the single-season record for thirty-four years. The Yankees won four World Series with him; the gate receipts paid for Yankee Stadium, which the press called "the House that Ruth Built." With Lou Gehrig batting behind him in the lineup the team's "Murderers' Row" terrorized opposing pitchers. He sold soap, suits, and cigars by his name, ate hot dogs and drank beer by the bucket, called the Pope on the telephone, visited dying children in hospitals, and supposedly — in the third game of the 1932 Series against the Cubs at Wrigley Field — pointed to the outfield bleachers and hit a home run there on the next pitch.

Released by the Yankees in 1934, he played a half-season for the Braves and retired. Hopes of managing in the majors were never granted him; he died of throat cancer in 1948, the first inductee of the Baseball Hall of Fame, and the figure to whom every later slugger's totals are still compared.

The road continues to Lou Gehrig.
