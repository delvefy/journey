Pope Leo I

Pope and Doctor of the Church, c.400–461

Leo, born around the year 400, served as a leading deacon of the Roman church before his election to the papacy in 440, in the troubled twilight of the Western Empire. Across more than two decades he raised the prestige of the see of Rome to new heights, preaching tirelessly and asserting in letter after letter that the bishop of Rome was the heir of Saint Peter and the rightful guardian of the Church’s unity. His many sermons and epistles, written in dignified Latin in the tradition of the Western fathers such as Augustine, made him a model of papal authority.

His greatest doctrinal act was the Tome of Leo, a letter expounding how the divine and human natures are joined undivided in the one person of Christ, against the contrary teaching of the Monophysites. Read out at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, it was hailed by the assembled bishops with the cry that Peter had spoken through Leo, and it became, alongside the work of Cyril of Alexandria, a cornerstone of orthodox belief about Christ.

Twice he stood between Rome and destruction. In 452 he rode north to meet Attila the Hun and was credited with turning the invader back from Italy; and in 455, when the Vandals under Gaiseric fell upon the defenceless city, Leo confronted their king and won at least a promise to spare the inhabitants from slaughter and Rome from the torch. Honoured as Leo the Great and a Doctor of the Church, he died in 461, the most commanding pope of the early Christian centuries.
