Charles Baudelaire

French poet and critic, 1821–1867

Baudelaire was the founder of modern poetry, the dark dandy of Paris who found beauty in the city’s squalor, vice, and ennui. Squandering an inheritance on a life of bohemian extravagance, hashish, and debt, he poured his genius into a single great book of verse, Les Fleurs du mal (The Flowers of Evil), published in 1857. Its unsparing treatment of sex, decay, and spleen led to prosecution for offending public morals, and six poems were banned—yet the volume transformed the art forever, fusing classical form with a wholly new urban, psychological modernity.

He was also a critic of penetrating brilliance, the champion of the painter Manet and of the idea of “the painter of modern life,” and a pioneer of the prose poem in Le Spleen de Paris. Across the Atlantic he discovered a kindred spirit in Edgar Allan Poe, whose tales he translated into French with such devotion and skill that the American writer became, through Baudelaire, a giant of European letters.

Ravaged by illness and addiction, harried by creditors, Baudelaire suffered a stroke that left him aphasic, and died in his mother’s arms in 1867, only forty-six. The poets who followed—Verlaine, Rimbaud, Mallarmé, and the whole Symbolist movement—hailed him as their master, and Victor Hugo told him he had given art “a new shudder.”

The road continues to Edgar Allan Poe.
