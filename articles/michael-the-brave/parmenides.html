Parmenides

Eleatic philosopher, c. 515–450 BCE

Parmenides was the founder of the Eleatic school of philosophy, born around 515 BCE at Elea, a Greek colony on the coast of southern Italy. He set out his thought in a difficult hexameter poem, On Nature, in which a goddess reveals to him two paths: the Way of Truth and the Way of Opinion, the deceptive beliefs of ordinary mortals.

His central argument overturned the assumptions of earlier thinkers. Reason, he insisted, shows that “what is” simply is, while “what is not” cannot even be thought or spoken. From this he concluded that Being must be one, eternal, ungenerated, indivisible, and changeless—for change would require something to arise from nothing. The world of motion, plurality, and becoming that the senses report is therefore mere illusion.

This austere monism was the most radical claim in early Greek philosophy, and every later thinker had to reckon with it. Empedocles, the atomists, Plato, and Aristotle all framed their systems partly as responses to Parmenides's challenge that nothing can truly come to be or pass away.

His doctrine was defended by his devoted pupil Zeno of Elea, whose famous paradoxes of motion were designed to show that the commonsense world of change collapses into absurdity. In Plato's dialogue named for him, the aged Parmenides is shown instructing a young Socrates, a tribute to his towering authority. Though little of his poem survives, Parmenides stands as the father of metaphysics and of the rigorous use of logical argument in philosophy.
