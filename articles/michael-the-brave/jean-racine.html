Jean Racine

French dramatist, 1639–1699

Orphaned as a child, Racine received a rigorous classical education from the austere Jansenists of Port-Royal, steeping himself in Greek tragedy—a debt that would later sit uneasily with his worldly career on the stage. Arriving in Paris, he had his first plays produced by Molière’s company, but soon transferred his work to a rival troupe and lured away its leading actress, opening a lasting breach between the two men.

Over a single brilliant decade he perfected French classical tragedy. In Andromaque, Britannicus, Bérénice, and his supreme Phèdre, he stripped the form to its essentials—a few highborn characters consumed by passion, jealousy, and fate—and gave their torment voice in verse of unmatched purity and music. His rise provoked the jealousy of the ageing Corneille’s admirers and bitter cabals against him.

At the peak of his success Racine abruptly abandoned the public theatre, reconciled with his old Jansenist teachers, married, and was appointed royal historiographer to Louis XIV alongside his close friend the critic Boileau. Only late in life did he write again for the stage, composing the sacred dramas Esther and Athalie for the girls of Madame de Maintenon’s school. With Corneille and Molière he is counted one of the three masters of France’s classical age.
