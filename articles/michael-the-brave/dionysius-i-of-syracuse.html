Dionysius I of Syracuse

Tyrant of Syracuse, c. 432–367 BCE

Dionysius I was tyrant of Syracuse for nearly four decades, from 405 to 367 BCE, and the most powerful Greek ruler of his day west of the Aegean. He rose to power as a young officer amid the panic of the Carthaginian invasions of Sicily, exploiting the crisis to make himself master of the city, which he then held by a combination of mercenary armies, fortifications, and ruthless vigilance.

He waged repeated wars against Carthage for control of Sicily, with mixed fortunes, and extended Syracusan power across the sea into southern Italy and the Adriatic, making his city the head of a small empire. He ringed Syracuse with massive walls and built it into one of the strongest fortresses of the Greek world.

His court drew artists and thinkers, and Dionysius fancied himself a poet, entering tragedies in the Athenian festivals, where one at last won first prize. But he was also the archetype of the suspicious despot: from his reign come the famous tales of the Sword of Damocles, hung above a flatterer to reveal the precariousness of a tyrant's life, and the eavesdropping cavern called the Ear of Dionysius. His stormy dealings with the philosopher Plato, whom he is said to have sold into slavery in a fit of rage, became equally legendary.

He died in 367 BCE—by tradition from over-celebrating a dramatic victory—and left his power to his far less capable son, Dionysius II.
