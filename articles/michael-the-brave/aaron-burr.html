Aaron Burr

3rd Vice President of the United States, 1756–1836

Grandson of the great preacher Jonathan Edwards and a precocious Princeton graduate, Burr served bravely in the Revolution before becoming a brilliant lawyer and the architect of New York’s Democratic-Republican machine. In the deadlocked election of 1800 he tied Thomas Jefferson in the Electoral College; when the House finally chose Jefferson, Burr became vice-president, but the president never trusted the ambitious New Yorker again.

His career was destroyed by his lifelong rivalry with Alexander Hamilton. Goaded beyond endurance, Burr challenged Hamilton to a duel and shot him dead at Weehawken in July 1804, an act that left him reviled though still in office. Out of politics, he drifted west into a shadowy scheme—whether to seize Spanish territory or detach the western states was never clear—that led to his arrest and a sensational trial for treason, from which he was acquitted.

Ruined and in debt, Burr fled to Europe, where in London he found shelter and stimulating company in the household of the philosopher Jeremy Bentham, whose ideas on law and reform he came to admire. He eventually crept back to New York under an assumed name to practise law, outliving the daughter he adored, lost at sea, and dying in obscurity in 1836. The English thinker who took him in during his exile was Jeremy Bentham.
