Hans Christian Andersen

Danish author and fairy-tale writer, 1805–1875

Born into deep poverty in the provincial town of Odense, the son of a sickly shoemaker and a washerwoman, Andersen set out at fourteen for Copenhagen to make his name on the stage. Awkward and ungainly, he failed as a singer and actor, but won the help of patrons who paid for the schooling that turned him into a writer. He produced novels, plays, poems, and lively travel books and longed to be taken seriously for them.

Yet it was the modest little volumes of fairy tales he began publishing in 1835 that made him immortal. “The Little Mermaid,” “The Ugly Duckling,” “The Emperor’s New Clothes,” “The Snow Queen,” and “The Little Match Girl” transformed the folk tale into original art of piercing tenderness and irony, and have since been translated into well over a hundred languages and loved by children and adults the world over.

Restless, vain, and perpetually insecure, he travelled ceaselessly across Europe and counted many writers among his acquaintances, famously overstaying a visit to Charles Dickens in England. As a struggling young author he had felt the lash of a sharp review of his novel from a fellow Dane just beginning his own strange career—the philosopher who would become the father of existentialism. Andersen died, world-famous, in 1875. That early critic was Søren Kierkegaard.
