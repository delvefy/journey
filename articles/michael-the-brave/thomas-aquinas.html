Thomas Aquinas

Dominican theologian and philosopher, c. 1225–1274

Thomas Aquinas was born about 1225 at Roccasecca, a hill castle in the Kingdom of Sicily then ruled by the emperor Frederick II. Sent as a boy to the great abbey of Monte Cassino, he went on to the new University of Naples, where he met two forces that would define his life: the rediscovered philosophy of Aristotle and the recently founded Order of Preachers. When he resolved to become a Dominican friar his horrified noble family seized and held him for a year to break his will, but he would not yield.

At Paris and Cologne he studied under Albertus Magnus, who saw past the silent, heavy-set student his fellows mocked as the Dumb Ox and predicted that his bellowing would one day fill the world. So it did. As a master at Paris and in Italy, Aquinas set himself the boldest intellectual task of the age: to reconcile the pagan reason of Aristotle with the revealed truth of Christianity, insisting that faith and reason, both gifts of the same God, could never finally contradict one another.

The fruit was an immense body of work crowned by the unfinished Summa Theologiae, with its calm marshalling of objections and replies and its famous Five Ways of arguing toward God. He also composed the lucid hymns of Corpus Christi still sung today. In December 1273, after a mystical experience at Mass, he laid down his pen, saying that all he had written seemed to him as straw beside what he had glimpsed. He died a few months later, in 1274, on his way to a council. Canonised and later named a Doctor of the Church, he became the master theologian of Catholicism, his thought a permanent foundation of Western philosophy.
