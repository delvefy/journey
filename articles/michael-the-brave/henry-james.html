Henry James

1843–1916, American-British novelist of consciousness

Henry James was born on Washington Place in lower Manhattan, the second son of the philosophical theologian Henry James Sr. and the brother of William James the psychologist. The father moved the family back and forth across the Atlantic in pursuit of an ideal education — Geneva, Paris, Boulogne, Bonn, Newport — so that the young Henry grew up between cultures, a fact he turned into the great subject of his fiction. A back injury he sustained fighting a fire at Newport in 1861 kept him out of the Civil War and from Harvard Law School into letters: stories in the Atlantic Monthly and the Galaxy, then an extended return to Europe in 1872 from which he never properly came home.

He settled in London in 1876, took British citizenship in his last year as a protest against America's reluctance to enter the war, and produced over forty years one of the largest serious œuvres in English literature. The early phase ran from Roderick Hudson and Daisy Miller through Washington Square to the great Portrait of a Lady (1881). The middle phase included The Bostonians, The Princess Casamassima, and the experimental short novels and ghost stories — The Aspern Papers, The Turn of the Screw. A disastrous attempt to write for the theatre with Guy Domville in 1895, hissed off the stage on its opening night, drove him back to fiction and into his late "major phase" with the great triad The Wings of the Dove (1902), The Ambassadors (1903) and The Golden Bowl (1904).

He kept up an enormous correspondence with Edith Wharton, Joseph Conrad, Robert Louis Stevenson and H. G. Wells, with whom he eventually fell out over the place of form in fiction. From Lamb House at Rye, his retreat from 1898, he dictated his late prose to a typist while pacing the room. A stroke in December 1915 carried him off the following February, three months after his naturalisation. The Order of Merit was awarded him on his deathbed.

The road continues to T. S. Eliot.
