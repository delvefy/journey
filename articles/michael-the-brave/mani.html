Mani

Prophet and founder of Manichaeism, c. 216–274 AD

Mani was born around 216 in Mesopotamia, then under Parthian and soon Sasanian rule, and was raised within a Jewish-Christian baptist sect. From boyhood he reported visions of a heavenly “Twin” who, he said, revealed to him divine truth. About the age of twenty-four he broke with his sect and began to proclaim himself the final messenger of God.

His teaching was a grand and austere dualism: the cosmos is the battleground of two eternal principles, Light and Darkness, spirit and matter, and the human soul is a spark of light trapped in the corruption of the body, to be freed through knowledge and the renunciation of the flesh. Mani deliberately gathered up what he saw as the true revelations of earlier prophets—Zoroaster in Persia, the Buddha in India, and Jesus in the West—and presented his own faith as their fulfilment and seal.

Uniquely among the founders of religions, he wrote down his own scriptures and even illustrated them, to guard his message against corruption. He travelled as far as India, and won the favour of the Sasanian king Shapur I, who let him preach freely throughout the empire. From there Manichaeism spread astonishingly far—west into the Roman world and North Africa, where the young Augustine followed it for years, and east along the Silk Road into Central Asia and China.

Under a later king, Bahram I, and pressed by the entrenched Zoroastrian priesthood, Mani fell from favour, was imprisoned, and died in chains around 274. Yet the religion he founded endured for more than a thousand years, one of the great missionary faiths of antiquity.
