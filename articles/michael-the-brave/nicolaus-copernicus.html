Nicolaus Copernicus

Polish astronomer of the heliocentric revolution, 1473–1543

Nicolaus Copernicus was born at Torún in Poland and educated at Kraków and in Italy, where he studied law, medicine, and the mathematics of the heavens. For most of his life he served as a canon of the cathedral at Frombork, managing estates and church affairs while pursuing astronomy in his spare hours. Out of that patient private study came the idea that would overturn the ancient picture of the universe.

Since Ptolemy, scholars had placed a motionless Earth at the centre of the cosmos. Copernicus, dissatisfied with the tangle of circles this required, proposed instead that the sun stands at the centre and that the Earth is a planet like the others, turning daily on its axis and circling the sun once a year. This heliocentric model explained the wandering motions of the planets more naturally, and though it kept circular orbits and was not in every respect simpler, it was a profound break with two thousand years of belief.

Cautious of controversy, he circulated his ideas quietly for decades and only consented to full publication near the end of his life. His great book, On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, dedicated to Pope Paul III, is said to have reached him on his deathbed in 1543. The revolution it began was carried forward by Kepler, who gave the orbits their true elliptical shape, and Galileo, whose telescope furnished the evidence—and it forever changed humanity’s sense of its place in the cosmos.

The road continues to Tycho Brahe.
