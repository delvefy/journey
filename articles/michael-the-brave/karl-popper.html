Karl Popper

Austrian-British philosopher of science, 1902–1994

Karl Raimund Popper was born in 1902 into a cultivated assimilated Jewish family in Vienna, the son of a lawyer and bibliophile. He read widely as a schoolboy in physics, mathematics, and political theory, briefly flirted with Marxism after the violent street fighting of 1919, and turned against it under the impression that its theory was so flexible it explained every outcome and could not be refuted by any.

From this came the central idea of his Logic of Scientific Discovery, published in Vienna in 1934 and in English in 1959: a scientific theory is one that forbids certain observations, and is to be judged not by how many cases confirm it but by whether it can in principle be falsified by an experiment. Induction, the old problem of David Hume, cannot justify scientific knowledge; theories are bold conjectures that survive only as long as no observation refutes them. The Vienna Circle around Carnap and Schlick read him as a fellow traveller; Popper insisted he was their opponent and called himself "the official Opposition" to logical positivism.

The Anschluss drove him to a lectureship in New Zealand, where during the war he wrote the work that made him famous outside philosophy: The Open Society and Its Enemies. The two volumes traced the totalitarian temptation to Plato, to Hegel, and to Marx, and defended a liberal political order in which institutions could be peacefully criticised and replaced. He moved in 1946 to the London School of Economics and there, in 1947, occurred the celebrated ten-minute encounter at a Cambridge seminar in which Ludwig Wittgenstein, brandishing a fire poker, demanded an example of a moral rule; Popper supplied "not to threaten visiting lecturers with pokers."

He held the LSE chair in logic and scientific method until his retirement, debated with Thomas Kuhn over the structure of scientific change, sparred warmly with his pupil Imre Lakatos, and after his wife's death in 1985 lived alone in Croydon until his own death in 1994. The Austrian-British economist who had brought him to LSE, his lifelong friend, and the great twentieth-century champion of liberal markets against central planning was Friedrich Hayek.
