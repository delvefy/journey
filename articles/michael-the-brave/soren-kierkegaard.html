Søren Kierkegaard

Danish philosopher, father of existentialism, 1813–1855

Born in Copenhagen to a prosperous, deeply religious, and melancholy father whose brooding guilt shadowed the household, Kierkegaard studied theology and philosophy and might have settled into a comfortable life had he not broken off his engagement to Regine Olsen—a wound that haunted and fed his writing ever after. From it poured an astonishing torrent of books, many issued under elaborate pseudonyms that argued with one another.

In Either/Or, Fear and Trembling, and The Sickness unto Death he probed dread, despair, and the “leap of faith,” insisting that truth is subjectivity and that the single existing individual, in fear and trembling before God, matters more than any grand system of thought. He aimed this assault squarely at the all-embracing philosophy of Hegel, which he believed had dissolved the living person into abstraction.

His very first book had been a critical review of a novel by his countryman Hans Christian Andersen, and he ended his life in a furious public “attack upon Christendom,” accusing the comfortable Danish Church of betraying the New Testament. Worn out and embattled, he collapsed in the street and died in 1855, only forty-two, and largely unread outside Denmark. A century later he was recognized as the forerunner of existentialism, shaping Heidegger, Sartre, and modern theology. The philosopher whose towering system he spent his life resisting was Hegel.
