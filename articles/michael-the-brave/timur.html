Timur

Turco-Mongol conqueror, founder of the Timurid Empire, 1336–1405

Timur was born on 9 April 1336 at Kesh, south of Samarkand, into the Turkicised Mongol clan of the Barlas. He came to manhood as a minor warlord in the troubled aftermath of the Chagatai khanate, picked up the wounds in his right leg and arm that gave him the Persian byname Timur-i Lang — Timur the Lame, Tamerlane to Europe — and by 1370 had killed his patron and brother-in-law Husayn at Balkh and made himself master of Transoxiana. He never assumed the title of khan, because he was not a descendant of Chinggis Khan; he ruled as “amir” in the name of puppet Chagatayid figureheads, and used the prestige of son-in-law to a Chinggisid princess, Saray Mulk Khanum, to legitimise his court at Samarkand.

For thirty-five years he campaigned almost without pause across the heartlands of the old Mongol empire. He devastated the Golden Horde of his protégé-turned-rival Tokhtamysh at the Kondurcha (1391) and the Terek (1395), burning Sarai and breaking the steppe power that had held Russia in tribute. He sacked Isfahan in 1387, building a tower of seventy thousand skulls; took Baghdad in 1393 and again, more thoroughly, in 1401; harried Georgia year after year for refusing Islam. The Indian campaign of 1398 culminated in the storming of Delhi in December, the slaughter of its inhabitants, and the extraction of its craftsmen and elephants for the workshops of Samarkand. The Syrian campaign of 1400–01 took Aleppo and Damascus; the Mamluk sultan’s envoys included the Tunisian historian Ibn Khaldun, who left a remarkable record of the interviews held with Timur in the camp outside the walls.

The clash with Bayezid the Thunderbolt at Ankara on 28 July 1402 settled the eastern question for a generation: the Ottoman empire shattered, and Anatolia returned to the patchwork of beyliks Bayezid had absorbed. Timur turned next to the conquest of Ming China, set out across the Syr Darya in mid-winter, and died on 17 or 18 February 1405 at Otrar, the snowed-in border town, before his vanguard had reached the desert. He was buried at Samarkand under the blue dome of the Gur-i Amir, which his grandson Ulugh Beg finished. The empire dissolved within a decade into the principalities of his sons; the Timurid renaissance of letters and astronomy that followed, however, would feed directly into Mughal India through his descendant Babur.

The road continues to Mehmed I.
