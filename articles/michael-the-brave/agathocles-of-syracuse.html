Agathocles of Syracuse

Tyrant and king of Syracuse, 361–289 BCE

Agathocles was the tyrant, and later self-proclaimed king, of Syracuse, the most powerful Greek city of Sicily, and one of the boldest adventurers of the Hellenistic age. The son of a potter, he rose through the army and through popular agitation, and in 317 BCE he seized supreme power in a coup accompanied by a massacre of his aristocratic opponents.

His reign was dominated by the long Sicilian struggle against Carthage. When a Carthaginian army drove him back and besieged him in Syracuse itself, Agathocles conceived an audacious stroke: rather than wait to be starved out, he slipped a fleet through the blockade in 310 BCE and carried the war into Africa, ravaging Carthage's own territory and throwing the great city onto the defensive—a gamble that long foreshadowed the strategy of the Romans.

In Africa he sought reinforcement from Ophellas, the lord of Cyrene, but treacherously murdered his ally and absorbed his troops. The African campaign ultimately failed, and Agathocles returned to Sicily to patch up a peace with Carthage.

Around 304 BCE he took the title of king in imitation of Alexander's successors, married his daughter Lanassa to Pyrrhus of Epirus, and extended his power into southern Italy. He died in 289 BCE, by some accounts poisoned, and his death plunged Syracuse back into disorder. Later historians judged him a man of extraordinary energy and ruthlessness.
