Joseph Conrad

1857–1924, Polish-British novelist of the sea and of empire

Józef Teodor Konrad Korzeniowski was born in Berdychiv in the Russian-partitioned Ukraine to Apollo Korzeniowski, a Polish patriot poet, and Ewa Bobrowska. Both parents died in exile of the tuberculosis they contracted after Russian arrest, and the boy was raised by his uncle Tadeusz Bobrowski in Cracow and Lwów. At sixteen, refusing conscription into the Russian army, he left for Marseille and at twenty-one for the British merchant marine. Over the next sixteen years he served in every rank from ordinary seaman to master, sailed to Bombay, Singapore, Bangkok and Borneo, took a steamboat up the Congo in 1890 — a journey that ruined his health and gave him the matter of Heart of Darkness — and gained his British naturalisation in 1886.

He came ashore in 1894 with the manuscript of Almayer's Folly, his first novel, written in his third language. The Nigger of the Narcissus followed in 1897, Lord Jim in 1900, and over the next twenty years a sequence of works that effectively founded the modern English novel of moral isolation under pressure: Heart of Darkness, Nostromo, The Secret Agent, Under Western Eyes, Chance and Victory. Critical respect came earlier than money; he was helped through long stretches of penury by loans from the publisher J. M. Dent and grants from the Royal Literary Fund.

He married the typist Jessie George in 1896, raised two sons in a series of rented houses in Kent, and counted Henry James, Stephen Crane, Ford Madox Ford, H. G. Wells, John Galsworthy and Bertrand Russell among his neighbours and friends. Chance, serialised in 1913, finally made him popular; he refused a knighthood in 1924 because he believed art was its own honour, and that same year, on the morning of 3 August, he died of a heart attack at his house Oswalds at Bishopsbourne. He is buried in the Roman Catholic cemetery at Canterbury.
