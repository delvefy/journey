Romain Rolland

1866–1944, French novelist, biographer, and pacifist, Nobel laureate

Romain Rolland was born in Clamecy, Burgundy, the son of a notary, and educated at the Lycée Saint-Louis and the École Normale Supérieure, where he took the agrégation in history in 1889. A scholarship at the École Française de Rome between 1889 and 1891 introduced him to Malwida von Meysenbug, the Wagnerite intimate of Nietzsche and Mazzini, and through her to the European literary world. He took a doctorate on the history of opera before Lully and Scarlatti and taught the history of music at the Sorbonne and the École Normale.

His ten-volume novel Jean-Christophe, the life of a German composer of genius modelled in part on Beethoven, appeared in instalments between 1904 and 1912 in the Cahiers de la Quinzaine of Charles Péguy and won him the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1915. The award came in the middle of the Great War, which he denounced from neutral Switzerland in his pamphlet Au-dessus de la mêlée ("Above the Battle"), a stance that cost him the goodwill of patriots on every side and made him one of the great voices of European pacifism. He spent the rest of his life at Villeneuve on the Lake of Geneva and from 1938 in Vézelay.

He produced popular biographies of Beethoven, Michelangelo, Tolstoy and Handel and three studies of Indian thought — the lives of Ramakrishna, Vivekananda, and a Mahatma Gandhi he met at Villeneuve in 1931. He corresponded for decades with Sigmund Freud about religious feeling (it was Rolland who coined the phrase "oceanic feeling"), with Albert Einstein about disarmament, with Stefan Zweig and Hermann Hesse in an unbroken European republic of letters, with Tolstoy from his youth and with Gorky and Tagore through his last years. He greeted the Soviet Union enthusiastically and visited Stalin in 1935, an enthusiasm he later qualified in his private journals. He died at Vézelay in December 1944 under returning French liberation.
