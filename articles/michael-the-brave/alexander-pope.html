Alexander Pope

English poet, 1688–1744.

Born in Lombard Street in the City of London to a wealthy linen-draper of recusant Catholic stock, Pope was barred by the Test Acts from the English universities and educated by an underground priest at home, where Pott's disease — tubercular spinal infection — gave him in childhood the disfiguring hunch and the four-foot-six adult height that he would later complain about and exploit in self-portraiture. He read precociously in his father's library at Binfield in Windsor Forest, learned French, Italian, Greek, and Latin without a tutor, and at twelve was already corresponding with literary elders.

The 1709 Pastorals, the 1711 Essay on Criticism — a verse manifesto for English neoclassicism modelled on Boileau's Art poétique — and the 1712 mock-epic The Rape of the Lock, an iridescent satire on a society quarrel about a stolen curl, made him at twenty-four the most discussed living English poet. With Jonathan Swift, John Gay, John Arbuthnot, and Thomas Parnell he founded in 1714 the Scriblerus Club, the loose Tory wits' alliance that would shape a generation of satire. His translation of Homer's Iliad (1715–20) and his collaborative Odyssey (1725–26) sold by subscription so successfully that they bought him financial independence and the lease on the villa at Twickenham, with its famous grotto down to the Thames, where he gardened, entertained, and wrote for the rest of his life.

The accession of George I had driven his Tory friends out of office; from Twickenham he conducted a long ground war in verse against the Whig establishment and its lesser hacks. The four-book Dunciad (1728, expanded 1743) catalogued his enemies with a malicious precision that became a literary form; the Essay on Man (1733–34) versified Bolingbroke's deistic optimism into the most-quoted philosophical poem in English ("Whatever is, is right"); the Imitations of Horace set his own grievances and friendships against the originals in parallel pages. He never married, suffered increasingly from the spinal disease that had warped him, and died at Twickenham in 1744 attended by his closest friend Martha Blount, leaving Pope's hill on every English landscape as a literary type.
