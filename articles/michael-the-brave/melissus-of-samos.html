Melissus of Samos

Eleatic philosopher and Samian commander, 5th century BCE

Melissus of Samos was the last of the great philosophers of the Eleatic school and the only one not from Elea itself. Following Parmenides and Zeno, he defended and recast their teaching that all reality is a single, unchanging One, setting out his arguments in a prose treatise, On Nature, or On What Is.

He agreed that Being is one, eternal, and motionless, but pressed the logic further in a striking way: where Parmenides had pictured Being as a finite, bounded sphere, Melissus argued that it must be infinite in extent as well as in time, for there is nothing outside it to limit it. He held too that what truly is must be incorporeal. These refinements made him an important, if much criticised, link in early Greek metaphysics; Aristotle took his arguments seriously enough to set out to refute them.

Unlike his fellow philosophers, Melissus was also a man of affairs. As a leading citizen and admiral of Samos, he commanded its fleet during the island's revolt against Athenian domination. In the Samian War of 440 BCE he won a notable victory over the Athenian navy—then led by Pericles himself—before Athens regrouped and at last reduced the island.

Little of his writing survives beyond fragments and the summaries of his critics, but his union of bold philosophical speculation with practical command made him a singular figure: the philosopher-admiral who briefly bested the greatest power in Greece.
