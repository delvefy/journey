Aristides

Athenian statesman, c. 530–468 BCE

Aristides, called "the Just," was an Athenian statesman and general of the Persian War generation, remembered as much for his reputation for fairness as for his service to his city. He fought as one of the ten generals at Marathon in 490 BCE and emerged as a leading figure of the early democracy, where he became the great political rival of the more flexible and ambitious Themistocles.

Their rivalry led to his ostracism around 482 BCE. A famous story, told by Plutarch, has an illiterate countryman, not recognizing him, ask Aristides to scratch the name "Aristides" on his ostrakon; when Aristides asked whether the man had been wronged by him, he answered only that he was tired of hearing him everywhere called "the Just"—whereupon Aristides calmly wrote his own name.

Recalled when Xerxes invaded, he set the quarrel aside, fought at Salamis in 480, and the next year commanded the Athenian contingent at the decisive land victory of Plataea. In the war's aftermath he played a central part in organizing the Delian League, the alliance of Greek states against Persia, and was entrusted with the famously equitable assessment of each member's tribute.

He is said to have died around 468 BCE so poor that the state had to provide dowries for his daughters—a fittingly austere end for the man whom Athenians held up as the very model of public integrity.
