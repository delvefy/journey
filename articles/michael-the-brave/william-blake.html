William Blake

English poet, painter, and printmaker, 1757–1827

Trained as an engraver and largely ignored in his lifetime, Blake was a visionary in the most literal sense, claiming to see angels and the spirits of the dead from childhood. He invented a method of “illuminated printing” that fused his poetry and his designs on a single etched plate, and through it issued the works that would one day make him one of the most original figures in English art and letters.

His Songs of Innocence and of Experience set the gentle and the terrible side by side—the meek Lamb against the burning Tyger—to show “the two contrary states of the human soul.” In longer prophetic books such as The Marriage of Heaven and Hell and Jerusalem he built a vast private mythology in revolt against tyranny, organised religion, and the “dark Satanic Mills” of the new industrial age. He moved on the radical fringe of London, in the circle of Mary Wollstonecraft and Thomas Paine.

As an artist he scorned the oil-painting orthodoxy of the Royal Academy and its president Joshua Reynolds, working instead in watercolour and engraving on biblical and literary themes. He revered John Milton above all poets and produced luminous illustrations to Milton, to the Book of Job, and, at the very end of his life, to Dante. He died in poverty in 1827, singing, and was later recognised as a prophet of the imagination.
