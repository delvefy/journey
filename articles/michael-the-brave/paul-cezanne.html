Paul Cézanne

French Post-Impressionist painter, 1839–1906

Born in Aix-en-Provence to a prosperous banker, Cézanne defied his father’s wish that he study law and went to Paris to paint, encouraged by his boyhood friend Émile Zola. His early work was dark and turbulent, but under the patient guidance of the older Impressionist Camille Pissarro he learned to work directly from nature in bright, broken colour, exhibiting with the Impressionists in the 1870s.

Dissatisfied with mere fleeting impressions, he set himself a deeper goal: “to make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the museums.” Retreating increasingly to Provence, he built his pictures from patches of carefully modulated colour, analysing the underlying geometry of the world—treating nature, as he put it, “by means of the cylinder, the sphere, and the cone.” In endless still lifes of apples, portraits of card players, and views of his beloved Mont Sainte-Victoire, he reconstructed solid form on the flat canvas with a slow, searching gravity.

Little understood in his lifetime and working in growing isolation, he was hailed by the next generation as a liberator. Both Henri Matisse and Pablo Picasso called him their master, and his dismantling of traditional perspective led directly to Cubism and the whole adventure of modern art. He died in 1906, justly remembered as the father of modern painting.
