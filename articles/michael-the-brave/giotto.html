Giotto

Florentine painter and architect, c.1267–1337

Giotto di Bondone was born about 1267 at Colle di Vespignano, in the Mugello north of Florence, the son of a smallholder. The tradition recorded by Vasari that Cimabue discovered him as a shepherd boy drawing a sheep on a stone is most likely legend, but Cimabue did become his master, and by the 1290s Giotto was running the leading Florentine workshop and breaking decisively with the Byzantine manner of his teachers. In the late 1290s and around the Jubilee of 1300 he worked in Rome — the Boniface VIII fresco fragment at the Lateran is plausibly his — and the cycle of the Life of St Francis in the Upper Basilica at Assisi, whether by his hand or by his close collaborators, set the new direction: bulky bodies in real space, faces and gestures registering thought.

His indisputable masterpiece is the Arena Chapel at Padua, painted about 1303–1305 for the banker Enrico Scrovegni in expiation of his father's usury. The thirty-eight scenes of the lives of the Virgin and Christ, set against a blue ground, build a continuous narrative in painted architecture: figures step into and out of the picture plane; pity, anger, and grief are the engine of the story; the Lamentation over the dead Christ, with the angels arching overhead in horror, may be the first wholly modern picture in European art. After Padua he painted the Bardi and Peruzzi chapels in Santa Croce in Florence, the Crucifix in Santa Maria Novella, and panel altarpieces of unprecedented monumentality.

He was a friend of Dante's, named in Purgatorio XI as the supplanter of Cimabue's fame, and Boccaccio devoted one of the Decameron's tales to his short, ugly, witty person. From 1328 to 1333 he was court painter to King Robert of Naples; in 1334 the Signoria of Florence appointed him capomaestro of the cathedral and city walls, and he designed the lower stories of the Campanile that still bears his name. He died on 8 January 1337 and was buried in Santa Reparata, then in the new cathedral that rose around it.
