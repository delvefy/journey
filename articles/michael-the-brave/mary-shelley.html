Mary Shelley

English novelist, author of Frankenstein, 1797–1851

Mary Shelley was born to a remarkable pair of radical minds: the feminist philosopher Mary Wollstonecraft, who died days after the birth, and the political theorist William Godwin. Raised in a house alive with ideas, at sixteen she fell in love with the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley and ran away with him to the Continent, marrying him after the suicide of his first wife. Theirs was a passionate, grief-stricken union, marked by the deaths of three of their children.

In the rainy summer of 1816, lodged near Lord Byron beside Lake Geneva, the company amused themselves by composing ghost stories. From Mary’s came one of the most influential novels ever written. Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus, published in 1818 when she was only twenty, tells of a student who assembles and animates a living creature, then recoils from his creation, with tragic consequences. At once Gothic horror, philosophical fable, and a founding work of science fiction, it has never ceased to grip the imagination.

After Percy drowned in 1822, Mary returned to England a widow at twenty-four and supported herself and her surviving son by her pen. She wrote further novels, including the visionary plague-romance The Last Man, edited and championed her husband’s poetry, and secured his place in the canon. She died in 1851, her own dark masterpiece long since woven into the modern myth of science and its perils.
