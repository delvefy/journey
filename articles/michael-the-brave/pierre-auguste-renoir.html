Pierre-Auguste Renoir

French Impressionist painter, 1841–1919

Renoir began as a painter of porcelain, and a love of pretty, glowing surfaces never left him. As a struggling young artist in Paris he formed a close friendship with Claude Monet, and the two worked side by side along the Seine, dissolving solid form into shimmering dabs of sunlight and reflection—experiments that helped give birth to Impressionism. He exhibited in the movement’s first independent shows in the 1870s amid public ridicule.

Where others painted landscapes, Renoir was above all a painter of people and pleasure. His Bal du moulin de la Galette and Luncheon of the Boating Party capture the dappled gaiety of Parisian leisure, crowded with laughing friends under sun and shade; his nudes and portraits glow with warm, rounded flesh. “Why shouldn’t art be pretty?” he asked. “There are enough unpleasant things in the world.”

In later life he travelled to Italy, absorbed the lessons of Raphael and the old masters, and moved toward a more classical solidity. Crippled by rheumatoid arthritis, he settled in the warmth of the south at Cagnes, where he was visited by the admiring younger Matisse, and painted on with the brush strapped to his deformed hand almost to the end. He died in 1919, the most sensuous and beloved of the Impressionists.
