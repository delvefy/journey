Macrina the Younger

Ascetic and teacher, c. 327–379 AD

Macrina was the eldest of the ten children of a wealthy and pious Cappadocian family, born around 327 and named for her grandmother, herself a celebrated Christian raised in an older tradition of the faith. Betrothed in girlhood, she lost her intended husband to an early death; rather than accept another match, she declared herself faithful to him beyond the grave and vowed to remain a virgin devoted to God.

She became the spiritual centre of her remarkable family. After her father died, she helped her mother Emmelia raise the younger children, and her example turned her gifted brothers from worldly ambition toward the service of the Church—Basil, who became the great bishop of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, and Peter of Sebaste. Her brother Gregory honoured her simply as “the Teacher.”

On the family estate at Annesi in Pontus she and her mother transformed their household into a monastic community, where mistresses and former servants lived side by side as equals in prayer, work, and simplicity. It became one of the early models of organised female monastic life in the Christian East.

She died in 379, only months after Basil. Gregory of Nyssa, arriving to find her dying, recorded the scene in his Life of Macrina and in the dialogue On the Soul and the Resurrection, in which the serene and learned Macrina, like a Christian Socrates, discourses on death and the life to come even as she expires. Through these works she became one of the most vividly remembered holy women of the early Church.
