Cardinal Wolsey

Lord Chancellor of England, Archbishop of York, c.1473–1530

Thomas Wolsey was born about 1473 at Ipswich, the son of a successful butcher and cattle-dealer. He took his BA at Magdalen College, Oxford at fifteen — earning the tag "the boy bachelor" — was elected fellow, and after ordination in 1498 became chaplain to Henry VII, who sent him on a swift embassy to Maximilian I in 1508. Henry VIII, succeeding in 1509, recognised the young almoner's capacity for sustained administrative work, and the war with France of 1512–1514 gave Wolsey the chance to display it: he organised the supply, the shipping, and the politics of the campaign that won Tournai. The bishoprics of Tournai, Lincoln, and York followed; he was named cardinal by Pope Leo X in 1515 and lord chancellor of England the same year.

For fifteen years thereafter Wolsey was, after the king, the most powerful man in England. He reformed the Court of Chancery and the Star Chamber, raised English revenues through innovative subsidies, and presided as papal legate a latere over an English church under his personal direction from 1518. In foreign policy he balanced between Francis I and Charles V, staging the Field of Cloth of Gold in 1520, the Treaty of London in 1518 with its general peace, and later turning toward an imperial alliance against France. He suppressed twenty-nine small monasteries to endow Cardinal College at Oxford and a grammar school at Ipswich, and built Hampton Court Palace on a scale that ultimately had to be presented to the king.

His failure to obtain the annulment of Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon from Pope Clement VII destroyed him. The legatine court at Blackfriars in 1529 was revoked to Rome; Anne Boleyn's faction pressed; he was indicted under praemunire in October 1529, stripped of the chancellorship, and exiled to his see of York. Arrested for treason in November 1530, he died on the journey south at Leicester Abbey on 29 November 1530.
