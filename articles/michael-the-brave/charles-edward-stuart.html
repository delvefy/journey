Charles Edward Stuart

“Bonnie Prince Charlie,” the Young Pretender, 1720–1788

Charles Edward Stuart, remembered as Bonnie Prince Charlie, was the last Stuart claimant to make a real bid for the British throne. Born in Rome in 1720, the grandson of the deposed James II, he was raised in the exiled Jacobite court and grew into a charming, ardent young man fired by his family's lost cause.

In 1745 he gambled everything. Landing in the Scottish Highlands with almost no foreign help, he raised the clans, proclaimed his father king, and swept into Edinburgh. He scattered a government army at Prestonpans and then led his Highlanders deep into England, reaching Derby before his cautious officers, despairing of English or French support, forced him to turn back.

The retreat was fatal. On the moor of Culloden in April 1746, the last pitched battle fought on British soil, the Duke of Cumberland destroyed his exhausted army in under an hour, and savage reprisals fell upon the Highlands afterward. With a great price on his head, the prince became a fugitive, hunted through the glens and islands for five months, sheltered by loyal clansfolk and famously spirited to safety by Flora MacDonald before he escaped by sea to France.

The cause died at Culloden, and so, slowly, did the man. His later decades were a long decline of wandering, disappointment, and drink, and he died in Rome in 1788, the romance of his legend already outgrowing the truth of his life. The blood of warrior kings ran in him: his mother was the granddaughter of the Polish hero who had saved Vienna from the Turks, John III Sobieski.
