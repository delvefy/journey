Aeschylus

Athenian tragedian, c. 525–456 BCE

Aeschylus, the earliest of the three great tragic poets of Athens, is justly called the father of tragedy. Born around 525 BCE at Eleusis, he came of age as Athens threw off tyranny and faced the onslaught of the Persian Empire. He fought in the front line at the Battle of Marathon in 490, where his brother fell, and probably again at Salamis in 480—and it was of his soldiership at Marathon, not his poetry, that his epitaph chose to boast.

He transformed the early choral performances into true drama by introducing a second actor, so that characters could confront one another in dialogue and conflict, while reducing the role of the chorus. Tradition credits him with some seventy or more plays and around thirteen victories in the dramatic festivals.

Seven of his tragedies survive. The Persians, staged in 472 with the young Pericles as its sponsor, is unique as a drama drawn from recent history—the defeat of Xerxes at Salamis, told from within the grieving Persian court, with the ghost of the old king Darius rising to lament his son's folly. His masterpiece is the Oresteia, the only complete trilogy to come down to us, which follows a chain of murder and vengeance in the house of Agamemnon to its resolution in the first trial by jury at Athens.

Late in life he travelled to Sicily, to the court of the tyrant of Syracuse, and there he died at Gela in 456 BCE—by a famous legend, killed when an eagle dropped a tortoise upon his bald head. His grand, solemn vision of divine justice working through human suffering laid the foundations of Western drama.
