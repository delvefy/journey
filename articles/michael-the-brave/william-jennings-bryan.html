William Jennings Bryan

1860–1925, American populist orator, three-time Democratic presidential nominee

William Jennings Bryan was born in Salem, Illinois, the son of a Jacksonian Democrat circuit judge and a devout Baptist mother. He read for the law at Union College of Law in Chicago, moved his practice to Lincoln, Nebraska, in 1887, and within three years was elected to Congress on a platform of free silver, lower tariffs and railroad regulation. Two terms in the House made him the most prominent young silverite in the West.

At the Democratic national convention in Chicago in 1896 he gave the “Cross of Gold” speech, an attack on the gold standard that ended with the cry “you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold.” The hall roared, and the delegates, repudiating Grover Cleveland's monetary policy, nominated him at thirty-six, the youngest major-party candidate in American history. Outspent ten to one by Mark Hanna and the Republicans, he travelled eighteen thousand miles by rail and gave hundreds of speeches but lost to William McKinley. He was renominated in 1900, denouncing the annexation of the Philippines, and again in 1908, when William Howard Taft defeated him.

From 1913 he served as Woodrow Wilson's Secretary of State, negotiating thirty “cooling-off” treaties for compulsory arbitration. He resigned in June 1915 rather than sign the second Lusitania note, judging it too provocative toward Germany. After the war he campaigned for prohibition and for the suffrage of women and worked Florida real estate.

His last public act was the prosecution of John T. Scopes in Dayton, Tennessee, in July 1925 for teaching evolution. Clarence Darrow placed him on the witness stand to defend a literal reading of Genesis, and the cross-examination, broadcast nationally and savagely reported by H.L. Mencken, was widely judged a humiliation. Bryan died in his sleep five days after the verdict.
