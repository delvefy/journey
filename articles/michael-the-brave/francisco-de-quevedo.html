Francisco de Quevedo

Spanish poet and satirist, 1580–1645

One of the towering figures of the Spanish Golden Age, Quevedo was a nobleman, scholar, courtier, and the most savage wit of his generation. Limping and short-sighted, brilliant and quarrelsome, he mastered every register of Castilian, from the most exalted love sonnet to the gutter slang of the criminal underworld. He carried on a celebrated and venomous literary feud with the poet Luis de Góngora, the two men trading scathing satires that have entertained readers ever since.

His prose is as varied as his verse. The Sueños (Dreams) are biting visions of hell and human folly, and his picaresque novel El Buscón is a masterpiece of cruelty and verbal dazzle, the dark twin of the genre. As a thinker he wrote Stoic and political treatises, steeped in Seneca and a bleak sense of the vanity of worldly things, captured in lines on love that “will be dust, but dust in love.”

Quevedo’s life was as turbulent as his pen. He undertook secret diplomatic missions in Italy, attached himself to the powerful Count-Duke of Olivares, and then fell from favour; in 1639 he was arrested and imprisoned for years in a damp convent cell, his health broken. Released only as the minister himself fell, he died soon after in 1645. He stands beside Cervantes, Lope, and Góngora as a pillar of his nation’s literature.
