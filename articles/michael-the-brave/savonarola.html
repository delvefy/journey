Savonarola

Dominican preacher, prophet of Florence, 1452–1498

Girolamo Savonarola was born at Ferrara on 21 September 1452, the third of seven children of a court physician of the Este. He read humane letters under his grandfather, was meant for medicine, and at twenty-three slipped away from a feast to the Dominican convent of San Domenico at Bologna, leaving a treatise on the contempt of the world for his parents to find. He was sent to Florence in 1482 as lector at San Marco, found his Latin Sermons listened to politely and forgotten, and was rotated out to small north Italian houses. The change came at Reggio in 1486 with a sudden access of prophetic preaching on the coming scourge of the Church.

Lorenzo de’ Medici, persuaded by Pico della Mirandola, recalled him to Florence in 1490. From the cathedral pulpit on Septuagesima 1491 he preached the three great propositions of his Florentine career — the Church would be scourged, then renewed, and the time was near — and identified the scourge, when the French crossed the Alps in 1494, with the army of Charles VIII. He persuaded the king to spare the city, dominated the new constitution that followed the expulsion of Piero de’ Medici, and called his Great Council into being on a Venetian model. Florence, in his sermons, became the new Jerusalem; on 7 February 1497 his followers, the fanciulli, gathered the “vanities” — cards, dice, mirrors, profane books, certain paintings — and burnt them on a pyre in the Piazza della Signoria.

Alexander VI, whose private life he had publicly named, excommunicated him in May 1497. He preached against the excommunication and proposed a general council to depose the pope. Florence turned against him as the harvest failed and the rival Franciscans of Santa Croce challenged him to an ordeal by fire on 7 April 1498; on the day, the trial was called off in a downpour, the crowd’s patience snapped, and the next morning San Marco was stormed. He and two brother friars, Domenico Buonvicini and Silvestro Maruffi, were arrested, tortured, and condemned by a hostile commission. On 23 May 1498 they were hanged and then burned in the Piazza della Signoria, on the same spot where the vanities had gone up the year before. The Florentine republic he had inspired survived him by fifteen years.

The road continues to Charles VIII of France.
