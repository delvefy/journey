Clement of Alexandria

Early Christian theologian, c.150–215

Titus Flavius Clemens, known as Clement of Alexandria, was born about 150, probably at Athens, and came to Christianity as an adult after wandering the Mediterranean in search of teachers. He found his master in Pantaenus at Alexandria, and in time succeeded him as head of the Catechetical School there, the great centre of Christian learning in the most cultured city of the East.

Clement was the first major Christian thinker to embrace Greek philosophy openly as an ally of the faith. Philosophy, he argued, had been to the Greeks what the Law was to the Jews — a schoolmaster leading them toward Christ — and he wove Plato and the Stoics together with scripture, borrowing the allegorical method and the Logos theology of his Jewish predecessor Philo. His great trilogy, the Exhortation, the Tutor and the wide-ranging Miscellanies, set out a vision of the mature believer as the “true Gnostic,” deliberately reclaiming the word from the heretical Gnostics such as the followers of Valentinus, whose teachings he combated.

When the persecution under Septimius Severus struck Alexandria around 202, Clement fled the city, and he died, probably in Cappadocia, about 215. He left behind a pupil far greater in fame, Origen, who would carry the Alexandrian synthesis of faith and reason to its height. Long honoured as a saint, Clement was later quietly dropped from the calendar over doubts about his orthodoxy, but his effort to baptize the learning of the Greeks shaped Christian thought for centuries.
