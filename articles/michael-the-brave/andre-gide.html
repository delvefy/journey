André Gide

French writer and Nobel laureate, 1869–1951

Gide was born in Paris into a wealthy and severely Protestant family, and the conflict between that austere conscience and the claims of desire and freedom became the great theme of his life and work. After early Symbolist writings, a journey to North Africa in 1893–95, during which he encountered Oscar Wilde in Algiers, awakened his sense of his own homosexuality and his revolt against inherited morality.

He gave this rebellion lasting form in a series of searching novels and confessions: The Immoralist (1902), Strait Is the Gate (1909), and his most ambitious fiction, The Counterfeiters (1925), with its experiments in self-conscious narration. In 1908 he helped found the Nouvelle Revue Française, which became the most influential literary review in France, though his rejection of Proust’s Swann’s Way for its pages he later called the worst mistake of his career.

Gide was a restless moralist who prized sincerity above all, exposing his own contradictions in candid journals kept across half a century and defending homosexuality openly in Corydon. Drawn for a time to communism, he broke publicly with the Soviet Union after visiting it in 1936 and finding it a new tyranny. Awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1947, he remained to the end a figure of conscience, admired and attacked for his refusal to settle into any fixed belief.
