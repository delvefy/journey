Filippo Brunelleschi

Florentine architect and inventor of perspective, 1377–1446

Filippo was born at Florence in 1377, the son of a notary. He was apprenticed not to law but to a goldsmith in the Arte della Seta and matriculated as a master in 1398. With Donatello, his younger friend, he made the first archaeological journey to Rome about 1402, measuring the standing remains of antiquity at a time when this had occurred to no one; the studies in proportion and construction he brought home reset the question of what architecture could be. He had already, in 1401, lost the celebrated competition for the second pair of Baptistery doors at Florence to Lorenzo Ghiberti, by a single vote of the judges and after years of work; he went home and turned to building.

The problem of the cathedral was already a century old. Arnolfo di Cambio had planned a dome wider than that of the Pantheon and laid the octagonal drum without specifying how it would be roofed; for four generations Florence had said the dome would be built by him whom God should send. In 1418 the Opera del Duomo set a competition, and Brunelleschi proposed to raise the cupola without centring, using a double shell, herringbone brickwork that pinned itself as it climbed, and stone-and-iron tension chains around the haunch — a self-supporting solution drawn partly from the Pantheon and partly from his own invention. He took on Ghiberti as nominal partner and outmanoeuvred him; the lantern was authorised at his death. The dome went up between 1420 and 1436 and was consecrated by Eugenius IV in March 1436 during the Council that brought John VIII Palaiologos to the city.

His sacristy and church of San Lorenzo for the Medici, the Ospedale degli Innocenti with its first orderly arcade of the new style, the Pazzi Chapel at Santa Croce, and the central plan of Santa Maria degli Angeli set out a vocabulary of pietra serena pilasters, white-plastered walls, and modular bays that became the grammar of Italian Renaissance building. He demonstrated linear perspective with two small panels, now lost, one of the Baptistery seen from the cathedral door and one of the Palazzo della Signoria from the corner of the Via dei Calzaiuoli, viewed through a hole pierced in the back of the panel against a mirror. The procedure was written up after his death by Alberti. He died at Florence on 15 April 1446 and was buried in the cathedral, the only Florentine to be so honoured.
