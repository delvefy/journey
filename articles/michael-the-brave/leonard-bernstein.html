Leonard Bernstein

American conductor and composer, 1918–1990.

Born to a Russian-Jewish family in Lawrence, Massachusetts, Bernstein discovered the piano in his aunt's discarded upright and against his father's mercantile wishes pursued music at Harvard, the Curtis Institute, and the Berkshire Music Center at Tanglewood, where Serge Koussevitzky took him as a conducting protégé and Aaron Copland as a compositional friend. His professional debut on 14 November 1943, substituting at five hours' notice for an ill Bruno Walter at the New York Philharmonic and broadcasting nationwide, made him famous overnight at twenty-five.

For the next forty-seven years he was the most visible musician in America. As composer he wrote the symphonies Jeremiah, The Age of Anxiety, and Kaddish, the choral Chichester Psalms, the eclectic Mass commissioned by Jacqueline Kennedy for the opening of the Kennedy Center, and the Broadway scores On the Town, Wonderful Town, Candide, and the 1957 collaboration with Stephen Sondheim and Jerome Robbins, West Side Story, which transplanted Romeo and Juliet to the gang-rivalries of Manhattan. As conductor he led the New York Philharmonic from 1958 to 1969, the first American-born music director of a top United States orchestra, and through the televised Young People's Concerts taught a generation what a sonata, a mode, and a leitmotif were.

He was a great public exhibitor of conviction: a tireless champion of Mahler when the symphonies were still considered marginal, an outspoken supporter of civil rights and nuclear disarmament, host of the 1970 fundraiser for the Black Panthers' legal defense that Tom Wolfe immortalized as "radical chic." On Christmas Day 1989 he conducted Beethoven's Ninth at the fallen Berlin Wall, substituting Freiheit for Freude. He died ten months later, having lived flamboyantly, chain-smoked, and remained, in his own phrase, restlessly secular and unapologetically Jewish.

The road continues to George Gershwin.
