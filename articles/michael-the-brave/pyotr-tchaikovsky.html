Pyotr Tchaikovsky

Russian composer, 1840–1893

Tchaikovsky was born in the provincial town of Votkinsk and first trained for a career in the civil service before abandoning it for music. Among the earliest graduates of the new St Petersburg Conservatory, where he studied under Anton Rubinstein, he soon took a teaching post in Moscow and devoted himself to composition. He stood somewhat apart from the nationalist group known as "The Five," blending Russian feeling with the forms and craft of the Western tradition he revered.

He became the first Russian composer to win lasting international fame. His ballets Swan Lake, The Sleeping Beauty, and The Nutcracker remain the foundation of the classical repertory; his operas Eugene Onegin and The Queen of Spades set the verse of Pushkin; and his symphonies, concertos, the Romeo and Juliet fantasy-overture, and the thunderous 1812 Overture are among the most performed works in all of music. Above every other composer he idolized Mozart, paying him homage in an orchestral suite.

For thirteen years he was sustained by the generous patronage of the widow Nadezhda von Meck, with whom he corresponded intimately but, by mutual agreement, never met. His inner life was troubled, marked by a brief and catastrophic marriage and the strain of concealing his homosexuality. He died in St Petersburg in 1893, only days after conducting the premiere of his anguished Sixth Symphony, the Pathétique, amid lasting controversy over whether the cause was cholera or his own hand.
