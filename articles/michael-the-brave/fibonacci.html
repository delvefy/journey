Fibonacci

Italian mathematician, c.1170–1250

Leonardo of Pisa, remembered as Fibonacci, was born around 1170 in the great Tuscan trading city of Pisa. As a boy he joined his father, a customs official, at the port of Bugia on the North African coast, and there, among Arab merchants and scholars, he learned the Hindu-Arabic system of nine figures and a zero. Travels across the Mediterranean — to Egypt, Syria, Sicily and Byzantium — deepened his command of the mathematics of the Islamic world.

In 1202 he set it all down in the Liber Abaci, the Book of Calculation, which showed European merchants and scholars how to reckon with the new numerals and methods inherited from al-Khwarizmi, far surpassing the clumsy Roman system. To illustrate one rule he posed a puzzle about breeding rabbits whose answer unfolds in the sequence that now bears his name, each term the sum of the two before it — a pattern later found echoed in the spirals of nature and tied to the golden ratio.

Fibonacci wrote further works, a Practica Geometriae grounded in Euclid and a subtle Book of Squares on the theory of numbers that ranks among the finest mathematics of the Middle Ages. His reputation reached the dazzling court of the Emperor Frederick II, whose scholars set him problems to test his powers. Honoured by his native city, he died at Pisa around the middle of the century, having done more than anyone to bring the arithmetic of the East into the counting-houses and universities of the West.
