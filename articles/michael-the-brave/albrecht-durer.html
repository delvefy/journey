Albrecht Dürer

Nuremberg painter and printmaker, 1471–1528

Dürer was born in Nuremberg on 21 May 1471, the third of eighteen children of a Hungarian-born goldsmith of the same name and his wife Barbara Holper. He learned engraving in his father's workshop and then, from 1486, was apprenticed for three years to the painter and woodcut designer Michael Wolgemut. After a journeyman's tour through the upper Rhine, where he tried unsuccessfully to find Martin Schongauer alive, he returned to Nuremberg in 1494, was married by his father to Agnes Frey, and within months left her for the first of two long trips to Venice.

The Venetian journey of 1494–95 and the second of 1505–07 gave him a thorough Italian schooling: he studied Mantegna's engravings, met Giovanni Bellini, whom he called "the best in painting", and absorbed the mathematics of perspective. From 1495 onward he ran the most productive print workshop north of the Alps. The Apocalypse woodcuts of 1498, the engraved Adam and Eve of 1504, the great trio of master engravings Knight, Death, and the Devil, St Jerome in his Study, and Melencolia I of 1513–14, and the woodcut series of the Large Passion, Small Passion, and Life of the Virgin made him the first European artist whose fame and income rested chiefly on prints sold across the continent.

From 1512 he served the Emperor Maximilian I, designing the woodcuts of the colossal Triumphal Arch and the Triumphal Procession and receiving a pension that Charles V renewed after Maximilian's death. He drew Erasmus in 1520 and engraved him in 1526; he kept a diary of his journey through the Netherlands of 1520–21, and he sided publicly with Martin Luther, calling him "the man who has helped me out of great anguish". His later years were given to theoretical books — the Four Books of Human Proportion published just after his death, the manual on measurement of 1525, and a treatise on fortification of 1527. He died in Nuremberg on 6 April 1528.
