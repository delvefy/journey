Gregory of Nyssa

Cappadocian Father and bishop of Nyssa, c. 335–395 AD

Gregory was the younger brother of Basil the Great and of the holy Macrina, born into the same saintly Cappadocian household around 335. Unlike his brother, he was at first drawn to a secular career as a teacher of rhetoric and even married, before the influence of his family turned him toward the Church. About 372 Basil, needing loyal allies among the bishops, had him consecrated bishop of the obscure town of Nyssa.

His early years as bishop were stormy. Under the Arian emperor Valens he was accused of mismanagement, deposed, and driven from his see, returning only after Valens died in 378. When Basil died the following year, Gregory stepped into his brother's place as a foremost defender of Nicene orthodoxy, completing Basil's unfinished refutation of the Arian Eunomius.

He was the most speculative and original mind of the three Cappadocians. Drawing deeply on the legacy of Origen, he developed a profound mystical theology in works such as the Life of Moses and the homilies on the Song of Songs, teaching that the soul's ascent to God is an endless progress into the infinite. His dialogue On the Soul and the Resurrection records the deathbed conversation of his sister Macrina, whom he revered as his teacher.

He played a prominent part in the First Council of Constantinople in 381 and was named by the emperor Theodosius as one of the bishops whose communion defined orthodoxy in the East. He died around 395, long underrated but later recognised as one of the most penetrating theologians of the early Church.
