Amerigo Vespucci

Florentine navigator, eponym of America, 1454–1512

Vespucci was born in Florence on 9 March 1454, into a notarial family of moderate standing. He was educated by his uncle Giorgio Antonio, a Dominican humanist who taught the Medici children Latin and geography. The young Amerigo entered the service of the cadet branch of the Medici headed by Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco, and in 1492 was sent to Seville to manage the business of Giannotto Berardi, a Florentine outfitter who supplied the second and third Columbian fleets. When Berardi died in 1495, Vespucci took over his shipping concerns; he knew Columbus personally and corresponded with him until Columbus’s death.

From 1499 he turned navigator himself. On a Spanish expedition under Alonso de Ojeda he reached the coast of present-day Guyana and the mouth of the Amazon. He shifted to Portuguese service for a second, more important voyage in 1501–1502, sponsored by King Manuel I, which followed the Brazilian coast south almost to the latitude of the Plate, returning by way of Sierra Leone. The letters he wrote home from this voyage—the Mundus Novus of 1503 to Lorenzo, and the longer Letter to Soderini of 1504—made him the first European to argue plainly in print that the new lands were not the eastern fringe of Asia but a continent unknown to the ancients.

The pamphlets, more vivid than accurate, were translated everywhere. In 1507 the German cosmographer Martin Waldseemüller, working at Saint-Dié in Lorraine, published a great wall map of the world on which the southern landmass bore, for the first time, the name “America”—a feminine form of Amerigo. He retracted the name in a later edition but the printers ignored him, and the label spread north over both continents. Vespucci, who had returned permanently to Seville, was appointed Piloto Mayor of Spain in 1508, the chief examiner of pilots and keeper of the master padrón of charts. He held the office until his death from malaria on 22 February 1512.
