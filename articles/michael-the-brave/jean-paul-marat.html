Jean-Paul Marat

French Revolutionary journalist, 1743–1793.

Born in the Prussian-held principality of Neuchâtel to a Sardinian-born father who had converted to Calvinism, Jean-Paul Mara — the final t was a later affectation — was educated by his parents at home, left at sixteen, and wandered for thirty years across Europe in pursuit of a medical career. He studied in Toulouse, Paris, and London, took a medical degree at St. Andrews in 1775, practiced as a fashionable physician to the household of the Comte d'Artois, and on the side ran experiments on heat, light, and electricity that he hoped would unseat Newton. Rejected by the Academy of Sciences, ridiculed by Lavoisier and d'Alembert, he conceived a lifelong hatred of the scientific establishment.

The Revolution gave the disappointed savant the platform he had failed to win in the salons. In September 1789 he founded L'Ami du peuple, the journal he would edit, almost single-handed, for the next four years. From a Cordeliers cellar he denounced "les ennemis du peuple" by name — counter-revolutionary generals, hoarding merchants, moderate deputies, ministers of the crown, finally the king and queen — and called repeatedly for the heads of hundreds, then thousands, then five or six hundred thousand. His skin disease, by now widely identified with dermatitis herpetiformis, kept him in a medicated bath for many hours of each day, and it was there that his readers and visitors received him. Elected to the Convention in 1792, he sat with Robespierre and Danton on the Mountain, was acquitted on a Girondin-pressed prosecution in April 1793, and contributed his bile to the proscription of those same Girondins in June.

On 13 July 1793 a twenty-four-year-old Norman gentlewoman, Charlotte Corday, talked her way into his rooms on the rue des Cordeliers with a list of conspirators she said she had to denounce, drew a kitchen knife from her bodice, and stabbed him to death in his medicated bath. She was guillotined four days later. The Jacobins, led by his friend Jacques-Louis David, gave him a republican apotheosis: The Death of Marat, painted within months, is the most famous image of the Revolution. He was reinterred briefly in the Panthéon, then expelled when the political wind turned. The wound, the bath, the falling arm, and the bloodstained sheet had passed permanently into European memory.

The road continues to Charlotte Corday.
