Vlad the Impaler

Voivode of Wallachia, c.1428–1477

Vlad III, called Drăculea after his father Vlad Dracul, a knight of the Order of the Dragon, was born about 1428, probably in Transylvania. As a boy he and his brother Radu were handed to the Ottoman court as hostages, a captivity that taught him both the Turkish tongue and a lasting wariness of the sultans. He took the throne of Wallachia, a small principality caught between Hungary and the expanding Ottoman Empire, and held it — with interruptions — through the turbulent middle of the fifteenth century.

His reign became a byword for terror. He punished boyar treachery, crime and disloyalty with mass executions, above all by impalement, the practice that earned him the posthumous name Țepeș, “the Impaler.” Contemporary German, Ottoman and Slavic accounts — many of them hostile — recount these horrors in lurid detail, and though their numbers are surely exaggerated, the cruelty was real enough to brand him in memory.

Vlad turned that ferocity against the Ottomans. Refusing tribute, he raided across the Danube and in 1462 fell by night on the camp of Sultan Mehmed II; tradition holds that the advancing Turks recoiled before a field of thousands of impaled prisoners outside Târgoviște. Outmatched, he lost his throne to his Ottoman-backed brother and fled to Hungary, where King Matthias Corvinus held him prisoner for years before restoring him. He regained Wallachia briefly in 1476 and was killed in battle soon after. Lurid pamphlets kept his legend alive for centuries, until the novelist Bram Stoker borrowed his name — Dracula — and gave it to the world’s most famous vampire.
