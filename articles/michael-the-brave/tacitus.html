Tacitus

Roman historian, c. 56–c. 120 AD

Cornelius Tacitus rose through the Roman cursus honorum under the Flavian emperors, holding the praetorship under Domitian and the consulship in 97, and later governing the province of Asia. He won early fame as an orator, married the daughter of the general Agricola, and counted Pliny the Younger among his closest friends. But his lasting greatness lay in history, which he wrote with a terse, biting brilliance that has never been surpassed in Latin.

His shorter works came first: a loving biography of his father-in-law Agricola, conqueror of much of Britain, and the Germania, a searching account of the tribes beyond the Rhine. Then came the two great histories. The Histories told of the convulsive year of four emperors and the rule of Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian; the Annals, his masterpiece, traced the empire from the death of Augustus through the reigns of Tiberius, Claudius, and Nero.

Across these pages Tacitus became the unforgiving anatomist of imperial power. He dissected the tyranny and fear of Tiberius, the murders of Nero, the corruption of court and Senate, with an eye for the telling detail and a gift for the crushing epigram—"they make a desert and call it peace." Pessimistic, moralising, and politically acute, his work shaped the Western understanding of tyranny and freedom for centuries, and he is reckoned the foremost historian Rome produced.

The road continues to Agricola.
