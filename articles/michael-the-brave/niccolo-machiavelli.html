Niccolò Machiavelli

Florentine secretary and political philosopher, 1469–1527

Machiavelli was born in Florence on 3 May 1469, the son of Bernardo, a poor lawyer of an old but impoverished family, and Bartolomea de' Nelli. He grew up in the last years of Lorenzo the Magnificent and came of age through the upheavals of 1494: the descent of Charles VIII, the expulsion of the Medici, the four-year theocracy of Girolamo Savonarola, and the friar's burning in the Piazza della Signoria in 1498. Within weeks of that execution the new republican government appointed Machiavelli, then twenty-nine, second chancellor of Florence and secretary to the Ten of War.

For fourteen years he ran the chancery's diplomatic correspondence and travelled on missions of every importance — four times to the court of Louis XII, twice to the Emperor Maximilian, repeatedly to Cesare Borgia in the Romagna in 1502 and 1503, where he watched Borgia trap his condottieri at Senigallia and concluded that ruthless decision was the engine of political success. He fought to create a citizen militia in place of mercenaries, and his levies did reduce Pisa in 1509. Then in 1512 a Spanish army restored the Medici, the republic fell, and he was dismissed, briefly imprisoned and tortured on suspicion of conspiracy, and exiled to his small farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina.

Out of office, he wrote. By the end of 1513 he had drafted Il Principe and dedicated it to Lorenzo di Piero de' Medici in the hope of a return to service that never came. He went on to compose the longer Discourses on the First Decade of Livy, the dialogue Art of War, the comedies Mandragola and Clizia, and, commissioned by Giulio de' Medici (the future Clement VII), the Florentine Histories of 1525. He died in Florence on 21 June 1527, weeks after the Medici were expelled again, his books unpublished but already circulating in manuscript, his name a synonym for the cold separation of politics from Christian ethics.

The road continues to Cesare Borgia.
