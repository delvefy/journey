John Quincy Adams

6th President of the United States, 1767–1848

The son of President John Adams, he was carried into history young, crossing the Atlantic with his father on wartime diplomatic missions and serving as secretary to an American envoy in St Petersburg while still a teenager. He grew into perhaps the ablest diplomat the nation ever produced, representing it at the courts of the Netherlands, Prussia, Russia, and Britain, and helping to negotiate the Treaty of Ghent that ended the War of 1812.

As Secretary of State under Monroe he secured Florida from Spain and drafted the Monroe Doctrine, warning Europe away from the Americas. In 1824 he reached the presidency through a contested vote in the House—branded a “corrupt bargain” by Jackson’s furious supporters—but his visionary plans for national development were thwarted, and he was swept from office after a single term.

His greatest chapter came afterward. Rather than retire, he won a seat in the House of Representatives and served there for seventeen years as “Old Man Eloquent,” a relentless foe of slavery who battled the “gag rule” and, in 1841, argued before the Supreme Court for the freedom of the Africans of the Amistad. He suffered a stroke at his desk in the Capitol and died two days later in 1848. Among the towering orators who shared that chamber with him was Daniel Webster.
