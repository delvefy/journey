Bessarion

Greek scholar, Cardinal-Bishop of Tusculum, 1403–1472

Basilios was born at Trebizond on 2 January 1403, son of a craftsman in the small Komnenian empire of the Black Sea. He took the monastic name Bessarion in 1423 in a Constantinople monastery and was sent for his higher studies to Mistra, where Gemistos Plethon taught him Plato in the original. He returned to Constantinople, was ordained, made metropolitan of Nicaea in 1437, and was attached, with his master Plethon, to the embassy that John VIII Palaiologos led to Italy for the Council of Ferrara-Florence. At Florence in 1439 it was Bessarion who, with the Latin theologian Giovanni da Montenero, hammered out the formulae of union; his great oration in support of Laetentur coeli, delivered on the day of the proclamation, was the diplomatic triumph of his life.

Eugenius IV created him cardinal-priest in December 1439, and when the Greek Church repudiated the union Bessarion stayed in Italy. He gave the rest of his life to the rescue of Greek learning. His palace on the Quirinal became a hostel for refugees from Constantinople and Trebizond; Theodore Gaza, George of Trebizond, Andronikos Kallistos, Demetrios Chalkokondyles, John Argyropoulos all passed through it; under his patronage Latin translations of Aristotle and the Greek Fathers were turned out steadily, and his own In calumniatorem Platonis (1469) was the first scholastic defence of Plato in the West. In 1463 he was created the titular Latin Patriarch of Constantinople — a post by then symbolic — and was twice all but elected pope, in 1455 and 1471.

He was a tireless campaigner for a new crusade. Pius II’s aborted Mantua congress of 1459, his own legation to Germany in 1460–61, his last legation to Louis XI of France in 1472 all aimed at marshalling Latin arms against the conqueror of his city. He died at Ravenna on 18 November 1472 on the way back from that last mission and was buried in Santi Apostoli in Rome. In 1468 he bequeathed his library of nearly a thousand Greek and Latin manuscripts to the Republic of Venice, “so that they may be preserved for the common use,” founding what became the Biblioteca Marciana — the single richest collection of Greek texts in the world and the seedbed of the Italian Renaissance’s rediscovery of the language.

The road continues to Gemistos Plethon.
