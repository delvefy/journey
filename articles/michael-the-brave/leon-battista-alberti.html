Leon Battista Alberti

Humanist, architect, and theorist, 1404–1472

Battista degli Alberti was born at Genoa on 14 February 1404, the second illegitimate son of Lorenzo Alberti, a Florentine banker in exile from the political proscription of his house. He grew up between Venice and the curial cities, was given the cognomen Leon by his Latin schoolmaster, took his canon-law doctorate at Bologna in 1428, and entered the papal curia under Eugenius IV. The Alberti family was rehabilitated in 1428; he saw Florence properly for the first time in 1434 in the train of the displaced pope, fell into the company of Brunelleschi, Donatello, and Masaccio, and within two years had written De pictura, the small Latin treatise on painting whose first vernacular dedication to Brunelleschi codified the linear perspective the older man had demonstrated.

His Della famiglia (1434–41) and Momus, his Latin satire on the gods, his Intercoenales, his vernacular dialogues, made him one of the founders of Italian humanist prose. He invented a primitive bathysphere and a cipher disc — described in his De componendis cifris of about 1466 — that anticipated the polyalphabetic cryptography of the next century. He was a friend of the Mantuan marquesses and of the Estense at Ferrara; he advised on the rebuilding of the family palaces, the laying-out of pageants, the redaction of statutes. About 1450 he completed his greatest book, De re aedificatoria, the first new Latin treatise on architecture since Vitruvius, in ten books on the model of his predecessor; it circulated in manuscript and was printed at Florence in 1485.

The buildings, in the second half of his life, gave architectural form to his theory. The Tempio Malatestiano at Rimini (begun 1450) was a Roman triumphal arch wrapped around a Gothic church; the Rucellai palace and the green-and-white marble facade of Santa Maria Novella at Florence (1456–70) for the merchant Giovanni Rucellai applied a classical order to the Tuscan urban front; Sant’Andrea in Mantua (begun 1472 for Lodovico Gonzaga) put a coffered barrel-vault over a single nave for the first time since antiquity, the prototype of every later Italian church of the type. He died at Rome on 25 April 1472, leaving Sant’Andrea unfinished. Vasari, looking back, made him the first universal man of the new age.
