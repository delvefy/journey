Christiaan Huygens

Dutch mathematician, physicist, and astronomer, 1629–1695

Huygens came from a distinguished Dutch family, the son of the poet and diplomat Constantijn Huygens, and was raised among the leading minds of the age. He combined exceptional skill as a mathematician with a craftsman’s talent for grinding lenses and building instruments, and he became the foremost scientist of the generation between Galileo and Newton.

Turning his improved telescopes on the heavens, he discovered Titan, the largest moon of Saturn, and correctly explained the planet’s puzzling appearance as a thin ring surrounding it. Seeking a reliable measure of time for astronomy and navigation, he invented the pendulum clock, applying the principle of the regular swing to transform timekeeping, and he worked out the mathematics of the pendulum and of circular motion, including the force that draws a body toward the centre of its path.

His greatest theoretical contribution was a wave theory of light, set out in his Treatise on Light, which explained reflection and refraction by imagining each point of a wavefront as the source of new wavelets. This view stood opposed to the rival corpuscular theory of his younger contemporary Newton, with whom he debated optics and gravitation. A founding member of the French Academy of Sciences, he also helped guide the young Leibniz in mathematics. Late in life he even speculated, in a posthumously published book, about the possibility of life on other worlds, capping a career that bridged the era of the telescope’s pioneers and the age of mathematical physics.
