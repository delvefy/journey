Ivan Turgenev

Russian novelist, 1818–1883

Born to a wealthy landowning family on the Oryol steppe, ruled over by a cruel and despotic serf-owning mother, Turgenev grew up to detest serfdom and to look westward for Russia’s future. Educated in Moscow, St Petersburg, and Berlin, he won fame with A Sportsman’s Sketches, a set of quietly devastating portraits of peasant life that helped turn educated opinion against serfdom; for an admiring obituary of Gogol he was placed under arrest and confined to his estate.

His novels charted the shifting moods of the Russian intelligentsia, culminating in Fathers and Sons, whose hero Bazarov gave the world the word “nihilist” and earned its author abuse from radicals and conservatives alike. The most European of the great Russians, he spent much of his life abroad, trailing the singer Pauline Viardot across the continent and forming warm friendships with Flaubert, Henry James, and George Sand, to whom he was a welcome guest at Nohant.

He did more than anyone to introduce Russian literature to Western readers. His relations with his towering compatriots were stormier: he quarrelled so bitterly with one that the two nearly fought a duel and were estranged for years, though near the end he wrote a moving deathbed plea begging that giant to return to his art. He died near Paris in 1883. That estranged friend and rival was Leo Tolstoy.
