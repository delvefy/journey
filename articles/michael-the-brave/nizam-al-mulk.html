Nizam al-Mulk

Seljuk vizier and statesman, 1018–1092

Born in 1018 near Tus in Khorasan, Abu Ali Hasan rose through the service of the Seljuk Turks to become the most powerful minister of his age, earning the honorific Nizam al-Mulk, “Order of the Realm.” As vizier first to Sultan Alp Arslan and then, for two decades, to his son Malik-Shah, he was the true governor of an empire that stretched from Anatolia to Central Asia, holding its turbulent Turkish soldiery and Persian bureaucracy together by sheer administrative skill.

He reorganized the army and the system of land grants that paid it, and he made learning an instrument of state. The network of colleges he founded — the Nizamiyya madrasas of Baghdad, Nishapur and other cities — trained jurists and theologians in Sunni orthodoxy, and it was to the chair at Baghdad that he summoned the young al-Ghazali. He also gathered scientists and poets, supporting the astronomical work and calendar reform of his protégé Omar Khayyam.

His experience of power he distilled into the Siyasatnama, or Book of Government, a celebrated manual of statecraft studded with anecdote and counsel that became a classic of Persian political literature. Implacably hostile to the Ismaili sectarians, he made bitter enemies of the followers of Hassan-i Sabbah, and in 1092, on the road near Nahavand, he was struck down by an assassin’s knife — the first illustrious victim of that shadowy order. His master Malik-Shah died within weeks, and the great Seljuk empire he had steadied began to come apart.
