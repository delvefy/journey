Friedrich Nietzsche

German philosopher, 1844–1900

A pastor’s son from Saxony, Nietzsche was so precocious a classical scholar that the University of Basel made him a professor of philology at twenty-four. His first book, The Birth of Tragedy, contrasted the serene Apollonian and the ecstatic Dionysian impulses in Greek art and bore the deep imprint of two enthusiasms: the pessimism of Schopenhauer and his intoxicating friendship with the composer Richard Wagner, whom he revered as a living god of culture before recoiling from him in disgust.

Driven from teaching by wretched health, he became a wandering invalid, scribbling in mountain boarding-houses the aphoristic books that would shake modern thought. He proclaimed that “God is dead,” that conventional morality was a slave revolt of the weak against the strong, and that humanity must overcome itself to produce the “Übermensch.” In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Beyond Good and Evil, and On the Genealogy of Morals he philosophised with a hammer, exposing the hidden will to power beneath ideals.

In January 1889, in Turin, he collapsed into a madness from which he never recovered, spending his last eleven years mute and helpless in the care of his mother and sister. That sister later edited and falsified his writings to serve a German nationalism he had despised, fastening on him an undeserved association with the antisemites he loathed. He died in 1900, his influence on existentialism, psychology, and literature only beginning. The friend and idol of his youth had been Richard Wagner.
