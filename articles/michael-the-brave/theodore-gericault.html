Théodore Géricault

French Romantic painter, 1791–1824

Théodore Géricault was born in 1791 at Rouen into a prosperous Norman family and trained in Paris under Carle Vernet and then Pierre-Narcisse Guérin. He stood at the hinge between Jacques-Louis David's neoclassical establishment and the Romantic generation that would supplant it, and he had little patience with the academic regime. He copied tirelessly in the Louvre, immersing himself above all in Peter Paul Rubens and the Italian baroque.

His debut at the Salon of 1812, the Charging Chasseur, was an enormous portrait of a Napoleonic cavalry officer that shocked viewers with its movement and storm. He spent 1816 and 1817 in Italy and returned with the muscular rhetoric of Michelangelo and the chiaroscuro of Caravaggio in his eye.

In 1819 he exhibited The Raft of the Medusa, a vast canvas built around the survivors of the wreck of the French frigate Méduse, abandoned by their captain off the West African coast. Géricault interviewed survivors, studied corpses in the morgue and severed limbs in his studio, and composed the dying and the desperate into a pyramid that culminates in a Black sailor waving a rag at a barely visible ship on the horizon. The picture was at once a journalistic indictment of a Bourbon scandal and a Michelangelesque drama of bodies.

A trip to England produced a series of pictures and lithographs of horses and of the London poor. He had taken up institutional portraiture of a strange new subject—the inmates of an asylum—when, weakened by riding accidents, he died at thirty-two in 1824. His younger friend Eugène Delacroix mourned him as the founder of their school. But the older master against whom Géricault's whole generation defined itself, and whose neoclassical art still ruled the French Salon when Géricault began, was Jacques-Louis David.
