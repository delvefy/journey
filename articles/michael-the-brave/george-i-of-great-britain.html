George I of Great Britain

Elector of Hanover and King of Great Britain, 1660–1727 (reigned 1714–1727)

George Louis was already the ruler of Hanover when, under the Act of Settlement, which barred Catholics from the throne, he was chosen to succeed Queen Anne. His claim rested on his descent from James VI and I through his mother, Sophia of Hanover; with the nearer Catholic heirs excluded, this distant Protestant prince became king of Great Britain in 1714. At his court in Hanover he had employed the philosopher Leibniz as historian and librarian, a scholar he left behind when he crossed to his new kingdom.

A German speaker who never fully mastered English and who preferred Hanover to London, George leaned heavily on his ministers and absented himself from much of the detail of government. In this vacuum power flowed to his Whig politicians, and above all to Robert Walpole, whose management of the Crown’s business made him in effect the first British prime minister. The reign thus quietly advanced the shift of real authority from the monarch toward Parliament and cabinet.

His accession was challenged in 1715 by a Jacobite rising on behalf of the exiled Stuart claimant, but the revolt collapsed. His private life was unhappy and scandalous: he had divorced and imprisoned his wife, Sophia Dorothea, for an alleged affair, keeping her confined for the rest of her life. A patron of Handel, for whom the Water Music was played on the Thames, George secured the Hanoverian dynasty on the British throne, where it would reign for nearly two centuries. He died on a journey back to his beloved Hanover.
