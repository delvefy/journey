Marcus Junius Brutus

Roman senator and assassin of Caesar, 85–42 BC

Brutus belonged to a family that prized its legend of tyrant-slaying, claiming descent from the Lucius Junius Brutus who had expelled Rome's last king and founded the Republic. His mother Servilia was the lover of Julius Caesar, and his political conscience was shaped by his uncle and father-in-law, Cato the Younger, the great champion of the old order. A serious, Stoic-minded man, Brutus joined Pompey against Caesar in the civil war, though Pompey had once put his father to death; after Pharsalus, Caesar spared him and advanced him to high office.

That clemency did not bind him. Persuaded by Cassius and by his own sense of inherited duty, Brutus came to believe that only Caesar's death could save the Republic from monarchy. On the Ides of March, 44 BC, he was among the senators who struck Caesar down in the Senate house—the blow from Brutus, his protege, becoming the most famous wound of all.

The liberators won the deed but lost the aftermath. Mark Antony's funeral oration turned the people against them, and Brutus and Cassius withdrew to the east to gather armies. In 42 BC they faced the avengers Antony and Octavian on the plains of Philippi. After defeat in the decisive engagement, Brutus, like Cassius before him, chose to die by his own hand rather than be taken. Shakespeare would later call him the noblest Roman of them all, and his name became forever bound up with the dilemma of tyranny and liberty.
