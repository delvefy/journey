Andrew Carnegie

Scottish-American steel magnate and philanthropist, 1835–1919

Andrew Carnegie was born in 1835 in a single-room weaver's cottage at Dunfermline in Fife, Scotland, the son of a hand-loom weaver whose trade was being destroyed by the new steam-powered mills. The family emigrated in 1848 to Allegheny, Pennsylvania, where the thirteen-year-old Andrew became a bobbin boy in a cotton mill at $1.20 a week. A succession of jobs as telegraph messenger and operator brought him to the notice of Tom Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad, who took him on as his private secretary and through whom Carnegie made his first investments and learned modern industrial management.

From 1865 he turned to iron and then to steel. His Edgar Thomson works at Braddock, opened in 1875, was the first American mill built to the new Bessemer process; his Homestead works near Pittsburgh became, by the 1890s, the largest steel plant in the world. The 1892 Homestead strike, broken by Pinkerton agents and the state militia in the absence of Carnegie himself, was the bloodiest American industrial dispute of the century and a permanent stain on his reputation as a friend of working men. His partner Henry Clay Frick took the public blame.

In 1901 J. Pierpont Morgan bought Carnegie Steel for the equivalent of some 480 million dollars, folding it into the new United States Steel Corporation, and made Carnegie, at sixty-five, the richest man in the world. From that moment Carnegie devoted his life to systematically giving the fortune away. His Gospel of Wealth of 1889 had argued that the rich man held his fortune as a trustee for the public good; he now applied the doctrine, endowing more than 2,500 free public libraries in the English-speaking world, building Carnegie Hall in New York, funding the Carnegie Institute, the Carnegie Mellon University, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and Hero Funds in eleven countries.

He spent his later years lobbying for international arbitration to abolish war, built the Peace Palace at The Hague, and was bitterly disappointed by the First World War. He died at his Massachusetts summer estate in 1919. The Wall Street financier who had purchased Carnegie Steel from him and at that moment controlled the largest concentration of industrial capital the world had yet seen was J.P. Morgan.
