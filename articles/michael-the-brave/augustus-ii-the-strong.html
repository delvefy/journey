Augustus II the Strong

Elector of Saxony and King of Poland, 1670–1733

Augustus II, called the Strong, was Elector of Saxony and King of Poland, a ruler remembered as much for his appetites and his patronage as for his troubled reign. Born in 1670, he succeeded as Elector in 1694, and three years later converted from Lutheranism to Catholicism—scandalising his Protestant Saxon subjects—in order to win election to the throne of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.

His foreign ambitions drew him into the Great Northern War, in alliance with Peter the Great and Denmark against Sweden. It went badly. Charles XII invaded his lands, beat his armies, and in 1706 compelled him to surrender the Polish crown to a rival, Stanisław Leszczyński. Only after Charles's ruin at Poltava in 1709 did Augustus recover his throne, but his hopes of strengthening royal power against the unruly Polish nobility came to nothing.

His nickname celebrated a famous physical strength—he was said to snap horseshoes in his hands—and a legendary appetite for women that left him a swarm of illegitimate children, the greatest of whom, Maurice de Saxe, would become a marshal of France.

Where he triumphed was in splendour. Augustus turned Dresden into one of the jewels of baroque Europe, raising the Zwinger, amassing dazzling collections of art and treasure, and founding the Meissen works, the first manufactory in Europe to master true porcelain. He died in 1733, leaving his Saxon and Polish crowns to his son Augustus III.

The road continues to Maurice de Saxe.
