Pierre-Joseph Proudhon

First self-described anarchist, 1809–1865

Pierre-Joseph Proudhon was the founder of the anarchist tradition, the first man to claim the name. Born at Besançon in 1809 into a poor family, largely self-taught, he earned his bread as a printer and typesetter before turning his sharp, combative pen to the great social questions of the age.

In 1840 he published What Is Property?, answering his own question with the famous thunderclap, “Property is theft!” By anarchy he meant not chaos but a society without rulers, ordered through free contract and mutual exchange—the doctrine he called mutualism. The book made him a leading voice of French socialism.

His System of Economic Contradictions drew a withering reply from Karl Marx, whose Poverty of Philosophy mocked its title and ended their friendship, opening the long rift between anarchist and Marxist socialism. In the Revolution of 1848 Proudhon won a seat in the National Assembly and tried to found a people's bank of interest-free credit; his assaults on Louis-Napoleon soon landed him in prison for three years.

He opposed capitalism and authoritarian communism alike, trusting instead in federation, workers' associations, and the independent producer—ideas that flowed on to Bakunin, the First International, and the later syndicalists. He wrote, too, on art, hailing a new realism that painted the common people without flattery. He died near Paris in 1865. The friend whose pictures he defended, and who painted his own portrait, was Gustave Courbet.
