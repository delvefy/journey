Luís de Camões

Portuguese poet, author of Os Lusíadas, c. 1524–1580

Luís de Camões was born around 1524 into the lesser nobility, probably with some schooling at Coimbra, and spent a stormy youth at the edges of the Lisbon court. He lost the use of his right eye soldiering in North Africa, and after a street brawl in the capital he was imprisoned, winning release only by agreeing to serve the crown overseas. Around 1553 he sailed for India, beginning some seventeen years of wandering through the Portuguese East — Goa, Malacca, the Moluccas, Macau, Mozambique — in poverty, exile and recurring trouble. Tradition holds that in one shipwreck he swam ashore with one arm, holding the manuscript of his great poem above the water with the other.

That poem was Os Lusíadas, published in 1572 after his return to Lisbon. It is the epic of his nation, built around Vasco da Gama’s voyage to India and crowded with the kings and captains of Portuguese history, from Manuel I to the heroes of the discoveries, the whole woven through with the gods of classical mythology. Within it stands the haunting episode of Inês de Castro, the murdered lady mourned in some of the most famous verses in the language. Camões dedicated the work to the young King Sebastian, who rewarded him with only a meagre pension.

He was also a supreme lyric poet, master of the sonnet and of a music of longing that later ages called saudade. He died in 1580, poor and largely unrewarded, in the very year his country lost its independence. Posterity made him the national poet, and the Portuguese still call their tongue the language of Camões.

The road continues to Inês de Castro.
