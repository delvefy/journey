Frédéric Chopin

Polish composer and pianist, 1810–1849

Born near Warsaw to a French father and Polish mother, Chopin was a prodigy who had published works and dazzled audiences before he was twenty. He left Poland in 1830, and when the November Uprising against Russian rule was crushed he chose never to return, settling in Paris as the supreme poet of the piano. Almost his entire output was written for that single instrument, which he made sing with an intimacy and harmonic subtlety no one had achieved before.

His nocturnes, preludes, and études refined the keyboard miniature into high art, while his mazurkas and polonaises distilled the spirit of his lost homeland into music that became an emblem of Polish nationhood. Avoiding the grand concert hall he preferred the intimacy of the salon, where his playing—delicate, rubato-laced, deeply expressive—enchanted a circle that included Liszt, Berlioz, and Delacroix, and won the early acclaim of Schumann.

For nearly a decade his life was bound up with the formidable novelist George Sand, whose care sustained him through worsening illness, including a disastrous winter on Mallorca. When their relationship broke down his health collapsed with it. Consumptive and frail, he gave his last concerts and died in Paris in 1849, only thirty-nine; by his wish his heart was carried back to Warsaw. His companion through those productive years had been George Sand.
