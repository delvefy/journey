Sebastian of Portugal

King of Portugal, lost at Alcácer Quibir, 1554–1578

Sebastian was born in 1554, a posthumous son whose father had died before his birth and whose mother, Joanna of Austria, was a daughter of the Emperor Charles V and sister of Philip II of Spain. The death of his grandfather John III in 1557 made him king while he was still a small child, and Portugal was governed for years by regents — his grandmother Catherine of Austria and his great-uncle the Cardinal Henry. Educated by Jesuits and steeped in tales of chivalry, the boy grew into a fervent, restless young man consumed by the dream of a crusade against the Muslim powers of North Africa, and he neglected the marriage and heir his kingdom needed.

In 1578 he gathered a large and showy but poorly organized army and crossed to Morocco to intervene in a quarrel over its throne. On 4 August, at Alcácer Quibir — the Battle of the Three Kings — the Portuguese host was surrounded and destroyed. Sebastian himself was killed in the rout, and his body was never certainly identified, a fact that would feed legends for generations.

The disaster was a national catastrophe. The king had left no child, and after the brief reign of the aged Cardinal Henry the crown was claimed in 1580 by his uncle Philip II of Spain, who joined Portugal to his vast monarchy in the Iberian Union. Among the mourners of the old glory was the poet Luís de Camões, who had dedicated his great epic of the discoveries to the lost king. From Sebastian’s vanishing grew “Sebastianism,” a stubborn folk hope that the king would one day return to redeem his people.
