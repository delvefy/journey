Alexandre Dumas

French novelist and dramatist, 1802–1870

Grandson of an enslaved woman of Saint-Domingue and son of one of Napoleon’s most celebrated generals, Dumas rose from humble clerkship to become the most popular storyteller of his age. He first made his name in the theatre during the Romantic ferment of the 1820s and 1830s, alongside his friend Victor Hugo, before turning to the serialised novel, which he industrialised on a prodigious scale with the help of collaborators such as Auguste Maquet.

From this workshop poured some of the most thrilling adventure stories ever told. The Three Musketeers, with its dashing d’Artagnan and the rallying cry “all for one and one for all,” set its swordsmen amid the intrigues of Louis XIII’s France and the schemes of Cardinal Richelieu; The Count of Monte Cristo spun an unforgettable tale of wrongful imprisonment and patient, magnificent revenge. Drawing freely on French history, his romances have never gone out of print or out of fashion.

Dumas earned and squandered fortunes, built a fantastical château, travelled restlessly, and lived as flamboyantly as any of his heroes. His son, Alexandre Dumas fils, became a noted writer in his own right. Long enjoyed as mere entertainment, the elder Dumas was at last honoured as a master of his nation’s literature; in 2002 his remains were transferred to the Panthéon in Paris.
