Montesquieu

French political philosopher, 1689–1755

Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu, was the political theorist who gave the modern world the doctrine of the separation of powers. Born in 1689 near Bordeaux into the robe nobility, he served as president of the regional parlement before abandoning the magistracy for a life of study and travel.

He first won fame with the Persian Letters of 1721, a witty epistolary satire in which two Persian visitors survey the manners, politics, and religion of Europe with an outsider's astonished eye. Election to the Académie française followed, and a long journey across the Continent—above all a stay in England, whose constitution he studied closely—shaped the great work of his maturity.

That work was The Spirit of the Laws, published in 1748 after twenty years' labour. In it Montesquieu compared the governments of the world, distinguishing republics, monarchies, and despotisms, each sustained by its own animating principle, and argued that laws must answer to a people's climate, history, and circumstances. His most enduring claim was that liberty is secured only when legislative, executive, and judicial powers are lodged in separate hands and made to check one another.

The idea passed directly into the constitution-making of the age. Madison and the framers of the United States built their government upon it, and reforming monarchs took notice as well: Catherine the Great drew openly on The Spirit of the Laws in drafting her program for the laws of Russia. His earlier study of Rome's rise and fall, meanwhile, pointed the way for Gibbon.

The road continues to Catherine the Great.
