Julia Domna

Roman empress, c. 160–217 AD

Julia Domna was born at Emesa in Syria, the daughter of a hereditary high priest of the local sun-god. She married the rising general Septimius Severus—by one story because her horoscope foretold she would wed a king—and when he seized the empire in 193 she became one of the most powerful women Rome had known. She travelled with him on campaign, earning the title Mother of the Camp, and bore him the sons Caracalla and Geta.

More than a consort, she was a woman of formidable intellect who gathered around her a brilliant circle of philosophers, orators, and writers; it was she who set the sophist Philostratus to compose his celebrated life of the sage Apollonius of Tyana. Under her son Caracalla she took an active hand in government, managing his correspondence and petitions while he warred in the field, so that her influence reached into the daily running of the state.

Her life was also marked by grief and intrigue. She lived through the murder of her younger son Geta at Caracalla's command, and when Caracalla too was assassinated and the throne passed to Macrinus, she found herself stripped of power and, by report, already stricken with disease. Refusing to outlive her position, she starved herself to death in 217. Yet her family did not fall: her sister Julia Maesa rallied the dynasty and raised their kinsman Elagabalus to the purple, so that the line of the Syrian empresses endured.
