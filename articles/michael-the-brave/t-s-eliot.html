T. S. Eliot

1888–1965, American-British poet, dramatist, and critic

Thomas Stearns Eliot was born in St. Louis, Missouri, the seventh and youngest child of a brick manufacturer descended on his father's side from the Massachusetts Eliots and on his mother's from a New England line of clergy. He took two degrees at Harvard and a third at the Sorbonne in 1910–11, where he sat under Henri Bergson, and continued at Oxford on a doctoral dissertation on the idealist philosopher F. H. Bradley that he never quite defended. In 1914 he arrived in London with the manuscript of "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" in his pocket and showed it to Ezra Pound, who pronounced him a poet of genius and badgered Harriet Monroe to print it in Poetry the following June.

The young marriage to Vivienne Haigh-Wood in 1915 was a disaster of mutual ill health and incompatibility. Eliot took a clerk's stool at Lloyds Bank in the City, taught evening classes, edited The Egoist, and in 1922 published, drastically cut by Pound, the poem that announced modernism in English: The Waste Land. The same year he founded the quarterly The Criterion; in 1925 he moved from the bank to Faber and Gwyer, soon Faber and Faber, where he served as editor and director for the rest of his life and shaped the poetry list of an English generation.

He became an Anglican and a British subject in 1927, kept up a steady output of essays on tradition and individual talent that reorganised the canon for two generations of readers, and turned in his late years to verse drama (Murder in the Cathedral, The Family Reunion, The Cocktail Party) and to the religious-philosophical sequence Four Quartets completed in 1942. The Nobel Prize in Literature and the Order of Merit both came in 1948. After Vivienne's death and a long widowerhood he married his secretary Valerie Fletcher in 1957 and lived another eight years of quiet companionship. He died in London in January 1965.
