Didius Julianus

Roman emperor, r. 193

Marcus Didius Julianus, born in 133, was a wealthy senator and jurist who had been raised in the household of Marcus Aurelius’s mother and had served as consul and provincial governor. His name is fixed in history by the disgraceful manner of his rise. When the Praetorian Guard murdered the emperor Pertinax in March 193, the soldiers offered the throne to whoever would pay the most for it, and from the walls of their camp the empire was auctioned like plunder. Julianus outbid Pertinax’s own father-in-law, promising each guardsman a vast sum, and so bought the rule of the Roman world.

The purchase brought him nothing but contempt. The people of Rome jeered him, the Senate loathed him, and he could neither win affection nor command obedience. Across the provinces the great armies refused to accept a bargain struck in the barracks, and three governors rose against him almost at once — Septimius Severus on the Danube, Pescennius Niger in Syria, and Clodius Albinus in Britain.

Severus, the nearest, marched swiftly on Italy. Deserted by the Guard he had bribed and condemned by the Senate, Julianus was killed in the palace on the first of June, after a reign of just sixty-six days, plaintively asking what crime he had committed. The auction of the empire became a lasting emblem of how low the office of the Caesars had fallen.
