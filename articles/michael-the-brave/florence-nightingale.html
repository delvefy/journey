Florence Nightingale

1820–1910, English nurse and statistician, founder of modern nursing

Florence Nightingale was born in the Tuscan city after which she was named, the younger daughter of William Edward Nightingale, a wealthy English landowner, and his wife Frances. She was educated rigorously by her father in Latin, Greek, mathematics, and philosophy, and from the age of seventeen believed she had received a divine call to a useful life. The family resisted; for a decade she fought a slow battle to study nursing, finally training in 1851 at the Kaiserswerth deaconess hospital in Prussia and at the Sisters of Charity in Paris.

In October 1854 her friend Sidney Herbert, Secretary at War, asked her to lead a party of thirty-eight nurses to the British military hospital at Scutari on the Bosphorus, where the wounded of the Crimean War were dying of cholera and dysentery in greater numbers than of Russian fire. She found filth, no laundry, eight inches of cesspool seepage under the wards, and four miles of patients on the floor. Within six months she had cut the death rate from forty-two percent to two. The Times correspondent William Howard Russell described her nightly rounds with the lamp, and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem “Santa Filomena” fixed the image.

She returned to London in 1856 a celebrity. Privately exhausted and chronically ill from a disease likely contracted at Scutari, she ran the rest of her campaigns from a bed in South Street, Mayfair. With Sidney Herbert she pressed the Royal Commission on the Health of the Army; with William Farr she developed the polar-area diagrams that taught Victorian statisticians to visualize mortality. Her Notes on Nursing (1859) and the Nightingale Training School at St Thomas's Hospital, opened the next year, established nursing as a respected secular profession.

She corresponded with Queen Victoria, Charles Dickens, John Stuart Mill, William Gladstone and Benjamin Jowett, advised every government on military medicine and Indian sanitation, and refused all honours. She was the first woman appointed to the Order of Merit, in 1907, and died in her sleep in London in August 1910, aged ninety.
