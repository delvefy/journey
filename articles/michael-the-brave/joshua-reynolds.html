Joshua Reynolds

English portrait painter, 1723–1792

Born in Devon, Reynolds rose to become the foremost British painter of his century and the chief architect of his nation’s artistic prestige. Formative years in Italy steeped him in the masters he revered above all others—the grandeur of Michelangelo, the perfection of Raphael, whose Vatican frescoes he copied until the cold damaged his hearing, and the rich colour of Titian and the Venetians. From their example he forged the “Grand Style,” the belief that portraiture could aspire to the dignity of history painting.

Returning to London, he made himself indispensable to fashionable society, painting statesmen, beauties, soldiers, and actors with an inventive variety of pose and mood. He was a man of wide cultivation as much as a craftsman: with his friend Samuel Johnson he founded the Literary Club, and he numbered Edmund Burke, Oliver Goldsmith, and David Garrick among his intimates, recording many of them on canvas.

In 1768 he became the first president of the new Royal Academy, a post he held until his death, and through its annual Discourses he set out a learned theory of art that urged students to study the great traditions rather than mere appearances. Knighted and honoured, he worked in friendly rivalry with the more instinctive Thomas Gainsborough. When failing eyesight at last forced down his brush, he was mourned as a national figure and laid to rest in St Paul’s Cathedral.
