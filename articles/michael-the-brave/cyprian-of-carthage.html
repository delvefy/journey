Cyprian of Carthage

Bishop and martyr, c.200–258

Thascius Caecilius Cyprianus was a wealthy and cultivated pagan rhetorician of Carthage who converted to Christianity around 246 and, within a few years, was chosen bishop of the great African city. He came to the faith steeped in the writings of his countryman Tertullian, whom he so revered that he is said to have called for his books each day with the words, “Give me the master.”

His episcopate was a school of crisis. When the persecution of Decius struck, Cyprian withdrew into hiding and governed his flock by letter, and when the storm passed he had to confront the bitter question of the “lapsed” — those who had bought safety by sacrificing — steering a middle course between harsh rigorists and easy forgivers. He supported the lawful pope against the schism of Novatian and later quarrelled with Rome over whether heretics’ baptisms were valid. From these struggles came his great treatise on the unity of the Church, with its famous maxim that no one can have God for a father who does not have the Church for a mother.

When the emperor Valerian renewed the persecution, Cyprian was first exiled and then, in 258, beheaded outside Carthage, meeting death with composure before a great crowd — the first African bishop to win the martyr’s crown. His thought shaped the Latin Church profoundly, and a century and a half later Augustine would invoke his authority in the battles of the African church.
