Alexander Hamilton

American founding father and first Secretary of the Treasury, c.1755–1804

Born out of wedlock on the Caribbean island of Nevis and orphaned young, Hamilton rose entirely by talent. Sent to New York to study, he threw himself into the Revolutionary cause, and his energy and intellect soon brought him to the side of George Washington, whose chief aide-de-camp and trusted confidant he became. He commanded troops with distinction at the climactic siege of Yorktown.

After the war he turned his formidable mind to building a nation. Convinced that the loose Confederation was failing, he became a driving force behind the Constitutional Convention and then, with James Madison and John Jay, wrote the bulk of The Federalist, the essays that remain the classic defence of the Constitution. As Washington’s first Treasury Secretary he created the financial architecture of the United States: a national bank, federal assumption of state debts, a system of credit and revenue that placed the new republic on a sound footing.

His vision of a strong central government and commercial economy set him against Thomas Jefferson, and their clash gave birth to the nation’s first party system. He also quarrelled bitterly with John Adams, splitting their Federalist Party, and made a lasting enemy of Aaron Burr. In 1804 that enmity ended in a duel at Weehawken, where Burr’s shot mortally wounded him, cutting short one of the most consequential lives of the founding era.
