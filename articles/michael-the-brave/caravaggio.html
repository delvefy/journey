Caravaggio

Italian Baroque painter, 1571–1610

Born Michelangelo Merisi and known by the name of his Lombard hometown, Caravaggio arrived in Rome a poor and unruly young man and there transformed European painting. Rejecting idealised convention, he worked directly from living models—using street people and courtesans for his saints and Virgins—and plunged his figures into a fierce, theatrical play of light and shadow. His great public cycles, such as the scenes of Saint Matthew painted for the Contarelli Chapel and his startling Conversion of Saint Paul, made him the most talked-about and imitated artist in the city.

His genius was matched by a violent temper. He brawled, carried a sword, and was repeatedly in trouble with the law until, in 1606, he killed a man in a fight and fled Rome under sentence of death. The remaining years were a desperate flight—to Naples, to Malta, where he was made a knight only to be imprisoned and expelled, and to Sicily—all the while painting masterpieces darker and more urgent than before.

In 1610, still hoping for a papal pardon, he died alone on a Tuscan beach at thirty-eight. Yet his revolution outlived him: his dramatic realism and tenebrism swept through Italy, the Low Countries, and Spain, where it shaped the young Diego Velázquez. Long neglected, he is now ranked among the boldest innovators in the history of art.
