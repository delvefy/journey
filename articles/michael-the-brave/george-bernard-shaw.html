George Bernard Shaw

Irish playwright and critic, 1856–1950

Born in Dublin and largely self-taught, Shaw moved to London as a young man and spent lean years as a struggling novelist before finding his voice as a music and theatre critic of dazzling, combative brilliance. A committed socialist, he helped found the Fabian Society and devoted endless energy to pamphlets, lectures, and the cause of reform. He championed the dramas of Henrik Ibsen and set out to make the English stage a forum for ideas.

His own plays did exactly that, wrapping serious argument—about poverty, war, religion, and the relations of the sexes—in irresistible wit. Mrs Warren’s Profession, Man and Superman, Major Barbara, Saint Joan, and above all Pygmalion, later transformed into the musical My Fair Lady, made him the most important British dramatist since the seventeenth century. A relentless iconoclast, he needled the cult of Shakespeare with the coinage “Bardolatry,” even as he measured himself against the master.

Tall, bearded, vegetarian, and teetotal, “G.B.S.” became a public sage and the most quoted man of his time. He won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1925 and, uniquely, an Academy Award for the screen Pygmalion. He went on writing and provoking almost to the end, dying in 1950 at ninety-four after a fall in his garden.
