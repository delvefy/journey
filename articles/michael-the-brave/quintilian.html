Quintilian

Roman rhetorician, c. 35–c. 100 CE

Marcus Fabius Quintilianus was the most celebrated teacher of the Roman Empire and the author of antiquity's fullest treatise on education. Born around 35 CE at Calagurris in Roman Spain, he was brought to Rome and rose to become the leading master of rhetoric of his day.

His eminence was made official when the emperor Vespasian appointed him to a publicly salaried chair of rhetoric—the first such professorship in Roman history. For some twenty years he trained the sons of the elite, numbering the young Pliny the Younger among his pupils, and the emperor Domitian honoured him with consular rank and entrusted to him the education of his designated heirs.

In retirement Quintilian composed the work that made him immortal, the Institutio Oratoria, in twelve books. It traces the formation of the ideal orator from the cradle to the height of his powers, insisting that true eloquence belongs only to the good man—"a good man skilled in speaking." Throughout, he held up Cicero as the unsurpassable model and condemned the clever, fragmented manner of Seneca as a corruption of taste. His tenth book, a swift survey of the great Greek and Latin authors, became a reading list for centuries.

The complete Institutio was lost in the Middle Ages, surviving only in mangled fragments that Petrarch mourned. When the humanist Poggio Bracciolini unearthed a whole manuscript in 1416, the recovery electrified Renaissance Italy, and Quintilian became the guiding authority of humanist schooling from Erasmus onward.
