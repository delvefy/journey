Domitian

Last Flavian emperor of Rome, 51–96 AD

Domitian was the younger son of Vespasian and the brother of Titus, born into a family that seized the empire in the chaos of 69. As a young man he was caught in Rome during the final struggle, narrowly escaping with his life before his father's victory. Under Vespasian and Titus he was kept on the margins of power, heaped with honours but given little real authority, and the resentment of those years marked his character. On the sudden death of Titus in 81 he at last became emperor.

He proved a hard, suspicious, and highly capable ruler. He reformed the coinage and managed the finances with care, secured the Rhine and Danube frontiers, and completed great works begun by his family, including the Colosseum, while raising a vast palace on the Palatine. But he governed as an open autocrat, styling himself "lord and god," humiliating the Senate, and stripping away the polite fictions of Augustan rule.

His later years darkened into a reign of terror, as treason trials and executions fell upon senators and suspected enemies. Among the men whose careers he shaped was the historian Tacitus, who would help blacken his memory, and he recalled the successful general Agricola from Britain. In 96 Domitian was struck down in a palace conspiracy, possibly with his own wife's knowledge, and the relieved Senate condemned his memory and chose the elderly Nerva to succeed him. With his death the Flavian dynasty came to an end.
