Carl Jung

Swiss psychiatrist, founder of analytical psychology, 1875–1961

A Swiss pastor’s son, Jung trained in psychiatry at the Burghölzli clinic in Zurich, where his word-association experiments gave clinical weight to the idea of the unconscious “complex.” His brilliant early papers drew the attention of Freud, who embraced him as a son and heir to the psychoanalytic movement. But the younger man could not accept Freud’s insistence on sexuality as the root of all neurosis, and in 1913 the two parted in bitterness, plunging Jung into a long, near-psychotic confrontation with his own visions.

From that ordeal he built his own analytical psychology. Beneath the personal unconscious, he argued, lay a deeper collective unconscious shared by all humanity and populated by archetypes—the shadow, the anima, the wise old man—recurring in the myths and religions of every culture. He described the lifelong process of “individuation,” coined the terms introvert and extravert, and ranged widely through alchemy, astrology, Eastern philosophy, and what he called synchronicity, meaningful coincidence.

Controversial among scientists for his mysticism, Jung nonetheless exerted a vast influence far beyond the clinic, shaping the modern study of myth, religion, and creativity, and inspiring later systems of personality typing. His ideas spoke powerfully to artists and writers in search of the soul, among them the novelist Hermann Hesse. He died at Küsnacht on Lake Zurich in 1961.
