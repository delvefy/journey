Theodora

Byzantine empress, c.500–548

Theodora rose from the very bottom of Byzantine society to its summit. Born around 500, the daughter of a bear-keeper at the Hippodrome of Constantinople, she worked as an actress and entertainer — a calling that the hostile historian Procopius painted in scandalous colours in his Secret History. She caught the eye of Justinian, heir to the throne, who loved her enough to have the law changed so that a man of his rank might marry a woman of hers, and when he became emperor in 527 she was crowned beside him.

She proved no mere consort but a partner in power, whose counsel Justinian sought and whose will shaped his reign. Her finest hour came in 532, when the Nika riots filled the streets with fire and the emperor and his court prepared to flee. Theodora, by the famous account, refused, declaring that royalty made a fine burial shroud and that she for one would not live to see herself uncrowned. Shamed into resolve, Justinian stayed, and the revolt was drowned in blood.

As empress she used her influence to protect women, backing laws against the trafficking of girls and easing the lot of wives, and she sheltered the Monophysite Christians whom her husband’s orthodoxy condemned, perhaps by design dividing the imperial favour between the rival faiths. Her serene image still gazes out in gold from the mosaics of San Vitale at Ravenna. She died, probably of cancer, in 548, long before Justinian, who is said never to have ceased to mourn her.
