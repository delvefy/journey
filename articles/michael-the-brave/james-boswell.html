James Boswell

Scottish biographer and diarist, 1740–1795

The son of a stern Edinburgh judge, Boswell trained reluctantly in law while burning with an ambition to know the great men of his time. In 1763 he engineered a meeting with Samuel Johnson in a London bookshop, beginning the friendship that would define his life. Sociable, vain, self-doubting, and endlessly curious, he had a genius for drawing others into conversation and for setting it all down on paper.

On a continental tour he boldly sought out the age’s most celebrated minds, calling on Jean-Jacques Rousseau and on Voltaire at Ferney, and attaching himself to the Corsican patriot Pasquale Paoli, whose struggle he publicised in An Account of Corsica—a book that briefly made “Corsica Boswell” a celebrity. He even pressed the dying David Hume for his thoughts on death and unbelief, recording the philosopher’s untroubled calm.

Boswell’s masterpiece was The Life of Samuel Johnson (1791), assembled from decades of intimate notes and conversation. By preserving Johnson’s talk, manner, and moods in unprecedented detail, it created a new kind of biography and is often called the greatest in the English language. Boswell himself died little honoured, his reputation as a mere hanger-on; only in the twentieth century, when his astonishingly candid private journals came to light, was he recognised as one of the great self-revealing writers of all time.
