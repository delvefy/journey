Valentinus

Gnostic teacher, c.100–160

Valentinus was the most brilliant and influential of the Gnostic teachers of the second century. Born in the Nile Delta around the year 100 and educated in the Greek learning of Alexandria, he came to Rome in the middle decades of the century and gathered a wide following. According to a story told by his enemy Tertullian, he was once a candidate for the bishopric of Rome and, passed over, turned to teaching a Christianity of his own.

His system was a vast and intricate cosmic drama, deeply coloured by Platonism. From the unknowable Father unfolded the Pleroma, the fullness of paired divine emanations called aeons; from the fall of the youngest of them, Sophia, or Wisdom, came the flawed material world, fashioned by a lower craftsman-god, the Demiurge. Trapped within matter, the divine spark in certain souls could be freed only by gnosis, the saving knowledge that Valentinus claimed to impart.

Almost none of his own writings survive; his teaching is known largely through the fierce refutations of the orthodox — Irenaeus, whose great work against heresy was aimed above all at the Valentinians, and Tertullian and Clement after him. Through disciples such as Ptolemy and Heracleon his school spread across the empire and long troubled the Church, and the beautiful meditation called the Gospel of Truth, recovered in modern times, may be his. He died around the middle of the century, the chief architect of Christian Gnosticism and the great adversary against whom the early Church defined its orthodoxy.
