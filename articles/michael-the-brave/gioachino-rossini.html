Gioachino Rossini

Italian opera composer, 1792–1868

Rossini was born in Pesaro to musician parents and showed such precocious facility that by his twenties he was the most celebrated and prolific opera composer in Europe, turning out scores at astonishing speed. His sparkling comedies, above all The Barber of Seville (1816), said to have been written in a matter of weeks, together with La Cenerentola and L’italiana in Algeri, remain pillars of the comic repertory.

He was equally a master of serious opera, and works such as Tancredi, Otello, and Semiramide spread his fame and refined the art of bel canto singing. Settling for a time in Paris as director of the Théâtre-Italien, he crowned his theatrical career with the grand William Tell (1829), whose galloping overture became one of the best-known passages in all music.

Then, at the height of his powers and only thirty-seven, Rossini astonished the world by giving up the operatic stage almost entirely, though he lived for nearly four more decades. He turned to sacred music, the Stabat Mater and the Petite messe solennelle, and to witty piano trifles he called his "Sins of Old Age," while cultivating a legendary reputation as a gourmand. He had once made a pilgrimage to the deaf Beethoven in Vienna; when he died near Paris in 1868, the younger Verdi proposed that Italy’s composers honour him with a collective Requiem.
