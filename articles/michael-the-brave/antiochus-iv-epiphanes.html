Antiochus IV Epiphanes

Seleucid king, reigned 175–164 BC

Antiochus IV was a younger son of Antiochus III the Great. After Rome crushed his father's army and imposed the harsh Treaty of Apamea, the young prince was sent to Rome as a hostage, where he spent years absorbing Roman customs and ambition. When his brother Seleucus IV was assassinated, Antiochus returned and seized the throne in 175 BC, brushing aside the rightful heir. He took the grandiose title Epiphanes, "God Manifest," though detractors twisted it to Epimanes, "the Mad."

An energetic and aggressive ruler, he sought to restore Seleucid power and to bind his diverse realm together through Greek culture. He waged the Sixth Syrian War against Ptolemaic Egypt and came close to conquering it, but on his second invasion the Roman envoy Gaius Popillius Laenas met him outside Alexandria, drew a circle in the sand around his feet, and demanded he agree to withdraw before stepping out of it. Humiliated, Antiochus obeyed.

Turning his frustration on Judea, he plundered the Temple in Jerusalem, forbade the practice of the Jewish religion on pain of death, and in 167 BC rededicated the sanctuary to Olympian Zeus. This assault on the faith ignited the Maccabean Revolt under Judas Maccabeus, which would in time tear Judea from Seleucid control. Antiochus marched east to replenish his treasury among the upper provinces and died there in 164 BC. To the authors of Daniel and the Books of the Maccabees he remained the archetype of the tyrant who wars against God.
