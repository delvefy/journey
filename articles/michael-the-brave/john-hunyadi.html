John Hunyadi

Voivode of Transylvania, Regent of Hungary, c.1406–1456

János Hunyadi — Iancu de Hunedoara to his Romanian-speaking soldiers, Ioannes Corvinus to the chanceries — was born about 1406, the son of Vajk, a Wallachian boyar who had taken service with Sigismund of Luxembourg and was granted the castle of Hunyad in southern Transylvania for it. He learnt soldiering in the bands of the Serbian despot Stefan Lazarević and at the Italian court of Filippo Maria Visconti, and returned home schooled in Hussite wagon-fort tactics and Italian artillery. By 1441 King Władysław III had made him voivode of Transylvania and count of the Székelys, the joint border command facing the Ottoman frontier.

The 1442 raid of Mezid Bey into Transylvania ended in disaster for the Ottomans at Sibiu and on the Ialomița: Hunyadi destroyed two armies in eight months. The Long Campaign that he and Władysław led down the Morava valley in the winter of 1443–44 reached the Balkan passes, retook much of Serbia, and forced Murad II to the truce of Edirne—a triumph the papal legate Cesarini almost immediately persuaded the king to break. The crusade that resulted ended at Varna on 10 November 1444; Władysław died charging the Janissary line, and Hunyadi escaped through Wallachia disguised as a peasant. The estates of Hungary elected him regent in 1446 for the absent boy-king Ladislas V, and for seven years he ran the kingdom from his fortified town of Hunedoara.

The second Kosovo, in October 1448, cost him another army and his nephew. His greatest victory came at the end. When Mehmed II laid siege to Belgrade in July 1456 with the same heavy guns that had taken Constantinople, Hunyadi crossed the Sava with a relief force largely raised by the Franciscan preacher Giovanni da Capistrano, broke the Ottoman boom on the Danube, and on 22 July routed the besiegers in a sortie from the walls. The pope ordered the noonday Angelus bell rung throughout Christendom to mark the victory, and the practice remains. Hunyadi himself died of plague three weeks later, on 11 August 1456, at the camp of Zemun. His younger son became King Matthias Corvinus of Hungary.
