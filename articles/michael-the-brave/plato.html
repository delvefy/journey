Plato

Athenian philosopher, c. 428–348 BC

Plato was born into an aristocratic Athenian family and seemed destined for politics, but the trial and execution of his teacher Socrates turned him against the city’s democracy and toward philosophy. He spent his early years preserving Socrates’s memory and, after travels that took him to southern Italy and Sicily, founded the Academy at Athens—an institution of study and research often called the first university of the Western world, where the young Aristotle would one day come to learn.

Almost all of his work survives in the form of dialogues, in most of which Socrates is the principal speaker, probing his companions toward truth. In the Republic Plato imagined an ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings and offered the famous allegory of the cave, in which prisoners mistake shadows for reality. Across the dialogues he developed his theory of Forms—the doctrine that the changing things we see are imperfect copies of eternal, perfect ideas grasped only by reason.

His attempts to put philosophy into practice failed: invited to tutor the rulers of Syracuse, he found tyranny resistant to wisdom and barely escaped with his life. But as a writer and teacher he succeeded beyond measure. His range—ethics, politics, knowledge, love, the soul, the cosmos—set the agenda for all that followed, prompting the remark that the European philosophical tradition is a series of footnotes to Plato. Through the Academy and his pupil Aristotle, his influence reached across the centuries into the heart of Western thought.
