Dante Alighieri

c.1265–1321, Florentine poet

Dante Alighieri was born in Florence in May or June of 1265, into a family of the lesser nobility on the Guelph side of the city's politics. As a boy he encountered Beatrice Portinari, the daughter of a neighbour, and made her the object of an idealized love that would order his entire poetic life. He studied with the encyclopaedist Brunetto Latini, formed a friendship with the poet Guido Cavalcanti, and grew into a leading writer of the dolce stil novo. After Beatrice's death in 1290 he composed the Vita Nuova, half lyric and half prose memoir, in her memory.

He served Florence in arms at Campaldino in 1289, sat as one of the six priors of the city in the summer of 1300, and led a diplomatic embassy to Rome at the end of 1301. While he was absent his Black Guelph rivals, supported by Pope Boniface VIII, took power. In 1302 he was sentenced in absentia to confiscation, perpetual exile, and burning at the stake should he return. He never saw Florence again.

The remaining twenty years he passed in the courts of Verona, Treviso, and Ravenna, writing the unfinished Latin treatises De Vulgari Eloquentia on the vernacular and Monarchia on universal empire, the philosophical Convivio, and his great work, the Commedia — called Divina by Giovanni Boccaccio in the next generation. The poem casts him as a pilgrim led by Virgil through Hell and Purgatory and by Beatrice through Paradise. It draws on Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, Ovid, Cicero and Homer, packs the Tuscan vernacular with theology, politics and ferocious satire, and puts Boniface VIII himself among the simoniac popes.

He died at Ravenna of malaria in September 1321, weeks after finishing the Paradiso, and was buried there in the church of San Francesco. The Commedia at once became the foundational text of the Italian language, and through Petrarch, Boccaccio, and a long line of later readers fixed Dante as one of the central poets of the Western canon.
