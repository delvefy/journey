Orson Welles

1915–1985, American actor, director and writer

George Orson Welles was born in Kenosha, Wisconsin, the second son of an inventor and a concert pianist. Orphaned at fifteen, he travelled in Ireland and bluffed his way onto the stage of Dublin's Gate Theatre. He returned to New York at nineteen and, with John Houseman, joined the Federal Theatre Project, where his all-black “Voodoo” Macbeth and his anti-fascist Julius Caesar in modern dress made him the most talked-of director on Broadway by twenty-two.

With Houseman he founded the Mercury Theatre, whose 30 October 1938 radio broadcast of H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds, staged as live news bulletins, panicked thousands of listeners and made Welles a national name overnight. RKO offered him an unprecedented contract granting final cut. The result, Citizen Kane (1941), was written with Herman J. Mankiewicz, photographed by Gregg Toland, and modelled on William Randolph Hearst. Hearst tried to suppress it; the film failed at the box office and was long afterwards routinely named the greatest ever made.

The Magnificent Ambersons followed in 1942, but the studio recut it in his absence on a wartime good-neighbour mission to Brazil, and from that point Welles fought studios for the rest of his career. He still produced The Lady from Shanghai with his wife Rita Hayworth, a baroque Macbeth, a Mexican-shot Othello that won at Cannes, Touch of Evil with Marlene Dietrich, The Trial after Kafka, and the late masterpiece Chimes at Midnight, drawn from five Shakespeare plays and centred on his own performance as Falstaff.

He acted brilliantly for others, most famously as Harry Lime in Carol Reed's The Third Man, narrated documentaries, performed magic, and grew vast on television commercials and chat shows. Many late films he financed himself and left unfinished. He died of a heart attack in Los Angeles in 1985, dictating notes for one more project.

The road continues to Charlie Chaplin.
