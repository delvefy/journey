Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite

Christian Neoplatonist mystic, fl. c. 500

Around the year 500, somewhere in the Greek-speaking East, an unknown Christian writer composed a small body of works of extraordinary depth and published them under a borrowed name. He claimed to be Dionysius the Areopagite, the Athenian whom the apostle Paul had converted on the hill of Mars, and the disguise lent his writings an almost apostolic authority that they carried, unquestioned, for a thousand years. His true identity has never been recovered, and he is known to history simply as Pseudo-Dionysius.

His treatises — on the divine names, on the celestial and the church hierarchies, and the brief, dazzling Mystical Theology — wove the Neoplatonism of Proclus into Christian doctrine. He taught a God beyond all names and all being, who can be approached only by stripping away every concept until the soul, in a luminous darkness of unknowing, is united with the unknowable. He arranged the angels into nine ranks in three triads, an ordering that became the settled picture of heaven and that Dante would follow in his Paradiso.

Translated into Latin in the ninth century, the Areopagite became one of the supreme authorities of medieval thought; Thomas Aquinas cited him constantly, and his negative theology fed the mysticism of later ages, above all that of Meister Eckhart. Only with the new scholarship of the Renaissance was the pious fiction of his name at last exposed — long after his vision of ascent through unknowing had reshaped the spiritual life of the West.
