Farinelli

Carlo Broschi, castrato singer, 1705–1782

Farinelli, born Carlo Broschi in Andria in 1705, was the most celebrated singer of the eighteenth century and the supreme example of the castrato, the male voice preserved in boyhood range by a cruel operation and trained to a power and agility no natural voice could match. He studied in Naples under the renowned teacher Nicola Porpora, who shaped both his technique and his repertory.

His voice astonished every audience that heard it, prized for its enormous range, its long sustained breath, and its dazzling ornamentation. He triumphed across Italy and at Vienna, then went to London in the 1730s as the great attraction of the Opera of the Nobility, the company founded to rival Handel, where his celebrity reached extraordinary heights.

In 1737 he accepted an invitation to the Spanish court, and there his life took its strangest turn. King Philip V was sunk in a profound melancholy, and Farinelli's singing alone seemed to soothe him; the singer was retained at court and, by tradition, sang the same few arias to the king night after night for years.

He became a trusted royal favourite, showered with honours and entrusted with the direction of the court's operas and with influence in its affairs, all of which he is said to have used with notable modesty and good sense. Dismissed when a new king came to the throne, he retired in great wealth to Bologna, where he lived out his last decades as a cultivated gentleman, corresponding with his lifelong friend Metastasio and receiving visiting musicians. The monarch whose sorrows his voice had charmed was Philip V of Spain.
