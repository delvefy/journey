Pietro Metastasio

Imperial court poet and master of opera seria, 1698–1782

Pietro Metastasio was the most celebrated Italian poet of the eighteenth century, the man who gave the form of opera seria its classic shape. Born Pietro Trapassi in Rome in 1698, the son of a soldier turned shopkeeper, he was a child prodigy who improvised verse in the street. The jurist and scholar Gian Vincenzo Gravina noticed the boy, adopted him, gave him a rigorous classical education, and Hellenized his surname to Metastasio.

Inheriting Gravina's fortune, he moved to Naples, where the singer Marianna Bulgarelli encouraged his theatrical gifts. His first full libretto, Didone abbandonata, triumphed in 1724 and established his manner: elegant, lucid Italian verse, noble Roman and mythological subjects, and a moralizing exaltation of duty, clemency, and self-mastery.

In 1730 the Emperor Charles VI summoned him to Vienna as Caesarean court poet, the post of poeta cesareo once held by Apostolo Zeno. There Metastasio remained for the rest of his life, producing the great dramas of his maturity—Artaserse, Olimpiade, Demofoonte, Attilio Regolo, and La clemenza di Tito among them.

His librettos became the common property of European music: set, reset, and set again hundreds of times by composers across the century. Handel, Vivaldi, Hasse, Gluck, and the young Mozart all turned his verses into opera, so that a single Metastasian text might be clothed in dozens of different scores. He died in Vienna in 1782, the last great representative of an Arcadian, classicizing ideal that the new age of reform would soon sweep aside. Among the composers who set his words while reshaping the very art was the Venetian master Antonio Vivaldi.
