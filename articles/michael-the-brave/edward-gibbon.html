Edward Gibbon

English historian, 1737–1794

A sickly and bookish only child from Putney, Gibbon educated himself through omnivorous reading after a wayward youth that included a brief, scandalous conversion to Catholicism and back. On his grand tour he came to Rome, and there, by his own famous account, as he sat musing amid the ruins of the Capitol while barefoot friars sang vespers in the ruined Temple of Jupiter, the idea first started in his mind of writing the decline and fall of the city.

That idea became the great labour of his life. Published in six volumes between 1776 and 1788, The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire traced thirteen centuries from the height of the Antonine age to the fall of Constantinople in 1453. Written in measured, ironic, magnificent prose, it set a new standard for narrative history. Its chapters coolly attributing part of Rome’s collapse to the rise of Christianity scandalised the pious and embroiled their author in long controversy.

Gibbon sat for a time in Parliament, moved in the brilliant circle of London’s Literary Club, and was encouraged by David Hume, whose generous praise of the first volume he treasured. He completed the work in his beloved Lausanne, recording the bittersweet midnight hour when he laid down his pen after twenty years. His masterpiece has never gone out of print.
