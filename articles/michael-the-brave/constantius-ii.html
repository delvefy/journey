Constantius II

Roman emperor, 317–361 AD

Constantius II was the second surviving son of Constantine the Great and Fausta, born in 317. When his father died in 337, the empire was divided among the three brothers, and a bloody purge of their relatives—in which Constantius was at least complicit—swept away rival cousins and uncles, sparing only the boys Gallus and Julian. Constantius took the eastern provinces.

His brothers soon fell: Constantine II died invading Italy in 340, and Constans was killed by the usurper Magnentius in 350. After the immensely bloody Battle of Mursa in 351, one of the costliest of the age, Constantius broke Magnentius, and by 353 he ruled the whole empire alone.

A convinced supporter of a moderate Arian theology, he used imperial power to impose his religious settlement, summoning councils, exiling Athanasius again and again, and pressing Nicene bishops to conform—provoking the lament that “the world groaned to find itself Arian.” On the eastern frontier he waged a long, inconclusive war against the Persian king Shapur II.

Childless and overstretched, he raised his cousins to share the burden: first Gallus, whom he later put to death, then Julian, whom he sent to govern Gaul. When Julian's troops proclaimed him Augustus in 360, civil war loomed. Constantius marched west to confront him but fell ill and died in Cilicia in 361, reportedly naming Julian his heir with his last breath. He left the empire whole but its religious divisions unhealed.
