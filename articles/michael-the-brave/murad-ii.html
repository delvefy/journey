Murad II

Ottoman Sultan, victor of Varna, 1404–1451

Murad was born in June 1404 at Amasya, where his father Mehmed I held court during the Interregnum. He came to the throne at seventeen in May 1421, and the Byzantine government immediately released the pretender Mustafa “Düzme” — supposedly a surviving son of Bayezid — to start a civil war along the Marmara coast. Murad caught Mustafa near Edirne in January 1422, hanged him from the city wall, and turned at once to besiege Constantinople in June. The defence held; Murad raised the siege, suppressed a rebellion by his younger brother Mustafa in Anatolia the next year, and signed a humiliating tribute upon the Byzantines that made the Palaiologoi clients of the Porte in fact if not in name.

Twenty years of methodical campaigning followed. He took Thessalonica from Venice in March 1430 after a four-year siege; he reduced the Albanian and Bosnian princes to vassalage; he absorbed the despotate of the Morea and forced the Serbian despot George Branković to give him a daughter, Mara, in marriage. The crusade led by King Władysław III of Poland-Hungary, the regent John Hunyadi, and the papal legate Giuliano Cesarini broke into the Balkans during the summer of 1444 after Murad had abdicated in favour of his twelve-year-old son Mehmed and retired to Manisa; he was hauled back across the straits, met the crusaders at Varna on the Black Sea on 10 November, and crushed them. Władysław was beheaded at the front of his charge, Cesarini was killed in the rout, and the last great cross-Danubian crusade was over.

He abdicated a second time after Varna, returned to put down a Janissary mutiny and to fight Hunyadi again at the second battle of Kosovo in October 1448, where the Hungarian army was again broken. Murad combined the campaigner with the patron: he founded the Üç Şerefeli mosque at Edirne and the great külliye of Muradiye at Bursa; he kept the company of Persian poets in exile from the Timurid court; the Janissary corps and the system of devşirme recruitment took its mature form under him. He died of a stroke at Edirne on 3 February 1451, leaving the empire stable, the army superb, and the road to Constantinople open for his son Mehmed.

The road continues to John Hunyadi.
