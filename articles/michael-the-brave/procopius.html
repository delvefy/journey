Procopius

Byzantine historian, c.500–565

Procopius was born around 500 at Caesarea in Palestine and trained in law and rhetoric before entering the service of the general Belisarius about 527 as his legal secretary and adviser. In that capacity he travelled to the empire’s farthest fronts — to the Persian frontier against the great shah Khosrow, to North Africa for the war on the Vandals, and to Italy for the long struggle with the Goths — seeing at first hand the campaigns he would record.

His History of the Wars, in eight books, is the indispensable narrative of the age of Justinian, and he wrote it consciously in the manner of the classical masters, taking Thucydides as his model for sober military history and Herodotus for his ethnographic digressions on distant peoples. A second work, The Buildings, praised in glowing terms the churches and fortifications Justinian raised across the empire, the Hagia Sophia foremost among them.

But Procopius is most notorious for a third book that he dared not publish in his lifetime. The Secret History turned savagely on the very rulers he had elsewhere flattered, painting Justinian as a demon in human form and Theodora as a creature of boundless vice — a startling counter-narrative whose rediscovery astonished later readers. As the last great historian in the classical tradition, Procopius became a principal quarry for the scholars who came after; Edward Gibbon drew on him at length in tracing the decline of Rome. He died about 565.
