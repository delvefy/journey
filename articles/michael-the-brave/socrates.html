Socrates

Athenian philosopher, 470–399 BC

Socrates wrote nothing, founded no school, and charged no fees, yet he stands at the head of the Western philosophical tradition. The son of a stonemason and a midwife, he served as a citizen-soldier in the Peloponnesian War, showing notable courage at Potidaea and Delium, before devoting himself to questioning his fellow Athenians in the public spaces of the city. He claimed to know only that he knew nothing, and made a method of that humility.

That method—relentless questioning to expose the muddle beneath confident opinions—is still called Socratic. Buttonholing politicians, poets, and craftsmen, he showed that those who claimed to understand justice, piety, or virtue could not in fact define them. He insisted that the care of the soul mattered more than wealth or reputation, and that “the unexamined life is not worth living.” To many he was the wisest of men; to others a dangerous nuisance who corrupted the young and mocked the gods.

In 399 BC, with Athens bruised by defeat and political upheaval, he was brought to trial on charges of impiety and corrupting the youth. Offered chances to escape or to propose a lenient penalty, he refused to abandon his principles or flatter the court. Condemned to death, he drank the cup of hemlock calmly among his friends. His trial and death, recorded by his pupil Plato, became one of the defining moral dramas of antiquity and made Socrates the very pattern of the philosopher.
