Iamblichus

Neoplatonist philosopher, c.245–325

Iamblichus was born about 245 at Chalcis in Roman Syria, into a noble family, and studied under Porphyry before founding his own school at Apamea, where he became the dominant Neoplatonist of his age. Though he inherited the system of Plato as refined by Plotinus and his own master, he transformed it in a decisive way.

Where Plotinus had taught that the soul could rise to the divine by contemplation alone, Iamblichus insisted that it also needed theurgy — sacred rites and rituals through which the gods themselves lift the worshipper upward. In his treatise On the Mysteries he defended this ritual path and the old religion against the doubts of Porphyry, and he peopled the Neoplatonic cosmos with ranks of gods and spirits, giving the philosophy a frankly religious cast. A devoted admirer of Pythagoras, he composed works expounding the Pythagorean way of life and revered the ancient sage as a model of holiness.

So great was his reputation that later generations called him “the divine Iamblichus.” His theurgic Neoplatonism shaped the school of Athens, where Proclus would carry it to its most elaborate form, and it inspired the emperor Julian the Apostate in his doomed effort to restore the worship of the old gods. Iamblichus died about 325, just as the empire he hoped to keep pagan was turning Christian under Constantine.
