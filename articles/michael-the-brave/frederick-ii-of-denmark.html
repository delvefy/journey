Frederick II of Denmark

King of Denmark and Norway, 1534–1588

Frederick II came to the throne of Denmark and Norway in 1559 and ruled a kingdom that controlled the sound between the Baltic and the North Sea, a position of great strategic and commercial importance. Early in his reign he led his realm into the long and inconclusive Northern Seven Years’ War against Sweden, a costly struggle for dominance in the Baltic that ended without decisive gain. Thereafter he turned to consolidating his power, strengthening the navy, and enriching the crown through the tolls levied on the busy shipping that passed his shores.

A lover of display and culture, Frederick rebuilt the great castle of Kronborg at Elsinore as a magnificent Renaissance palace—the setting Shakespeare would later borrow for Hamlet—commanding the narrow strait where his toll-collectors grew rich. He gathered scholars and artists to his court and made his kingdom, for a time, a notable centre of learning in northern Europe.

His most lasting service was to science. Recognizing the genius of the astronomer Tycho Brahe, Frederick granted him the island of Hven and the revenues to build and run the observatory of Uraniborg, an act of royal patronage that made possible the most precise astronomical observations of the pre-telescopic age. Through his marriage he also wove his house into European royalty: his daughter Anne of Denmark married James VI of Scotland, who became James I of England, so that Frederick was grandfather to a line of British kings.
