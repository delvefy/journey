Charles de Gaulle

1890–1970, leader of Free France and founder of the Fifth Republic

Charles André Joseph Marie de Gaulle was born in Lille to a teacher of philosophy and grew up in a household where the humiliation of 1870 was kept alive as a private wound. He chose Saint-Cyr, fought as a young captain at Verdun in 1916, was wounded three times and taken prisoner. Between the wars he wrote on the future of armoured warfare in Vers l'Armée de Métier, arguing that small mobile professional armies of tanks and aircraft would decide the next war. The French general staff ignored him; the German army did not.

When the panzers broke through at Sedan in May 1940, de Gaulle, briefly an under-secretary of war, flew to London on 17 June and from a BBC studio broadcast his appeal of the eighteenth: "France has lost a battle, but France has not lost the war." For four years from this exile he assembled the Free French forces, negotiated with Churchill and Roosevelt who never quite trusted him, and built the legitimacy that allowed him to enter Paris at the head of the Liberation parade on 26 August 1944 and walk down the Champs-Élysées as bullets snapped at the cornices of the Crillon.

He resigned in January 1946 over the constitutional weakness of the Fourth Republic and spent twelve years writing his memoirs at Colombey-les-Deux-Églises. The Algerian crisis recalled him in May 1958; he drafted a new constitution that placed real power in an elected president, ended the Algerian war by granting independence in 1962, withdrew France from NATO's integrated command, vetoed Britain's entry into the Common Market twice, recognised the People's Republic of China and built the Franco-German treaty with Konrad Adenauer. The student and worker upheaval of May 1968 shook him, a referendum the following year went against him, and he resigned the day after. He died at Colombey on 9 November 1970.
