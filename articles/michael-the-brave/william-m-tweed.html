William M. Tweed

1823–1878, boss of New York's Tammany Hall

William Magear Tweed was born on the Lower East Side of Manhattan, the son of a Scots-Irish chairmaker. He worked as a bookkeeper and saddler before entering ward politics through the Americus, or Big Six, volunteer fire company, whose tiger emblem he later carried into Tammany Hall. By his early thirties he had served a single term in Congress, but his real career was local: alderman, school commissioner, deputy street commissioner, and from 1858 a member of the Tammany general committee.

In 1863 he became Grand Sachem of Tammany, and through the late 1860s he assembled the inner clique known as the Tweed Ring. With Mayor A. Oakey Hall, Comptroller Richard Connolly and Chamberlain Peter Sweeny, he controlled the public purse of the city and, through complicit Democrats at Albany, a large share of the state's. Contractors submitted padded bills for paving, sewers, and above all the new County Courthouse on Chambers Street, which absorbed somewhere between ten and thirteen million dollars in public funds while remaining unfinished.

Tweed also sheltered the Erie Railway clique of Jay Gould and Jim Fisk, sitting on the company's board and pushing the legislation that legalized their stock issues. At his peak in 1870 he was the third-largest landowner in New York City, a state senator, and a director of banks, gas companies, and the Brooklyn Bridge enterprise.

His fall came swiftly. In 1871 an embittered insider leaked the ring's account books to the New York Times, while Harper's Weekly ran a relentless campaign of cartoons by Thomas Nast that fixed Tweed's bloated image in the public mind. Samuel J. Tilden led the prosecution. Convicted of forgery and larceny in 1873, Tweed escaped briefly to Cuba and Spain, where he was identified from a Nast cartoon, was returned to New York, and died in the Ludlow Street jail in 1878.
