Justinian I

Byzantine emperor, 482–565

Justinian was born about 482 to a peasant family in the Latin-speaking Balkans and rose through the patronage of his uncle, the emperor Justin I, whom he succeeded in 527. With his wife Theodora, a woman of humble and scandalous origins who became his shrewdest counsellor, he ruled the Eastern Roman Empire for nearly four decades with boundless ambition, determined to restore the glory of Rome. In 532 the Nika riots in the Hippodrome nearly swept him from the throne, and only Theodora’s resolve and the swords of his generals saved his reign.

Out of the ashes of those riots he raised the church of Hagia Sophia, whose vast floating dome remained the wonder of the Christian world for a thousand years. Greater still was his work in law: the commission he set under the jurist Tribonian gathered the tangled mass of Roman jurisprudence into the Corpus Juris Civilis, the Code, Digest and Institutes that would become the foundation of the legal systems of much of Europe and beyond.

Through his general Belisarius and later the eunuch Narses he reconquered North Africa from the Vandals and Italy from the Ostrogoths, briefly restoring Roman rule across the western Mediterranean, while in the east he fought the Persia of Khosrow. He enforced Christian orthodoxy, closed the ancient philosophical academy of Athens, and weathered a catastrophic plague that bears his name. When he died in 565 his costly conquests were already slipping away, but his cathedral and his law endured as the great monuments of the Byzantine age.
