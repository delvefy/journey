Nikola Tesla

Serbian-American inventor and electrical engineer, 1856–1943

Born to a Serbian family in the Austrian Empire, Tesla was a visionary of electricity whose mind teemed with machines he claimed to see complete before he built them. He emigrated to America in 1884 to work briefly for Thomas Edison, but the two soon parted, and their dispute hardened into the famous “War of the Currents.” Against Edison’s direct current Tesla championed alternating current, which could be transmitted efficiently over long distances; backed by the industrialist George Westinghouse, AC won, and it powers the world to this day.

Tesla’s inventions were fundamental: the AC induction motor, the polyphase system, and the high-voltage “Tesla coil.” He helped harness Niagara Falls for electric power and did pioneering work in radio, wireless transmission, and remote control, anticipating technologies that arrived only decades later. A dazzling showman, he lit lamps wirelessly before astonished audiences, and counted among his friends the writer Mark Twain, who loved to tinker in his laboratory.

But Tesla was a poor businessman and an unworldly dreamer. His grand scheme for transmitting power without wires, funded for a time by J. P. Morgan, collapsed; his fortunes faded, and he spent his final years in genteel poverty in a New York hotel, feeding pigeons and spinning ever more fantastic prophecies. He died alone in 1943, his reputation long eclipsed, only to be rediscovered as one of the great—and strangest—geniuses of the electrical age.
