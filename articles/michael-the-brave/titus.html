Titus

Roman emperor, 39–81 AD

Titus, elder son of Vespasian, was raised at the imperial court and trained as a soldier, serving as a military tribune before joining his father's campaign to crush the great Jewish revolt. When Vespasian left Judea to claim the empire in 69, Titus took command of the war, and in 70 AD he stormed Jerusalem and destroyed the Second Temple—a calamity for the Jewish people commemorated in Rome by the triumphal Arch of Titus, which still depicts the sacred spoils being carried away.

Back in Rome he served as his father's right hand and praetorian prefect, feared for the hard measures by which he guarded the new dynasty. His love affair with the Judean queen Berenice scandalised Roman opinion, and on becoming emperor he reluctantly sent her away. For all that earlier reputation, his short reign from 79 won him lasting affection; contemporaries called him the love and delight of the human race for his open-handed generosity.

That generosity was sorely tested. Within months of his accession Mount Vesuvius erupted, burying Pompeii and Herculaneum and claiming the life of the scholar Pliny the Elder, and soon after a great fire and a plague struck Rome itself. Titus poured out relief for the stricken. He also completed and dedicated the Colosseum begun by his father, opening it with a hundred days of spectacles. It was to Titus that the scholar had dedicated his great encyclopaedia of nature. But he reigned barely two years, dying of fever in 81 and leaving the throne to his brother Domitian.
