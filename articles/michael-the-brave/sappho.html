Sappho

Archaic Greek lyric poet, c. 630–570 BCE

Sappho was an archaic Greek poet of the island of Lesbos, active around 600 BCE, and the most celebrated woman writer of the ancient world. Though only fragments of her work survive, ancient critics ranked her so highly that Plato is said to have called her the Tenth Muse, and later ages knew her simply as "the Poetess," as Homer was "the Poet."

She composed in the soft Aeolic dialect of her island, in intricate meters—one of which, the Sapphic stanza, still bears her name—and her subject was above all desire. Her poems, many addressed to the women and girls of her circle, speak of love, jealousy, and longing with a directness and psychological precision that has never lost its force; her great fragment describing the body's collapse at the sight of a beloved was famously recast in Latin by Catullus.

Of her life little is securely known. She belonged to an aristocratic family of Mytilene, had brothers and a daughter, and seems to have been caught up in the political feuds of her city, spending a period of exile in Sicily. The romantic tradition that she leapt to her death from a cliff for love of a ferryman named Phaon is a later legend, not history.

Enough of her verse survived, and was so admired, that her name became a byword for lyric genius; from her island and the women of her circle comes the very word by which love between women is still often described.
