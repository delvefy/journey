Leopold II, Holy Roman Emperor

Enlightened reformer and emperor, 1747–1792

Leopold II was the ablest of the reforming Habsburgs, a ruler whose quiet competence repaired much of what his impetuous elder brother had unsettled. Born in Vienna in 1747, a son of Maria Theresa and Francis I, he was sent while still young to govern Tuscany as its grand duke, and there he ruled for a quarter of a century as a model of enlightened statecraft.

In Tuscany he reordered the laws, the finances, and the administration, and in 1786 he made his small state the first in the world to abolish the death penalty and judicial torture—a measure for which he is still remembered.

When his brother Joseph II died in 1790, leaving the Habsburg lands seething with revolt against his rushed reforms, Leopold succeeded him as Holy Roman Emperor. With patience and political skill he calmed the rebellious Netherlands and Hungary, conceded what had to be conceded, and steadied the monarchy in a dangerous hour.

That hour was the French Revolution, and his sister Marie Antoinette was its prisoner-queen. Anxious for her safety, Leopold joined with Prussia in 1791 to issue the Declaration of Pillnitz, warning the revolutionaries against harming the royal family; the warning only inflamed Paris and helped bring on the long war. Leopold died suddenly in 1792, after barely two years on the throne, and was succeeded by his son, the last man to wear the imperial crown, Francis II.

The road continues to Francis II, Holy Roman Emperor.
