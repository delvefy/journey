Zeno of Elea

Eleatic philosopher, c. 490–430 BCE

Zeno of Elea was the favourite pupil and devoted defender of Parmenides, and one of the most original minds of early Greek philosophy. Aristotle credited him with the invention of dialectic—the art of reasoning by drawing out the contradictions hidden in an opponent's position.

His teacher had argued that all reality is one and unchanging, and that motion and plurality are illusions. To defend this startling view, Zeno turned the tables on common sense with a series of brilliant paradoxes. If a thing can be divided endlessly, he argued, a runner could never cross a racecourse, for he must first cover half the distance, then half of what remains, and so on without end. Swift Achilles could never overtake a tortoise given a head start, since each time he reached its last position it had crept a little farther on. And an arrow in flight, occupying at each instant a space exactly its own size, must at every instant be at rest—so how can it ever move?

These puzzles were no idle tricks: they exposed deep difficulties in the ideas of space, time, motion, and infinity, and they have provoked mathematicians and philosophers ever since. In Plato's dialogue the Parmenides, Zeno is shown travelling with his master to Athens and debating with the young Socrates.

Tradition holds that Zeno died a hero's death, implicated in a conspiracy against a tyrant and enduring torture without betraying his companions. Whatever the truth of the tale, his paradoxes secured him a permanent place in the history of thought.
