Euclid

Greek mathematician, “father of geometry,” active c. 300 BC

Almost nothing is known of Euclid’s life. He worked at Alexandria, the new Greek city in Egypt that under the first Ptolemy had become the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean, and there he taught and gathered the mathematical knowledge of his predecessors. A famous anecdote has him tell King Ptolemy, who wanted an easier path to learning, that “there is no royal road to geometry.” Whether true or not, the story captures his reputation as the great systematizer of an entire science.

His monument is the Elements, thirteen books that organize geometry and number theory into a single deductive structure. Beginning from a handful of definitions, postulates, and self-evident axioms, Euclid derived hundreds of propositions in strict logical order, each built on those proven before. The work gathers and proves the mathematics of earlier Greeks, including the theorem of Pythagoras and the demonstration that the prime numbers are infinite, and presents it with a rigor that became the model of mathematical reasoning itself.

No secular book has been more influential or longer in use. For more than two thousand years the Elements served as the standard geometry textbook, studied by schoolchildren and admired by philosophers and scientists who took its method as the very pattern of certain knowledge. From Archimedes in antiquity to Newton and beyond, those who built the edifice of mathematics and physics did so on the foundation Euclid laid, and “Euclidean” geometry remains a household phrase to this day.
