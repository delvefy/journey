Alexander II of Russia

1818–1881, Emperor of Russia, “the Tsar Liberator”

Alexander Nikolaevich Romanov was born in the Kremlin in April 1818, the eldest son of the future Nicholas I. His tutor was the poet Vasily Zhukovsky, who imparted a sentimental humanitarianism that distinguished him from his autocratic father. He travelled through European Russia and Siberia in 1837–38, the first heir apparent to do so, and after marrying Princess Marie of Hesse-Darmstadt in 1841 settled into the routine of court and committee work that prepared him for the throne.

He inherited the empire in March 1855 amid the disasters of the Crimean War. Within a year he had ended the war on terms imposed by Britain, France and the Austrians at Paris, and turned to the structural reform Nicholas I had refused. The Edict of Emancipation of 19 February 1861 freed twenty-three million serfs from personal bondage and gave them a portion of the land, charged to the peasants and the commune in long redemption payments. Universal trial by jury was introduced in 1864, elected provincial assemblies (the zemstvos) the same year, near-universal military conscription in 1874.

The reforms unsettled both reaction and revolution. Ivan Turgenev's Fathers and Sons captured the generational rupture; Fyodor Dostoevsky was amnestied from Siberian exile in his reign; Leo Tolstoy wrote of the emancipated countryside in War and Peace and Anna Karenina; Mikhail Bakunin assailed the autocracy from Geneva and Lyon. The Polish rising of 1863 hardened the tsar against further concessions, the conquest of Turkestan and the Caucasus continued, and the sale of Alaska to the United States in 1867 closed Russia's northeastern claim. Abroad he was a member of Otto von Bismarck's Three Emperors' League with the Habsburgs.

The Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 ended at the Congress of Berlin, where Disraeli and Bismarck stripped most of his San Stefano gains. He survived five attempts on his life; on 1 March 1881, returning along the Catherine Canal in St Petersburg from a review of troops, he was killed by a second bomb thrown by Ignacy Hryniewiecki of the People's Will. He was succeeded by his son Alexander III, who reversed the political direction of the reign.
