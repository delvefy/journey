Richard the Lionheart

King of England, 1157–1199

Richard I, called Cœur de Lion, was born in 1157, the favourite son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine. Raised to rule his mother’s great duchy of Aquitaine, he proved a formidable and ruthless soldier while still a youth, and in the end turned his arms against his own father, helping to hound the old king to his death in 1189. He inherited an empire stretching from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees, yet of his ten-year reign he spent barely six months in England, which he regarded chiefly as a source of money.

That money went to the Third Crusade. Taking the cross alongside Philip II Augustus of France, Richard sailed east in 1190, seized Cyprus on the way, and helped force the surrender of Acre. His victory at Arsuf in 1191 and his hard marches down the coast restored the crusader hold on the Palestinian shore, but Jerusalem itself eluded him. Unable to take the holy city, he concluded a truce with his great adversary Saladin that guaranteed Christian pilgrims safe access—a settlement between two commanders who became, in legend, the very models of chivalry.

The journey home undid him. Shipwrecked and travelling in disguise, he was seized by Duke Leopold of Austria and handed to the Holy Roman Emperor, who released him in 1194 only for a king’s ransom squeezed from England. His last years were spent fighting Philip to recover the ground lost in his absence, and raising the mighty fortress of Château Gaillard above the Seine. In 1199 a crossbow bolt at the petty siege of Châlus festered and killed him. He left no children, and the crown passed to his brother John.

The road continues to Saladin.
