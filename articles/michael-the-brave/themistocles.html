Themistocles

Athenian statesman and general, c. 524–459 BCE

Themistocles was the Athenian statesman and general who, more than any other man, made Athens a sea power and engineered the Greek victory over Persia at Salamis. Born around 524 BCE to a modest family, he rose through the young democracy by sheer intelligence and ambition. Foreseeing that the city's future lay on the water, he persuaded his fellow citizens to spend a rich new vein of silver from the mines at Laurion on a great fleet of triremes rather than divide it among themselves.

That fleet proved decisive when Xerxes invaded in 480 BCE. After the fall of Thermopylae, Themistocles oversaw the evacuation of Athens and, by a mixture of argument and cunning—including a false message that lured the Persian navy into the straits—forced a battle at Salamis in which the Greek ships destroyed the larger Persian fleet. The victory broke the momentum of the invasion.

Honored across Greece in the hour of triumph, Themistocles soon fell from favor at home. He rebuilt and fortified Athens over Spartan objections, but his arrogance and many rivalries told against him, and he was ostracized around 471 BCE. Hounded by his enemies, he made his way at last to the court of his old foe's son, the Persian king Artaxerxes I, who received him generously and made him governor of Magnesia in Asia Minor.

He died there around 459 BCE. Thucydides judged him a man of extraordinary natural genius, able to improvise the wisest course in any sudden crisis—the true founder of the Athenian naval empire that would soon dominate the Aegean.
