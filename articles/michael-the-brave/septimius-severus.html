Septimius Severus

Roman emperor, founder of the Severan dynasty, 145–211 AD

Septimius Severus was born at Leptis Magna on the African coast, of mixed Punic and Italian descent, and rose through the Senate to govern the great frontier province of Pannonia. When the murder of Commodus and then of Pertinax threw the empire into the Year of the Five Emperors, his Danube legions proclaimed him, and he marched on Rome to seize the throne in 193. He spent the following years crushing his rivals in civil war, defeating Pescennius Niger in the east and Clodius Albinus in the west at the great battle of Lugdunum.

To buttress his legitimacy he attached himself to the old ruling house, having himself proclaimed the posthumous son of Marcus Aurelius and deifying even the despised Commodus. But his real power rested on the army. He raised the soldiers' pay, opened their ranks to advancement, and openly favoured them above all others; his reported deathbed counsel to his sons was to enrich the troops and scorn everyone else. Under him the empire became frankly a military monarchy.

An energetic campaigner, he warred successfully against Parthia, sacking its capital, and late in life carried the war into northern Britain, repairing Hadrian's Wall and pressing into Caledonia. With his formidable Syrian wife Julia Domna he fathered the sons Caracalla and Geta. He died at York in 211, leaving the empire to the two brothers together—an arrangement that collapsed almost at once into murder. His dynasty, founded on the sword, would rule for decades more.
