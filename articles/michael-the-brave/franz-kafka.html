Franz Kafka

German-language novelist of Prague, 1883–1924

Franz Kafka was born on 3 July 1883 in the Old Town of Prague, the eldest surviving child of Hermann and Julie Kafka, German-speaking Jews of the merchant class. His father was a prosperous and physically imposing wholesaler of fancy goods, and the unequal struggle with him, set down at thirty-six in a hundred-page Letter to His Father, was one of the recurring keys of Kafka’s interior life. He took a law degree at the Charles University in 1906, and from 1908 until illness retired him in 1922 worked as a senior clerk at the Workers’ Accident Insurance Institute for the Kingdom of Bohemia, drafting risk reports by day and writing by night.

From his student years he shared a close friendship with Max Brod, who would do more than anyone to make him a writer. In life he published little — the story collections Betrachtung, Ein Landarzt, and Ein Hungerkünstler, the novella Die Verwandlung in which Gregor Samsa wakes as a monstrous insect, the sketches In der Strafkolonie and Das Urteil. He twice became engaged to Felice Bauer and twice broke it off; later attachments to Julie Wohryzek, Milena Jesenská, and finally Dora Diamant followed the same pattern of approach and recoil.

Tuberculosis, diagnosed in 1917, ate up his last seven years. He died on 3 June 1924 in a sanatorium at Kierling, near Vienna, of laryngeal disease that left him unable to swallow. His instructions to Brod were to burn all unpublished manuscripts; Brod, his executor, deliberately disobeyed and brought out in turn Der Prozess (1925), Das Schloss (1926) and Der Verschollene. Those three unfinished novels, written without thought of publication for a corner-of-Europe readership, made him one of the central figures of twentieth-century literature.
