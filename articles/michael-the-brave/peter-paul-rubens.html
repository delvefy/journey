Peter Paul Rubens

Flemish Baroque painter and diplomat, 1577–1640

Rubens was the towering figure of Northern European Baroque, a painter of boundless energy whose workshop in Antwerp turned out altarpieces, mythologies, hunting scenes, and portraits in astonishing abundance. He spent eight formative years in Italy in the service of the Duke of Mantua, steeping himself in classical antiquity and the great masters—above all the colour of Titian, whom he copied throughout his life, as well as the dramatic new realism of Caravaggio. He returned home transformed into the most sought-after artist on the Continent.

His mature style fused muscular movement, warm and sensuous flesh, and a dazzling command of colour into compositions of unprecedented dynamism. Monarchs competed for his services: he painted a vast allegorical cycle glorifying Marie de’ Medici for her Paris palace and the great ceiling of the Banqueting House in London for Charles I.

Uniquely among artists, Rubens was also a trusted statesman. Learned, multilingual, and urbane, he undertook delicate diplomatic missions to help negotiate peace between Spain and England, and was knighted by both Charles I and Philip IV of Spain, at whose court he befriended the young Velázquez. He trained the gifted Anthony van Dyck and presided over an enterprise that shaped European art for a century. Wealthy, happy, and endlessly productive, he died at Antwerp revered across the whole of Europe.
