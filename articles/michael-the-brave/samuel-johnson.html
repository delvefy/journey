Samuel Johnson

English writer, critic, and lexicographer, 1709–1784

Born in Lichfield to a struggling bookseller, Johnson overcame poverty, illness, and the scars of scrofula to become the dominant literary figure of eighteenth-century England. Forced to leave Oxford without a degree for want of money, he came to London with his former pupil, the actor David Garrick, and made a precarious living by his pen, producing periodical essays, the moralising poem “The Vanity of Human Wishes,” and the philosophical tale Rasselas.

His monument was A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), which he compiled over years with only a few assistants—a labour that elsewhere occupied whole academies. Authoritative, idiosyncratic, and enriched with literary quotations, it remained the standard English dictionary for a century and won him a royal pension and lasting renown. He also produced a landmark edition of Shakespeare and the critical Lives of the Poets.

Above all Johnson was the great talker of his age. With the painter Joshua Reynolds he founded the Literary Club, whose members included Edmund Burke, Edward Gibbon, and Oliver Goldsmith. Blunt, devout, melancholic, and fiercely witty, he toured the Scottish Highlands and dominated every company he entered. His conversation, opinions, and oddities were recorded in extraordinary detail by his young Scottish friend James Boswell, whose biography would make Johnson one of the most vividly known of all human beings.
