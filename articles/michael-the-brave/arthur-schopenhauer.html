Arthur Schopenhauer

German philosopher of pessimism, 1788–1860

Schopenhauer was born in Danzig to a prosperous merchant family and the novelist Johanna Schopenhauer. Groomed for trade, he turned to philosophy after his father’s death freed him to study, and he completed his great work while still in his twenties. He took as his starting point the philosophy of Kant, accepting that the world as we know it is mere appearance, or "representation," shaped by the mind’s own forms.

In The World as Will and Representation (1818) he argued that behind all appearances lies a single, blind, ceaseless striving he called the Will. Because the Will can never be satisfied, existence is essentially suffering; temporary release comes only through disinterested aesthetic contemplation, compassion, and ultimately the ascetic denial of the will to live. He drew openly on Plato’s theory of Ideas and on Hindu and Buddhist scriptures, becoming one of the first major Western thinkers to take Indian philosophy seriously.

His career was marked by bitterness and obscurity. Convinced of his own genius, he scheduled his Berlin lectures at the same hour as the celebrated Hegel, whom he despised, and lectured to an empty room. For decades his books went unread. Recognition came only late in life, with the essays of Parerga and Paralipomena (1851). Yet his influence proved immense: his vision of the irrational will shaped Nietzsche, Wagner, and the young Freud, and his uncompromising pessimism left its mark on a long line of artists and thinkers.
