Hypatia

Alexandrian philosopher and mathematician, c.360–415

Hypatia was born in Alexandria in the later fourth century, the daughter of Theon, a mathematician and astronomer of the city’s famous Museum who edited the works of Euclid and Ptolemy. Surpassing her father, she became the leading teacher of Neoplatonic philosophy in Alexandria, lecturing on Plato and Aristotle to students drawn from across the Mediterranean and from both pagan and Christian families. She wrote learned commentaries — on the Arithmetica of Diophantus, on the conics of Apollonius, and on the astronomical tables associated with Ptolemy’s Almagest — though these survive only in fragments or not at all.

Contemporaries praised her wisdom, eloquence and personal dignity, and she advised the leading men of the city; her devoted pupil Synesius, later a bishop, left affectionate letters that are among our best evidence for her. She moved freely in public life at a time when Alexandria was riven by violent conflict between its Christian, Jewish and pagan communities.

That conflict destroyed her. Caught in a bitter struggle for power between Orestes, the imperial prefect and her friend, and Cyril, the formidable patriarch of the city, she was seized in March 415 by a mob of Cyril’s partisans, dragged into a church, and brutally killed, her body torn apart and burned. Pagan and Christian sources alike recorded the horror. In later ages her death became a potent symbol — of the murder of reason by fanaticism, and of the twilight of the classical world — and her memory was revived by Enlightenment writers, novelists and filmmakers.
