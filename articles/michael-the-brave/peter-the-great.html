Peter the Great

Tsar and Emperor of Russia, 1672–1725

Peter I, called the Great, dragged Russia out of medieval isolation and made it a European power. Born in 1672, he became co-tsar as a boy under the regency of his half-sister Sophia, whom he pushed aside in 1689 to rule in his own right. Towering, restless, and insatiably curious, he was unlike any tsar before him.

His Grand Embassy of 1697–98 took him incognito across Europe, where he laboured with his own hands in the shipyards of the Dutch Republic and England, studied Western arts and institutions, and recruited engineers, officers, and craftsmen. Returning home, he imposed westernisation by decree: nobles were made to shave their beards and dress in European fashion, the calendar was reformed, a standing army was raised, a navy built from nothing, the government reorganised, and the Orthodox Church brought under state control.

The defining struggle of his reign was the Great Northern War against Sweden. Crushingly beaten at Narva in 1700 by the young Charles XII, Peter rebuilt his forces and won the decisive battle of Poltava in 1709, shattering Swedish supremacy and securing the Baltic coast. In 1721 he took the title of Emperor.

On marshland wrested from Sweden he founded a new capital, Saint Petersburg, his "window on Europe," raised at enormous cost in lives. He could be terrible—he had his own son Alexei tortured to death for opposing him—yet he founded the Academy of Sciences with advice from Leibniz, and left a transformed empire to his widow, who reigned as Catherine I. Voltaire would later write his history.
