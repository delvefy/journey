Trajan

Roman emperor, 53–117 AD

Trajan was born at Italica in Roman Spain, the son of a distinguished general, and made his own name as a soldier serving the Flavian emperors. He was governing Upper Germany when the aged and childless Nerva, raised to power after the murder of Domitian and pressed hard by the army, adopted the popular commander as his son and heir. On Nerva's death in 98 Trajan became emperor—the first to come from the provinces—and the Senate would in time hail him as Optimus Princeps, the best of rulers.

He was above all a conqueror. In two hard campaigns he crushed the Dacian king Decebalus and annexed the gold-rich lands beyond the Danube, victories carved in spiralling relief upon the great column that still bears his name in Rome. Late in his reign he marched east against Parthia, taking Armenia and Mesopotamia and reaching the Persian Gulf, so that under him the Roman Empire attained its greatest extent ever.

At home he matched his wars with works of peace, raising a magnificent new forum, markets, and public buildings, improving the harbour of Rome, and funding the alimenta, a scheme to support poor children. His correspondence with Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, survives as a rare window into the day-to-day cares of imperial government, including the famous exchange on how Christians should be treated. Trajan died in 117 on his way back from the east, leaving the throne to his kinsman Hadrian, and was remembered ever after as the model of the just and able emperor.
