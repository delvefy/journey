Martial

Roman poet, c. 38–c. 104 CE

Marcus Valerius Martialis, known in English as Martial, was the master of the Latin epigram and the sharpest observer of everyday life in imperial Rome. Born around 38 CE at Bilbilis in Roman Spain, he came to the capital in his twenties and lived for some thirty-five years as a client poet, dependent on the favours of richer men and viewing the great city from below.

From that vantage he produced more than fifteen hundred short poems, gathered into twelve books of Epigrams. Witty, obscene, tender, and merciless by turns, they hold up a mirror to every Roman type—the legacy-hunter, the social climber, the pretentious host, the faithless friend. Taking the earlier poet Catullus as his model, Martial perfected the pointed epigram that closes with a sting in its tail, fixing the form for all the European literature that followed.

He courted the powerful without shame. His first book celebrated the spectacles staged by Titus to open the Colosseum, and he flattered the emperor Domitian as master and god in verse after verse. Among his friends he counted the satirist Juvenal and the letter-writer Pliny the Younger, who once paid his passage and later mourned his passing.

When Domitian was murdered in 96, Martial's brand of imperial flattery fell out of fashion. Weary of the struggle for patronage, he returned to Spain around 98, where a wealthy admirer gave him a small estate, and there he died a few years later. His epigrams have never ceased to be read, imitated, and quoted.
