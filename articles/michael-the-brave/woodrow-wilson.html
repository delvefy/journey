Woodrow Wilson

28th President of the United States, 1856–1924

The son of a Presbyterian minister, raised in the defeated South, Wilson became a distinguished political scientist and president of Princeton University before a meteoric turn to politics carried him to the governorship of New Jersey and, in 1912, to the White House when the split between Theodore Roosevelt and Taft handed him the presidency. His “New Freedom” programme created the Federal Reserve, strengthened antitrust law, and established the Federal Trade Commission.

Re-elected in 1916 on the slogan that he had kept the country out of war, Wilson nonetheless led the United States into the First World War the following year after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine attacks. He framed the struggle in idealistic terms, setting out his Fourteen Points and a vision of a world “made safe for democracy.” At the Paris Peace Conference he was hailed by European crowds as a prophet of peace and championed a League of Nations to prevent future wars, winning the Nobel Peace Prize.

But the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, and the United States never joined his League. Campaigning to save it, Wilson suffered a severe stroke in 1919 that left him incapacitated, his condition concealed while his wife managed his affairs. His record on race was reactionary, his idealism unfulfilled. So compelling a psychological puzzle did he present that Sigmund Freud later co-authored a study of his character. He died in 1924.
