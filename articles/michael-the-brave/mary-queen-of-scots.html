Mary, Queen of Scots

Queen of Scotland, 1542–1587

Mary Stuart was born on 8 December 1542 at Linlithgow Palace, the only surviving child of James V of Scotland and Mary of Guise. Six days later her father died, defeated and broken-hearted after the rout of Solway Moss, and the infant became queen. Henry VIII of England tried to seize the match for his son Edward; the Scots refused, sent the five-year-old child to France in 1548, and betrothed her instead to the Dauphin. She was raised at the court of Henri II and Catherine de’ Medici, fluent in French, Latin and music, and in April 1558 married the fragile Francis. He became king fifteen months later and died in December 1560, leaving her a widowed queen consort at eighteen.

In August 1561 she returned to Edinburgh, a Catholic monarch in a country that had just adopted the Reformation. She kept her own Mass, listened politely to the abrasive sermons of John Knox, and pursued a moderate policy. In July 1565 she married her Catholic cousin Henry, Lord Darnley. The marriage soured within months. Darnley conspired in the murder of her secretary David Rizzio in front of her in March 1566; their son James, the future James VI and I, was born that June. In February 1567 Darnley himself was strangled and his lodging at Kirk o’Field blown up. Three months later, scandalously, Mary married the chief suspect, the Earl of Bothwell.

The Scottish nobility revolted. Defeated at Carberry Hill, she was forced to abdicate, escaped, and after Langside fled into England in May 1568, hoping for asylum from her cousin Elizabeth I. Instead she spent nineteen years in confinement, a focus for every Catholic conspiracy against the English throne. The Babington Plot of 1586 gave her enemies the letters they needed. She was tried at Fotheringhay, condemned, and beheaded on 8 February 1587, declaring her innocence to the last.
