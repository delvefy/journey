Galen

Greek physician of the Roman Empire, 129–c. 216 AD

Galen was born at Pergamon in Asia Minor, the son of a prosperous architect who gave him a broad education in philosophy before he turned to medicine. He studied at the great centres of the Greek world, including Alexandria, and gained his first hard experience as physician to the gladiators of his home city, treating their terrible wounds and learning much of the body in the process. Ambition drew him to Rome, where his skill and his genius for self-promotion soon made him the most celebrated doctor of the age.

He rose to attend the imperial court, serving as physician to the emperor Marcus Aurelius and afterward to his son Commodus. Around his practice he built an enormous body of writing—hundreds of treatises on anatomy, physiology, diagnosis, drugs, and philosophy—that gathered up the whole inheritance of Greek medicine. He revered Hippocrates above all and drew deeply on Aristotle, fashioning from them a grand, unified system of how the body works.

Forbidden to dissect human corpses, Galen based his anatomy on apes and pigs, and some of his conclusions—about the movement of the blood and the workings of the heart—were mistaken. Yet such was the authority of his vast and confident corpus that his teachings dominated medicine in both the Christian and Islamic worlds for some thirteen centuries. Only in the Renaissance, when Vesalius corrected his anatomy and Harvey overturned his account of the blood, was the long reign of Galen finally broken.

The road continues to Marcus Aurelius.
