Anthony van Dyck

Flemish Baroque painter, 1599–1641

A prodigy from Antwerp, Van Dyck was painting masterful works in his teens and soon became the chief assistant in the vast studio of Peter Paul Rubens, the dominant artist of the age. Having absorbed his master’s power, he set out to refine it into something of his own. Years in Italy, where he studied above all the great Venetian Titian and painted the haughty aristocracy of Genoa, taught him a new elegance of pose, colour, and shimmering fabric.

His destiny lay in England. In 1632 Charles I made him principal court painter and knighted him, and Van Dyck repaid the king with the most influential royal images ever made in Britain. His portraits—Charles on horseback, the king in three views, the doomed Stuart court in silks and easy grandeur—fixed forever the way that tragic monarch is remembered, lending an air of melancholy nobility to a reign that would end on the scaffold.

Van Dyck transformed portraiture itself. His relaxed, flattering elegance, his beautiful hands and atmospheric settings, set a model that English painters would follow for two centuries, down to Gainsborough and Reynolds. Astonishingly prolific, courted across Europe, he died in London at only forty-two, worn out by overwork, having created in a brief life a template for aristocratic portraiture that endured long after him.

The road continues to Peter Paul Rubens.
