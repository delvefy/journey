Jacob van Ruisdael

Dutch landscape painter, c.1628–1682

Jacob van Ruisdael was born around 1628 in Haarlem into a family of painters; his father Isaack and his uncle Solomon van Ruysdael were established landscapists, and Jacob seems to have learned chiefly from them. He matriculated as a master of the Haarlem guild in 1648 and within a few years had begun the steady output of forest interiors, dune scenes, mills and seashores that would make him the central figure of the Dutch landscape school of the second half of the seventeenth century.

He travelled to Bentheim and the German-Dutch border in the early 1650s and brought back the great water-tumbled boulders and wooded slopes that recur in his pictures. Around 1656 he moved to Amsterdam, where he is recorded as having been admitted to the citizenry, and where he spent the rest of his life. He took the painter Meindert Hobbema as his pupil and probably his collaborator.

Ruisdael's landscape is sombre, weather-soaked, and morally pitched. The View of Haarlem from the Dunes at Overveen, his Wheat Fields, the Jewish Cemetery with its ruined tombs and broken trees, and the Mill at Wijk bij Duurstede with its single windmill against banked clouds, all carry an implicit reflection on time, ruin, and the small place of human life in northern weather. He worked in restrained greens, ochres and silvery greys, with great skies often filling more than half the canvas.

He died in Amsterdam in 1682, having taken a degree in medicine late in life and possibly practised as a surgeon. His pictures became, in the next century, the supreme model of naturalistic landscape: Goethe devoted a small essay, "Ruysdael as Poet," to him; the British school of Turner and Constable absorbed him; the Barbizon painters revived him for France. The pupil who carried his manner into the next generation and whose own avenues became as famous as his master's panoramas was Meindert Hobbema.
