Dwight D. Eisenhower

1890–1969, Supreme Allied Commander and thirty-fourth President of the United States

Dwight David Eisenhower was born in Denison, Texas, the third of seven sons of pacifist Pennsylvania Dutch parents, and grew up in Abilene, Kansas. He won an appointment to West Point in 1911 and graduated in the class of 1915 that became known as "the class the stars fell on." Through the First World War he trained tank crews in Pennsylvania; through the inter-war decades he served on the staffs of Fox Conner, John J. Pershing, Douglas MacArthur in the Philippines, and finally George C. Marshall in Washington, who in 1942 leapfrogged him over hundreds of seniors to command American forces in Europe.

He directed the landings in North Africa and Sicily, the surrender of Italy, and from January 1944 the assault on north-west Europe. On 6 June, after a one-day weather postponement, he gave the order to launch the Normandy invasion with the message "OK, let's go." Through the next eleven months he held an alliance of fiery subordinates — Patton, Montgomery, Bradley, de Gaulle's Free French — together by force of patience, and on 8 May 1945 he accepted the German surrender at Reims.

After the war he served as army chief of staff, president of Columbia University, and the first supreme commander of NATO. Drafted by Republican moderates in 1952 to head off Robert Taft, he beat Adlai Stevenson in a landslide and again in 1956. As president he ended the Korean War, refused to intervene in Indochina, sent federal troops to integrate Little Rock Central High School, signed the Interstate Highway Act, expanded social security, presided over a prosperous decade and warned the country in his farewell address against "the acquisition of unwarranted influence by the military-industrial complex." He retired to a farm at Gettysburg and died in Washington in March 1969.
