Blaise Pascal

French mathematician, physicist, and religious thinker, 1623–1662

Pascal was a prodigy who, while still a boy, mastered geometry and proved a theorem on conic sections that bears his name. To ease his father’s labours as a tax official he designed and built one of the first mechanical calculating machines, the Pascaline. In a celebrated exchange of letters with Pierre de Fermat over a gambling problem, he helped lay the foundations of the mathematical theory of probability, reasoning about expectation and chance in ways that still underpin the subject.

He was equally important as a physicist. Building on the vacuum experiments of his day, he investigated air pressure and demonstrated, by having a barometer carried up the Puy de Dôme, that the column of mercury fell with altitude, evidence that the atmosphere had weight and that a vacuum could exist. His work on the equilibrium of fluids gave the principle of the transmission of pressure that is named after him, and the modern unit of pressure honours his name.

A profound religious experience in 1654 turned him toward the austere Catholic movement of Jansenism centred on Port-Royal. In the Provincial Letters he attacked the moral reasoning of the Jesuits with devastating wit, shaping French prose in the process. The fragments gathered after his death as the Pensées include his famous wager on belief in God and his meditations on the misery and greatness of humankind. He died at thirty-nine, having reshaped mathematics, physics, and religious thought alike.
