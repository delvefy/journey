Leonhard Euler

Swiss mathematician and physicist, 1707–1783

Leonhard Euler was born in Basel in 1707, the son of a Reformed pastor, and was taught the rudiments of mathematics by his father and then on Saturday mornings by Johann Bernoulli, the foremost mathematician in Europe. He completed a master's degree in philosophy at thirteen and abandoned the theology course for which his father had intended him. In 1727 his friend Daniel Bernoulli secured him a post at the new Imperial Academy of Sciences in St Petersburg, founded by Peter the Great, where Euler began the work that would never stop.

His output is unmatched in the history of mathematics: more than 850 papers and books, filling 76 volumes of his Opera Omnia and still being edited. He introduced the modern notation of analysis—the letter e for the base of the natural logarithm, i for the imaginary unit, Σ for summation, f(x) for a function. He found the relation between π, e, i and 1 that Gauss's biographer would call the most beautiful in mathematics. He created the calculus of variations, the analytic theory of numbers, modern combinatorics with the Königsberg bridges, the classical theory of rigid body mechanics, much of fluid dynamics, and the foundations of analytic mechanics that would pass to Lagrange and Hamilton.

From 1741 to 1766 he served Frederick the Great at the Berlin Academy, where his quiet Calvinism made him an awkward courtier—Voltaire mocked him as the Swiss "Cyclops" for the eye he had lost over a microscope—and from 1766 he returned to Catherine the Great's Russia, where, going blind, he produced perhaps half his published work by dictation. His mental arithmetic and his memory grew sharper with the loss of sight.

He died at his desk in St Petersburg in 1783 of a stroke, while playing with his grandson. His Newtonian mechanics shaped d'Alembert and Lagrange; the German mathematician who in the next generation would inherit Euler's analytic empire and carry it through to a still more comprehensive synthesis was Carl Friedrich Gauss.
