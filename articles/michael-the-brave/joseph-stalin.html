Joseph Stalin

Leader of the Soviet Union, 1878–1953

Born Ioseb Jughashvili to a cobbler’s family in Gori, Georgia, the future Stalin trained briefly for the priesthood before turning to revolutionary Marxism. As a Bolshevik agitator he robbed banks for the party, suffered repeated arrests and Siberian exile, and after the 1917 revolution rose through the bureaucracy. Appointed General Secretary in 1922, he used that obscure post to accumulate power, and after Lenin’s death outmanoeuvred Trotsky and his other rivals to make himself absolute master of the Soviet state.

His rule remade the country through terror. Forced collectivization of the peasantry brought famine that killed millions, especially in Ukraine, while crash industrialization under the Five-Year Plans turned the USSR into a great industrial power. In the Great Terror of the late 1930s he purged the party, the army, and society at large through show trials, executions, and a vast network of labour camps.

After signing a pact with Hitler in 1939, Stalin was caught off guard by the German invasion of 1941, yet he steadied the nation and led it to a costly victory at Stalingrad and on to Berlin. As one of the Big Three he negotiated the postwar order with Churchill and Roosevelt, then fastened communist regimes on Eastern Europe and entered the Cold War. He died in 1953, his cult of personality intact; three years later Khrushchev would denounce his crimes. His career was the most thoroughgoing application of the doctrines of Karl Marx the world had yet seen.
