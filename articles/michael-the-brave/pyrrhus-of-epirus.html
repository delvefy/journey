Pyrrhus of Epirus

King of Epirus and general, 319–272 BCE

Pyrrhus was king of Epirus, a mountainous land on the edge of the Greek world, and a member of a royal house that claimed descent from Achilles. A second cousin of Alexander the Great, he idolised his famous kinsman and strove all his life to rival his conquests. As a young exile he found refuge at the court of Ptolemy I in Egypt, married into the royal family, and with Ptolemy's help regained his throne.

He was reckoned by the ancients among the very greatest generals—Hannibal himself is said to have ranked him second only to Alexander. In 280 BCE, invited by the Greek city of Tarentum, he crossed to Italy to fight the rising power of Rome. With his phalanx and his war elephants he beat the Romans at Heraclea and again at Asculum, but his losses were so ruinous that he reportedly cried, “One more such victory and I am undone”—giving us the phrase “Pyrrhic victory.”

Turning to Sicily, he drove the Carthaginians back almost to the sea, but his high-handed rule alienated the Greek cities, and he withdrew. Back in Italy he was checked by the Romans at Beneventum in 275 and at last abandoned the western venture.

Restless to the end, he returned to Greece and warred against Antigonus Gonatas for the throne of Macedon. In 272, fighting in the narrow streets of Argos, he was stunned by a roof tile flung by an old woman and cut down where he fell. Brilliant but mercurial, he won every battle and lost every war, the very type of the gifted soldier undone by his own restlessness.
