Alexander the Great

King of Macedon, 356–323 BC

Alexander was born in 356 BC at Pella, son of King Philip II of Macedon and Olympias, and was tutored in his youth by Aristotle. When Philip was assassinated in 336 BC, the twenty-year-old inherited a kingdom that dominated Greece and an army forged into the finest in the world. He answered the revolt that greeted his accession by razing Thebes, then turned the whole of that machine eastward against the Persian Empire.

What followed was one of the most astonishing campaigns in history. At the Granicus, at Issus, and at last decisively at Gaugamela in 331 BC, he shattered the armies of the Persian king Darius III; he took the great cities of Babylon, Susa and Persepolis, and pursued Darius until the king was murdered by his own followers. Egypt received Alexander as a liberator, and there he founded the greatest of the many cities to bear his name. Still he pressed on—through Bactria and into the Punjab, defeating the elephants of King Porus at the Hydaspes in 326 BC—until his exhausted soldiers refused to march further into India and forced him to turn back.

He died at Babylon in 323 BC, not yet thirty-three, struck down by fever after years of wounds and hard living, his empire stretching from the Adriatic to the Indus. He left no able heir, and his generals carved the conquests into the warring kingdoms of the Hellenistic age, yet they carried Greek language, art and thought across the whole of that vast world. His legend outran even his deeds: retold from Iceland to India, he became the measure of earthly ambition, the conqueror against whom every later one—not least an ambitious young Roman who wept that he had achieved so little by comparison—would measure himself.
