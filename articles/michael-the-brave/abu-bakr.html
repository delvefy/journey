Abu Bakr

First caliph of Islam, c.573–634

Abu Bakr, born about 573 into the Quraysh of Mecca, was a respected merchant and, by the Sunni account, the first grown man to follow Muhammad, whose dearest friend and steadfast supporter he became. He was honoured with the name as-Siddiq, the truthful, and his bond with the Prophet was sealed when his daughter Aisha became Muhammad’s wife. When the two fled Mecca for Medina in 622, it was Abu Bakr who shared the Prophet’s hiding place in the cave, and in Muhammad’s final illness it was he who led the prayers.

On the Prophet’s death in 632 the community gathered and chose Abu Bakr as the first caliph, the successor charged with holding it together — though the passing over of Ali sowed a lasting seed of division. The crisis was immediate: many Arabian tribes, bound only to Muhammad in person, fell away into apostasy and revolt.

In the brief two years of his rule Abu Bakr met this danger head-on. Through the Ridda Wars, waged above all by his brilliant general Khalid ibn al-Walid, he subdued the rebels and welded Arabia back into a single Muslim polity, then launched the first campaigns against Sasanian Iraq and Byzantine Syria that his successors would carry to triumph. He also set in motion the gathering of the scattered revelations into a single text. Before his death in 634 he named Umar to follow him, and he was laid to rest beside the Prophet, revered ever after as the first of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs.
