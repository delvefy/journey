John Brown

American abolitionist insurrectionist, 1800–1859

John Brown was born on 9 May 1800 in Torrington, Connecticut, and raised in the Ohio frontier in a household of fierce Calvinist piety and equally fierce hatred of slavery. He drifted through a long succession of failed trades — tanner, farmer, wool merchant — fathering twenty children, while his conviction hardened that slavery was a sin that could be purged only by the sword, and that God had appointed him its instrument.

That conviction turned to violence in Kansas, where the question of slavery had erupted into open warfare between settlers. In 1856, in reprisal for an attack on the town of Lawrence, Brown and his sons dragged five pro-slavery men from their cabins along Pottawatomie Creek and hacked them to death. He then conceived a far grander design: to seize a mountain stronghold and arm the enslaved for a general uprising. Funded secretly by a circle of Northern backers, he sought in vain to enlist Frederick Douglass, who judged the scheme suicidal, and the redoubtable Harriet Tubman.

On the night of 16 October 1859 Brown led twenty-one men to capture the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. The slaves did not rise; townsmen and militia pinned the raiders down, and a detachment of U.S. Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed the engine house, killing several and taking Brown wounded. Tried for treason against Virginia, he refused to repent, telling the court he was content to die for God’s despised poor, and was hanged on 2 December 1859. North and South alike read the raid as a portent; within eighteen months the Civil War he had tried to ignite was under way, and Union soldiers marched singing that his soul went marching on.
