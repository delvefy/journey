Gary Cooper

American actor, 1901–1961.

Frank James Cooper was born in Helena, Montana, the second son of an English-born immigrant who became a Montana Supreme Court justice. He spent boyhood summers on his father's cattle ranch outside Helena, riding fence and learning the laconic Western manner he would later carry, almost unchanged, into the cinema. After failed attempts at editorial cartooning in Chicago and Los Angeles, he answered a casting call as a stunt rider and bit-player extra in 1925, was renamed "Gary" by his agent Nan Collins, and within two years had won the title part of The Virginian (1929), the first major sound Western.

Across the next thirty years he made nearly a hundred pictures and embodied, more than any other actor of his generation, an American ideal of plain-spoken male reticence. He won the Academy Award for Best Actor twice — for the title role in Howard Hawks's Sergeant York (1941), as the Tennessee mountaineer who became a First World War hero, and for Stanley Kramer's High Noon (1952), as the sheriff abandoned by his town. His range across that long career was wider than the laconic-Westerner image suggests: the romantic lead opposite Marlene Dietrich in Morocco, the small-town poet of Frank Capra's Mr. Deeds Goes to Town, the doomed Yankee first baseman of The Pride of the Yankees (1942), the war correspondent in his close friend Ernest Hemingway's For Whom the Bell Tolls.

His private life included a long marriage to Veronica Balfe and a string of affairs — with Lupe Vélez, Marlene Dietrich, Ingrid Bergman, and most disruptively with Patricia Neal during the filming of The Fountainhead. He testified as a friendly witness before the House Un-American Activities Committee in 1947 in vague, courteous terms; politically he was a Hollywood Republican who supported Eisenhower. The cancer that killed him in May 1961, at sixty, had been diagnosed in 1960; James Stewart accepted his honorary Academy Award for him a month before his death.
