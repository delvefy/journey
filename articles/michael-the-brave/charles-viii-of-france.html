Charles VIII of France

King of France, opener of the Italian Wars, 1470–1498

Charles was born at the château of Amboise on 30 June 1470, the only surviving son of Louis XI and Charlotte of Savoy. He came to the throne at thirteen, on his father’s death in August 1483, with his elder sister Anne of Beaujeu and her husband Pierre de Bourbon ruling as regents. The eight-year regency was as competent as Louis XI’s reign: it broke the Mad War of the Burgundian and Orléans factions on the field of Saint-Aubin-du-Cormier in July 1488, brought the duchy of Brittany under French dependence by marrying its young heiress Anne to Charles in December 1491, and tied up the loose treaties with Henry VII of England (Étaples, 1492) and with Ferdinand and Isabella (Barcelona, 1493) at the cost of returning Cerdagne and Roussillon to Aragon.

The price of the international peace was to be paid in Italy. Through his grandmother Marie of Anjou, Charles claimed the Angevin succession to the kingdom of Naples, lost to Aragon two generations before. Lodovico Sforza, the regent of Milan, encouraged the project as a counterweight to the Aragonese-Florentine axis that threatened him. In August 1494 Charles crossed the Mont-Genèvre with thirty thousand troops, the heaviest park of artillery yet seen in Italy, and a Swiss-pikeman vanguard. Florence opened its gates to him in November; Piero de’ Medici was sent into exile; Rome trembled and let him pass; on 22 February 1495 he was crowned king in Naples cathedral, having walked through Italy, as the diarist Commynes wrote, “with chalk in hand” to mark his lodgings.

The fall was as quick. Venice, Milan, Pope Alexander VI, the Emperor Maximilian, and Ferdinand of Aragon formed the Holy League of Venice in March 1495 to expel him. He fought his way back north and was caught at Fornovo on the Taro on 6 July 1495 by the Mantuan Francesco Gonzaga; the French held the field and reached France, but the baggage was lost and the kingdom of Naples lost with it. Charles spent his last years preparing a new expedition that never sailed. On 7 April 1498, hurrying through a low doorway at Amboise to see a game of jeu de paume, he struck his head on the lintel, fell, and died of a brain haemorrhage that evening, leaving no surviving children. The crown passed to his cousin the Duke of Orléans as Louis XII.
