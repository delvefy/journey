Paul Gauguin

French Post-Impressionist painter, 1848–1903

Paul Gauguin was born in Paris in 1848 and spent his earliest years in Peru, a childhood among colour and tropic light that he never afterwards stopped invoking. He served in the merchant marine and the French navy, settled into a comfortable career as a Paris stockbroker, married a Danish wife, and collected Impressionist paintings. In 1882, when the bourse collapsed, he abandoned office and family and resolved to live by art alone.

Camille Pissarro had been his first teacher; through him Gauguin entered the Impressionist exhibitions and learned the discipline of plein-air painting. But he soon broke with Impressionism's quiet observation in favour of flat zones of unmodulated colour and heavy black outline, a manner he called Synthetism and worked out with the young Émile Bernard at Pont-Aven in Brittany. The Vision after the Sermon of 1888, with its red field and angel-wrestling Jacob, was the manifesto picture of that style.

The same year he travelled south to live with Vincent van Gogh at Arles, in a brief and combustible collaboration that ended with Van Gogh's breakdown. In 1891 Gauguin sailed for Tahiti in search of an unspoiled paradise, and apart from one return to France he passed the rest of his life in French Polynesia and the Marquesas, painting Tahitian women with Edenic dignity and inventing a personal mythology around them. He died on Hiva Oa in 1903, poor, ill, and in conflict with the colonial authorities.

His refusal of European subject matter and his flat decorative colour made him a founding figure for Picasso, Matisse and the Fauves, and reset the boundaries of what modern art was allowed to look like. Among the older Impressionists who had recognised and patronised his early work was Edgar Degas.
