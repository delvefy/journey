Odaenathus

Lord of Palmyra and King of Kings, c. 220–267 AD

Odaenathus was a nobleman of Palmyra, the great caravan city of the Syrian desert, who had risen to Roman senatorial rank. In the 260s the Roman East lay in ruins: the emperor Valerian had been captured by the Sasanian king Shapur I, and Persian armies had sacked Antioch and ravaged Syria. Into this vacuum stepped Odaenathus.

Rallying the forces of Palmyra together with the remnants of the Roman legions, he counterattacked the retreating Persians, defeated Shapur I, and recovered the lost provinces. Twice he carried the war deep into enemy territory, advancing to the walls of the Persian capital Ctesiphon. These were the first real Roman successes against Persia in a generation.

The emperor Gallienus, powerless to defend the East himself, had little choice but to recognise Odaenathus's authority, granting him sweeping commands over the entire East. Odaenathus, for his part, took the grand title “King of Kings,” ruling Syria and the eastern provinces as a virtually independent monarch while still professing loyalty to Rome.

At the height of his power, around 267, he was assassinated together with his eldest son, in circumstances that remain obscure—blamed variously on a disaffected kinsman or a court intrigue. His widow Zenobia seized the reins on behalf of their young son Vaballathus and went on to build the short-lived Palmyrene Empire that would defy Rome itself. Odaenathus had laid its foundations, restoring the East when the empire could not.
