Hermann Hesse

German-Swiss novelist and poet, 1877–1962

Born into a pious missionary family in the Black Forest town of Calw, Hesse rebelled against a planned career in the church, fleeing seminary and drifting through bookshops and breakdowns before finding his vocation as a writer. His early novel Peter Camenzind brought success, but it was a midlife crisis, eased by a course of Jungian analysis, that gave his work its enduring theme: the lonely individual’s search for self and meaning against the demands of society.

From that wellspring came the books that made him one of the most widely read authors of the century. Demian probed the awakening of a divided soul; Siddhartha retold the path to enlightenment in an Indian setting; Steppenwolf dissected the alienation of modern man; and his late masterpiece The Glass Bead Game imagined an austere intellectual utopia. A pacifist who left Germany for Switzerland during the First World War, he was bitterly attacked at home for refusing the fevers of nationalism.

In 1946 he received the Nobel Prize in Literature. Decades later his novels found a vast new readership among the young of the 1960s, who took Siddhartha and Steppenwolf as scriptures of the counterculture. He spent his last years in the Ticino, painting watercolours and tending his garden, and died there in 1962. Among his closest literary friends was his great contemporary Thomas Mann.
