Edward the Black Prince

Prince of Wales and Aquitaine, 1330–1376

Edward of Woodstock was born at the manor of Woodstock on 15 June 1330, the eldest surviving son of Edward III and Philippa of Hainault. He was created Earl of Chester at four and Duke of Cornwall — the first English dukedom — at six, and Prince of Wales at thirteen. His career began on the Crécy campaign of 1346. On 26 August, sixteen years old, he commanded the right of the English line; when his standard was hard-pressed his father refused him reinforcements with the famous words that the boy should win his spurs. He did. Two years later he was a founding knight of the Order of the Garter.

In 1355 he was given the lieutenancy of Gascony with a free hand. The chevauchée of 1355–56 wasted Languedoc; the chevauchée of 1356 ended on 19 September outside Poitiers, where his outnumbered army destroyed the French chivalry and captured King John II of France himself. Brought to London in 1357 with the king his prisoner, he was at the height of his fame. The Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 surrendered the captured king for ransom and an enlarged Aquitaine in full sovereignty. Edward was created Prince of Aquitaine in 1362, married his cousin Joan of Kent the following year, and held a brilliant court at Bordeaux.

In 1367 he intervened in Castile to restore the deposed Pedro I against Henry of Trastámara and the French commander Bertrand du Guesclin, winning a hard battle at Nájera on 3 April. The campaign broke his health: he came back with the dysentery that would not leave him. Heavy taxes to pay for the war drove his Gascon vassals into revolt, the French king Charles V resumed the war, and the prince's last campaign in 1370 ended with the brutal sack of Limoges. He returned to England chronically ill, surrendered Aquitaine in 1372, and died at Westminster on 8 June 1376, a year before his father. He was buried at Canterbury Cathedral, his effigy in armour above the tomb.
