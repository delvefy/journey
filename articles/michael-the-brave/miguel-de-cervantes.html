Miguel de Cervantes

Spanish novelist, author of Don Quixote, 1547–1616

Cervantes lived a life of hardship far removed from literary ease. As a young soldier he fought against the Ottomans at the great sea-battle of Lepanto in 1571, where a wound permanently maimed his left hand, earning him the proud nickname “the one-handed man of Lepanto.” Captured by Barbary corsairs on the voyage home, he spent five years as a slave in Algiers, attempting escape four times before he was ransomed. Back in Spain he scratched a living as a tax collector and commissary, was twice imprisoned over irregular accounts, and knew chronic poverty.

Out of this battered life came the book that founded the modern novel. Don Quixote, published in two parts in 1605 and 1615, follows an elderly gentleman who, his wits turned by reading too many tales of chivalry, sets out as a knight-errant with his earthy squire Sancho Panza, tilting at windmills he takes for giants. By turns hilarious and profoundly moving, it weighs idealism against reality with an irony and humanity unmatched before it, and became the most widely read book of the Western canon after the Bible.

Cervantes wrote much else—plays, the Exemplary Novels, pastoral and Byzantine romances—and chafed under the dazzling success of his rival Lope de Vega in the theatre. He died in Madrid in April 1616, within days of William Shakespeare. The Spanish language itself is honoured as “the tongue of Cervantes.”

The road continues to Lope de Vega.
