Jean le Rond d'Alembert

French mathematician and philosophe, 1717–1783

Jean le Rond d'Alembert was found as an infant in 1717 on the steps of the church of Saint-Jean-le-Rond in Paris, the illegitimate son of an aristocratic salon hostess and an artillery officer. His father provided secretly for his upbringing with a glazier's family while his mother refused to acknowledge him. He read law at the Collège des Quatre-Nations, then turned to mathematics on his own, and by twenty-four had been elected to the Académie des Sciences.

His 1743 Traité de dynamique reduced the laws of moving bodies to a clean principle—d'Alembert's principle—in which the forces acting on a system can be analysed by introducing a virtual "inertial" reaction. The treatise sat among Newton's and Euler's at the foundation of rational mechanics. He went on to write on fluid resistance, on the motion of strings (where he gave the wave equation its first partial differential form), on the precession of the equinoxes, on the three-body problem, on probability, on music acoustics.

At the same time he was Diderot's collaborator. As co-editor of the Encyclopédie he wrote the famous Discours préliminaire of 1751, a programmatic survey of human knowledge organised from sensation outward, and contributed most of the mathematical and scientific entries through the first seven volumes. When the censor's pressure became unbearable in 1758 he withdrew, leaving Diderot to finish the great work alone.

He was a fixture of Mme du Deffand's and Mme Geoffrin's salons, declined a flattering invitation from Frederick the Great to come to Berlin, took only a small post as secretary of the French Academy, and lived plainly in the home of his lifelong companion Julie de Lespinasse. He died in Paris in 1783. The supreme mathematician of the age, whose rivalry with d'Alembert over the vibrating string drove both their analyses, and who in sheer productive power has had few equals in the history of science, was Leonhard Euler.
