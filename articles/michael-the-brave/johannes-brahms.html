Johannes Brahms

German composer, 1833–1897

Brahms was born in Hamburg, the son of a struggling musician, and earned money as a boy playing the piano in the city’s taverns. His life changed in 1853 when he was introduced to Robert and Clara Schumann; the older composer at once proclaimed the unknown youth, in a famous article, as the destined master of the coming age. The connection bound Brahms to the Schumanns forever, above all to Clara, the great pianist who became his closest lifelong friend.

Brahms was a classicist born into the romantic era. He worshipped Bach and Beethoven, immersed himself in older music, and built his works on the inherited forms of symphony, sonata, and variation, filling them with a deep romantic warmth and a craftsman’s rigour. This allegiance made him, somewhat against his will, the standard-bearer of a conservative party set against the radical "music of the future" championed by Liszt and Wagner.

So daunted was he by Beethoven’s shadow that he laboured for more than two decades over his First Symphony, whose triumphant completion led admirers to call it "Beethoven’s Tenth." His mature output, four symphonies, the consoling German Requiem, concertos, a wealth of chamber music and songs, and the popular Hungarian Dances, secured his place among the supreme composers. A self-critical perfectionist who destroyed much of his work, he died in Vienna in 1897, the last great voice of the German classical line.
