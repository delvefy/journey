Philip IV of France

King of France, “the Fair”, 1268–1314

Philip IV, called “the Fair” for his cold good looks, became king of France in 1285 and ruled for nearly thirty years as one of the most powerful and most enigmatic of medieval monarchs. Working through a corps of hard, able lawyer-ministers, he drove the centralization of royal government forward relentlessly, and he was forever short of the money his ambitions demanded.

His reign was defined by great collisions. When Pope Boniface VIII forbade the taxation of the clergy and proclaimed in the bull Unam Sanctam the supremacy of the papacy over kings, Philip met him head on; in 1303 his agents seized the aged pope at Anagni, and the humiliation hastened Boniface’s death. Philip then secured the election of a compliant French pope and the removal of the papacy to Avignon, where it would sit for most of the century. Hungriest of all for the wealth of others, he expelled the Jews of France and confiscated their goods, debased his coinage, and in 1307 launched a sudden assault on the rich crusading order of the Knights Templar, arresting them on charges of heresy and pressing for their suppression; their last grand master, Jacques de Molay, was burned in 1314.

Abroad he fought costly wars with England over Gascony and with Flanders, where his knights were cut to pieces by townsmen at Courtrai in 1302. His final years were darkened by a scandal of adultery touching the wives of his sons. Those three sons each reigned and each died without a surviving male heir, extinguishing the senior Capetian line within fourteen years of his death and opening, through his daughter Isabella, the disputed succession that would ignite the Hundred Years’ War. Philip died in 1314, the order of the Temple destroyed and the papacy in his shadow.
