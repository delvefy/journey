John Knox

Scottish reformer, c.1514–1572

Knox was born near Haddington around 1514 and ordained a Catholic priest before the preaching of the reformer George Wishart, soon burned for heresy, won him to the Protestant cause. After Protestants seized the castle of St Andrews, Knox joined them, but when a French force took the stronghold he was condemned to row as a galley slave for some nineteen months, an ordeal that hardened his resolve.

Freed at last, he preached in the England of the young Edward VI, then fled abroad when the Catholic Mary I came to the throne. In Geneva he sat under John Calvin, whose city he called the most perfect school of Christ since the apostles, and from exile he hurled his notorious tract, the First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women, against the Catholic queens who ruled in his day, an attack that would later sour his relations with Elizabeth I of England.

Returning to Scotland in 1559, his thunderous sermons helped ignite a revolution, and in 1560 the Scottish Parliament broke with Rome and adopted a Reformed confession of faith. Knox laboured to build a disciplined Presbyterian Kirk and clashed in a series of famous audiences with the young Catholic queen Mary, Queen of Scots, never yielding an inch. Eloquent, fearless, and unbending, he died in 1572 as the father of the Scottish Reformation.

The road continues to Mary of Guise.
