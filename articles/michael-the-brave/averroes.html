Averroes

Andalusian philosopher and jurist, 1126–1198

Ibn Rushd, Latinized as Averroes, was born in 1126 in Córdoba into a family that had supplied the city with its chief judges for generations. Trained in Islamic law, theology, medicine and philosophy, he was presented to the Almohad caliph Abu Ya’qub Yusuf by the philosopher Ibn Tufayl, and the ruler — finding Aristotle’s works obscure — charged him with explaining them. From that commission came the labour of his life. He served meanwhile as qadi of Seville and Córdoba and as court physician.

Averroes wrote commentaries, short, middle and long, on nearly the whole of Aristotle, striving to recover the philosopher’s true meaning from later accretions. So thorough was the achievement that the Latin schoolmen knew him simply as “the Commentator,” as they knew Aristotle as “the Philosopher.” Lacking Aristotle’s Politics, he commented instead on Plato’s Republic. In The Incoherence of the Incoherence he answered al-Ghazali’s famous assault on the philosophers, defending the rational tradition that Avicenna had carried, and in the Decisive Treatise he argued that philosophy and revelation, rightly understood, could not conflict.

His boldness cost him dearly: amid a wave of religious reaction late in life he was disgraced and his books were burned, and he died in Marrakesh in 1198 with few disciples in the Islamic world. Yet in Latin Christendom his commentaries became indispensable. They provoked the movement called Averroism, drew the careful refutations of Thomas Aquinas, and earned him a place among the great pagan and Muslim sages whom Dante set in Limbo. Through Averroes, much of Aristotle returned to the West.
