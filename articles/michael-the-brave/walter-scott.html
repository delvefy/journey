Walter Scott

1771–1832, Scottish novelist, poet, and historian

Walter Scott was born in Edinburgh, the ninth of twelve children of a Writer to the Signet. A childhood attack of polio left him lame in the right leg and was treated by a long convalescence at his grandfather's farm in the Border country, where he learned the ballads and traditions of the Scottish frontier. He read law under his father, was called to the bar in 1792, and from 1799 served as Sheriff-Depute of Selkirkshire, a sinecure that left him free to write.

His first books, Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802) and the narrative poems The Lay of the Last Minstrel, Marmion and The Lady of the Lake, gave verse romance a popularity not seen before, and a fortune large enough to build the baronial pile of Abbotsford on the Tweed. When Lord Byron's eastern tales eclipsed his own poetry, Scott turned to prose, and in 1814 published Waverley anonymously. Twenty-five further “Waverley Novels” followed in scarcely twenty years — Guy Mannering, Rob Roy, The Heart of Midlothian, The Bride of Lammermoor, Ivanhoe, Kenilworth, Quentin Durward — and effectively invented the historical novel.

The form swept Europe and the Americas. Honoré de Balzac, Victor Hugo and Alexandre Dumas all wrote in conscious imitation of him, while Mark Twain, half a century later, blamed Sir Walter for the romantic medievalism of the American South. He himself reviewed Jane Austen's Emma anonymously in the Quarterly Review and praised its quiet realism. In 1822 he stage-managed George IV's visit to Scotland, dressed the king in a tartan kilt, and fixed Highland dress in the popular imagination.

His publishing partnership with James Ballantyne collapsed in the panic of 1826, leaving him liable for some 130,000 pounds. He refused bankruptcy and wrote himself to death paying it off, completing the Life of Napoleon, Tales of a Grandfather, and several more novels in the effort. He died at Abbotsford in September 1832 and is buried at Dryburgh Abbey.
