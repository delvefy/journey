Bernhard Riemann

German mathematician, 1826–1866

Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann was born in 1826 in Breselenz in the Kingdom of Hanover, the son of a Lutheran pastor. He was a shy, sickly, deeply religious boy whose mathematical gift was uncovered when his schoolmaster gave him Legendre's Theory of Numbers as Sabbath reading and he returned it six days later, complete and understood. He entered Göttingen at nineteen to read theology and slid into mathematics under Gauss, then to Berlin under Jacobi and Dirichlet, and at last back to Göttingen as Gauss's chosen successor.

His doctoral thesis of 1851 redefined complex function theory in terms of what would come to be called Riemann surfaces—multi-sheeted geometric objects on which a many-valued function could be made single-valued—and in a few dozen pages laid the foundations of modern complex analysis. His Habilitation lecture of 1854, "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundation of Geometry," extended Gauss's work on curved surfaces into geometry of n dimensions, opening the way to manifolds of arbitrary curvature: the language in which Einstein would, sixty years later, write general relativity.

His other papers were just as decisive. The 1859 memoir on the prime-counting function introduced the Riemann zeta function and the still unproven Riemann hypothesis, which has presided over analytic number theory ever since. He gave the modern integral that bears his name, contributed to the theory of trigonometric series, and reformulated mathematical physics.

His health failed under the labour. He spent the last years of his life in Italy seeking warmth and rest, and died of tuberculosis at Selasca on Lake Maggiore in 1866, aged thirty-nine. His unpublished notebooks, gathered by Richard Dedekind, kept mathematicians busy for the rest of the century. The mathematician who, in the next generation, gathered Riemann's program into a master's grand survey of mathematics, and who would lay down at the 1900 Paris Congress the twenty-three problems that defined twentieth-century mathematics, was David Hilbert.
