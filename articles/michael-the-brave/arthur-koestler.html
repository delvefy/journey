Arthur Koestler

Hungarian-British novelist and essayist, 1905–1983

Arthur Koestler was born in Budapest on 5 September 1905, into the educated Hungarian-Jewish middle class. He studied engineering and then psychology in Vienna, dropped out, and in 1926 emigrated to Mandate Palestine as a follower of Vladimir Jabotinsky’s Revisionist Zionists. The kibbutz disillusioned him; he returned to Europe and by 1930 was a feature journalist in Berlin for the Ullstein press, the largest publishing house on the continent.

In 1931 he joined the Communist Party of Germany, travelled clandestinely in Soviet Central Asia, and entered the orbit of Willi Münzenberg, the great Comintern propagandist. Sent to Spain in 1937 to cover the Civil War, he was captured by Nationalist forces at Málaga, condemned to death, and held under sentence in Seville for several months before an international press campaign secured his exchange. The Moscow purges of 1938 broke his faith, and he left the Party, writing his break into the novel for which he is best remembered.

Darkness at Noon, drafted in German and published in English in 1940, dramatized the interrogation of an old Bolshevik who confesses to crimes he has not committed because the logic of his own creed leaves him nothing to refuse. It became the most influential anti-Stalinist novel of the century. Interned in France at the war’s outset, he escaped over the Pyrenees and reached Britain through Lisbon. In postwar Paris he became close to Camus and quarrelled bitterly with Sartre; he settled in England, took British citizenship in 1948, and turned in his last decades to large speculative books — The Sleepwalkers, The Act of Creation, The Ghost in the Machine — on the history of science and the structure of the mind. Stricken by Parkinson’s and leukaemia, he killed himself in London on 1 March 1983.
