Hipparchus

Greek astronomer and founder of trigonometry, c. 190–120 BC

Hipparchus was born at Nicaea in Bithynia and did most of his work on the island of Rhodes. He is generally counted the greatest astronomer of antiquity, a careful observer who insisted that the heavens be described by numbers tested against measurement. To do so he developed the mathematical tools he needed, compiling the first known table of chords and effectively founding trigonometry, the branch of mathematics that would become indispensable to astronomy and navigation.

His achievements were many. He compiled the first comprehensive catalogue of the stars, recording the positions and brightness of hundreds of them and grading them by magnitude, a system still echoed today. He worked out improved values for the length of the year and the distance and size of the Moon. His most famous discovery came from comparing his own observations with older records: the slow shifting of the stars’ positions over centuries, the phenomenon now called the precession of the equinoxes.

Much of his writing is lost, and he is known largely because a later astronomer preserved and built upon his results. Working in Alexandria three centuries after him, Claudius Ptolemy drew so heavily on Hipparchus in compiling his own great astronomical synthesis that the earlier man’s data and methods survive embedded within it. Through that channel Hipparchus shaped the picture of the heavens that Europe and the Islamic world would accept for well over a thousand years.

The road continues to Ptolemy.
