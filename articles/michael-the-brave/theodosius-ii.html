Theodosius II

Eastern Roman emperor, 401–450

Theodosius II was born in Constantinople in 401, the son of the emperor Arcadius and grandson of Theodosius I, and he came to the Eastern throne in 408 while still a child of seven. His reign of forty-two years was one of the longest in Roman history, yet he was a bookish and retiring ruler — nicknamed the Calligrapher for his fine hand — and real power lay with others: first a capable prefect, then for many years his formidable elder sister Pulcheria, and later his wife Eudocia and palace officials.

The age nonetheless produced monuments that long outlived it. The great triple land walls raised in his name made Constantinople all but impregnable and shielded the city for a thousand years. In 438 he promulgated the Theodosian Code, the first comprehensive collection of imperial law in over a century, which preserved the legislation of Christian Rome and pointed the way to the later codification under Justinian. He also reorganized the higher school of Constantinople into something like a university.

His government was harried on every frontier. To the east lay war with Persia; to the north rose the most fearsome danger of all, the Huns, whose king Attila wrung from the empire ever heavier payments of gold in exchange for an uneasy peace. In matters of faith Theodosius summoned the councils at Ephesus that convulsed the Church over the person of Christ. He died in 450 after a fall from his horse, leaving no son, and the soldier Marcian succeeded him.
