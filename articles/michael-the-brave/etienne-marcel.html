Étienne Marcel

Provost of the merchants of Paris, c. 1302–1358

Étienne Marcel was a rich Parisian cloth merchant who in 1354 became provost of the merchants, the head of the city’s government and the most powerful commoner in France. His hour came with catastrophe. When King John II was captured by the English at Poitiers in 1356 and the kingdom was left to his untried son, the Dauphin Charles, Marcel made himself the voice of a reforming Estates-General, pressing for control over taxation and the conduct of government and winning, in the Grande Ordonnance of 1357, sweeping concessions from a crown in disarray.

When the Dauphin resisted, Marcel turned to force. In February 1358 he led an armed crowd into the prince’s own chamber and had two of his marshals cut down before his eyes, then set the red-and-blue hood of Paris on the Dauphin’s head as a sign of who now ruled. He made common cause with Charles the Bad of Navarre and looked with sympathy on the peasant fury of the Jacquerie then sweeping the countryside.

But the Dauphin slipped from the city, gathered the nobility, and blockaded Paris into hunger and fear. Marcel’s alliances frightened his own citizens, and when it was rumoured that he meant to open the gates to Navarrese and English soldiers, he was struck down by fellow Parisians in July 1358. The Dauphin re-entered Paris, and the great experiment collapsed; yet later ages would remember Marcel as an early martyr of civic liberty against royal power.
