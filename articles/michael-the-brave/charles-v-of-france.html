Charles V of France

King of France, "le Sage", 1338–1380

Charles was born at the Château de Vincennes on 21 January 1338, the eldest son of the future John II of France and Bonne of Luxembourg. In 1349 his grandfather Philip VI gave him the newly acquired province of Dauphiné and the title Dauphin — the first French heir to bear it. He married Joanna of Bourbon in 1350 and was created Duke of Normandy in 1355. The disaster of Poitiers in September 1356, where his father was captured and he himself escaped the field, made him at eighteen the king's lieutenant in a kingdom in chaos.

The regency that followed was the hardest test of his life. The Estates General of 1357 under the Paris provost Étienne Marcel demanded oversight of taxation; in February 1358 Marcel and his men murdered the marshals of Champagne and Normandy in the dauphin's chamber, and the Jacquerie peasants rose at the same moment in the Beauvaisis. Charles waited, kept his nerve, and re-entered Paris in August after Marcel had been killed at the gate. He ratified the Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 to ransom his father, and on his father's death in 1364 was crowned at Reims with his wife by his side.

His own reign was the great recovery of fourteenth-century France. He fought a war of attrition, refusing pitched battles, fortifying towns, and giving the army to a small Breton knight, Bertrand du Guesclin, whom he made Constable of France in 1370. Du Guesclin's mobile chevauchées took back Poitou, Saintonge, and most of Aquitaine. By 1380 the English held only Calais, Bordeaux, and a coastal strip. Charles built or rebuilt the Bastille, the Louvre as a royal palace, and the keep at Vincennes; he gathered there the nine hundred volumes that became the kernel of the Bibliothèque royale; he commissioned Nicole Oresme's translations of Aristotle and Augustine. The Great Schism of 1378 found France committed to the Avignon obedience of Clement VII. He died at Beauté-sur-Marne on 16 September 1380, leaving an eleven-year-old son and four ambitious brothers.

The road continues to Charles VI of France.
