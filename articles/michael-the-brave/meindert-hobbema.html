Meindert Hobbema

Dutch landscape painter, 1638–1709

Meindert Hobbema was born in Amsterdam in 1638 to humble parents and orphaned young. He served as pupil and apparent journeyman to Jacob van Ruisdael through the late 1650s, and the two seem to have sketched together in the woods and dunes outside the city. His early landscapes follow Ruisdael closely—watermills, woodland pools, ruined cottages under banks of cloud—but in a lighter, more crystalline key.

In 1668 he married Eeltje Vinck and was appointed, through her connections, a wine-gauger for the Amsterdam octroi—a customs official charged with measuring imported casks. The post paid a regular salary; from that year his output as a painter fell off sharply, and most of his finest pictures are dated before he took up office. He produced almost nothing in the last two decades of his life and died in poverty in Amsterdam in 1709, having outlived his children and his wife.

His reputation rests on a small body of luminous landscapes—The Watermill at Singraven, A Wooded Landscape with a Travellers on a Path, and above all The Avenue at Middelharnis of 1689, in which a sandy lane lined with thin, tall, half-pollarded trees runs straight back to a village under a vast Dutch sky. The Avenue, acquired by the National Gallery in 1871, became one of the most reproduced landscapes in the world and a textbook lesson in linear perspective.

Forgotten in his own time, Hobbema was rediscovered by English collectors in the eighteenth century and absorbed into the practice of Thomas Gainsborough, of Norwich School painters, and most intensely of John Constable, who carried his combination of plain Dutch motif and serious sky into English landscape. Among Hobbema's elder Dutch contemporaries, the supreme painter of pastoral golden light—cattle and herdsmen on river banks under a soft southern sun—was Aelbert Cuyp.
