Pontius Pilate

Roman prefect of Judea, governed AD 26–36

Pontius Pilate was a Roman of the equestrian order whom the emperor Tiberius appointed prefect of Judea, an office he held for about a decade from AD 26. Once thought to be known only from the Gospels, his historicity is firmly attested: the Jewish writers Josephus and Philo describe his governorship, the historian Tacitus records that "Christus" was executed under him, and a stone inscription unearthed at Caesarea in 1961 bears his name and title.

The sources portray a hard and often tactless administrator. He provoked uproar in Jerusalem by introducing military standards bearing the emperor's image into the holy city, and again by drawing on Temple funds to build an aqueduct, which led to bloody clashes with protesting crowds. His rule sat uneasily over a province seething with religious and political tension.

Pilate's enduring fame rests on a single act. The Gospels relate that the Jewish authorities brought Jesus of Nazareth before him, and that, though portrayed as reluctant and offering to release a prisoner, he yielded to the crowd, freed Barabbas, and condemned Jesus to be crucified. The phrase "crucified under Pontius Pilate" passed into the Christian creeds, fixing his name forever in the memory of the faith. Around AD 36 a violent suppression of Samaritans led to complaints that brought his recall to Rome; what became of him afterward is unknown, though later legend supplied him with various ends.
