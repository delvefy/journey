Alexander Pushkin

Russian poet, 1799–1837

Pushkin was born in Moscow into an old aristocratic family that counted among its ancestors Abram Gannibal, an African brought to the court of Peter the Great. Educated at the new imperial Lyceum at Tsarskoye Selo, he was celebrated as a prodigy almost at once. His liberal and satirical verses, however, drew the displeasure of the authorities and earned him years of internal exile in the south of Russia and on his family estate.

Out of these years came the work that founded modern Russian literature. Casting off heavy Church Slavonic convention, he forged a supple, living literary language. His verse novel Eugene Onegin, composed over the 1820s and early 1830s, gave Russia its first great portrait of itself and became the model for the novelists who followed. The Shakespearean historical drama Boris Godunov, the narrative poem The Bronze Horseman, and the prose tales The Queen of Spades and The Captain’s Daughter ranged across nearly every genre, and his encouragement helped launch the young Gogol.

Throughout his maturity he lived under the personal surveillance of Tsar Nicholas I, who appointed himself the poet’s censor. In 1837, goaded by rumours about his wife, Pushkin fought a duel with a French officer and was mortally wounded at the age of thirty-seven. His early death made him a martyr as well as a master, and he remains revered as the fountainhead of all Russian letters.
