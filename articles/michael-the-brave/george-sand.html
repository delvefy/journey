George Sand

French novelist, 1804–1876

Born Amantine Lucile Aurore Dupin, she left an unhappy marriage to make her own way in Paris by her pen, adopting the masculine name George Sand and scandalising society by wearing men’s clothes and smoking cigars in public. Her early novels, beginning with Indiana, were passionate pleas for women’s freedom in love and marriage, and made her one of the most famous and prolific writers in Europe.

Her later work turned to tender pastoral tales of the French countryside around her beloved estate at Nohant, and to the socialist and republican sympathies she embraced in the revolution of 1848. Tireless and warm-hearted, she carried on celebrated love affairs—most famously with the poet Alfred de Musset and then, for nearly a decade, with the composer Frédéric Chopin, whom she nursed through his decline.

Her home became a gathering place and her friendships a who’s who of the age: she exchanged a magnificent correspondence with Flaubert, knew Balzac, Liszt, and Delacroix, and was mourned by Victor Hugo, who hailed her at her death as an immortal of French letters. Among the younger writers who admired her and made the pilgrimage to Nohant was the visiting Russian novelist Ivan Turgenev. She died there in 1876, having published some of the most widely read fiction of the century.
