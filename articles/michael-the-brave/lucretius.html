Lucretius

Roman poet and philosopher, c. 99–c. 55 BCE

Titus Lucretius Carus was the poet who gave Epicurean philosophy its grandest voice. Almost nothing certain is known of his life; a late and doubtful tradition held that he was driven mad by a love-potion and took his own life, but this is legend rather than fact. He left a single work, the six-book poem On the Nature of Things, the fullest account of Epicurus's thought to survive from the ancient world.

In sweeping Latin hexameters Lucretius set out a wholly material universe. All that exists, he argued, is atoms and void; the soul itself is made of fine particles and perishes with the body; the gods, though real, dwell apart and take no interest in human affairs. From these premises he drew his liberating conclusion: that death is nothing to us, and that superstition—the dread of divine punishment—is the deepest source of human misery.

The poem is as much art as argument. The famous swerve of the atoms, the opening hymn to Venus, and the grim description of the plague of Athens that closes the work show a poet of the first rank, who took Empedocles as his model and won the admiration of Cicero, Virgil, and Ovid.

Nearly lost in the Middle Ages, On the Nature of Things was rescued by Poggio Bracciolini in 1417, and its return helped reshape European thought—copied by Machiavelli, quoted endlessly by Montaigne, prized by Jefferson, and at last restored to philosophy by the seventeenth-century reviver of atomism, Pierre Gassendi.
