Victor Hugo

French Romantic novelist, poet, and dramatist, 1802–1885

The colossus of French Romanticism, Hugo bestrode the literature of his century as poet, playwright, and novelist. As a young man he led the Romantic revolt against classical rules; the riotous opening night of his verse drama Hernani in 1830 became a legendary battle between the old and new schools of art. He poured out volumes of lyric and visionary poetry that alone would have secured his fame.

To the world he is best known for two vast novels. The Hunchback of Notre-Dame conjured the teeming medieval city around its great cathedral, while Les Misérables, the story of the convict Jean Valjean and his pursuer Javert, wove an immense panorama of poverty, justice, and redemption that remains one of the most beloved and adapted books ever written. Both fused gripping narrative with passionate social conscience.

Hugo was also a political force. Once a supporter of Louis-Napoléon, he turned implacably against him after the coup of 1851, branding him “Napoléon le Petit,” and went into a defiant exile of nearly twenty years on the Channel Islands rather than live under the Second Empire. He returned in triumph at its fall, a living monument of the Republic and of the French conscience. When he died in 1885 some two million people followed his coffin to the Panthéon.
