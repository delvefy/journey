Buster Keaton

1895–1966, American silent-film comedian known as “the Great Stone Face”

Joseph Frank Keaton was born in Piqua, Kansas, to vaudevillians on tour. By the age of five he was performing with his parents as “The Three Keatons,” an act so violent that child welfare officers occasionally intervened; his father threw him about the stage by the suitcase handle sewn to his costume. The nickname “Buster” was reputedly given him at six months by Harry Houdini after a tumble down a flight of stairs. By his teens his refusal to smile on stage was a trademark.

In 1917 Roscoe “Fatty” Arbuckle brought him to New York to make short comedies for the Comique studio, and Keaton learned the camera in two years of supporting work. From 1920 he had his own production unit, and through the 1920s wrote, directed and starred in a sequence of features — Our Hospitality, Sherlock Jr., The Navigator, Seven Chances, The General, Steamboat Bill Jr. — whose dangerous stunts, geometric composition, and unsmiling lead made him, with Charlie Chaplin and Harold Lloyd, one of the three great silent comedians.

The General (1926), set during the Civil War and built around a stolen locomotive, failed commercially but later came to be ranked among the finest American films. In 1928 his brother-in-law Joseph Schenck sold his contract to MGM. Keaton lost creative control, the studio refused his proposals, and a punishing first marriage to Natalie Talmadge and worsening alcoholism nearly ended his career.

He recovered slowly through gag work for the Marx Brothers, Red Skelton and others, played a wordless cameo with Chaplin in Limelight (1952), appeared as one of Norma Desmond's bridge-playing “waxworks” in Sunset Boulevard, and in 1965 starred in Samuel Beckett's twenty-two-minute Film, his only collaboration with a major modernist writer. He died of lung cancer in 1966, just as his early features were being rediscovered.
