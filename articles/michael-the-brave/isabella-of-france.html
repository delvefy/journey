Isabella of France

Queen of England, “the She-Wolf”, c. 1295–1358

Isabella was born about 1295, a daughter of the formidable Philip IV of France, and in 1308 she was married to Edward II of England to seal a peace between the two crowns. The marriage was wretched. Edward lavished his affection and his patronage on favourites — first Piers Gaveston, then the grasping Despensers — while the queen was pushed aside, her revenues stripped, her dignity slighted. For years she endured it, but the humiliation hardened into resolve.

The chance came in 1325, when she was sent to France to negotiate with her brother King Charles IV. She refused to come home, and at the French court she joined herself, in politics and in bed, to the exiled English baron Roger Mortimer. In 1326 the pair landed in England with a small army; Edward’s hated government simply collapsed, the Despensers were put to grisly deaths, and in January 1327 the king was forced to abdicate in favour of his young son. Within months Edward II was dead at Berkeley Castle, almost certainly murdered.

Isabella and Mortimer now ruled in the boy-king’s name, and ruled badly — greedy, high-handed, and humiliating in their peace with the Scots. In 1330 the eighteen-year-old Edward III struck back, seizing Mortimer in a midnight coup at Nottingham and sending him to the gallows, while sparing his mother an honourable retirement. Isabella lived on until 1358. Her lasting bequest was unintended: as the last surviving child of Philip IV with her brothers all dead without sons, she passed to Edward III a claim to the throne of France, the spark of the Hundred Years’ War. Later ages, recoiling from her, called her the She-Wolf of France.
