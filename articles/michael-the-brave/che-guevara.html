Che Guevara

Argentine-Cuban revolutionary, 1928–1967.

Ernesto Guevara de la Serna was born to a left-leaning Argentine bourgeois family in Rosario, an asthmatic eldest child who finished a medical degree at the University of Buenos Aires in 1953. The two long motorcycle journeys across South America he had taken with his friend Alberto Granado — recorded in the posthumously published Motorcycle Diaries — persuaded him that the continent's poverty was the product of US capital and local oligarchy and could be answered only with revolution. He arrived in Guatemala just in time to see the CIA overthrow Jacobo Árbenz in 1954, took refuge in Mexico, and there in July 1955 was introduced by a fellow Cuban exile to Fidel Castro.

He sailed on the Granma in December 1956 as the unit's doctor and quickly took up the rifle. Through the Sierra Maestra campaign he commanded the second guerrilla column, took the city of Santa Clara in the last days of 1958 with a famous attack on Batista's armoured train, and rode into Havana on 2 January 1959. The new government made him head of the National Bank, then minister of industries; he travelled to Moscow and Beijing to negotiate Cuba's economic reorientation, wrote the manuals Guerrilla Warfare and Man and Socialism in Cuba, and at the United Nations in 1964 denounced US imperialism in a coat and boots.

Restless for further revolution, he resigned his Cuban posts and his Argentine citizenship in 1965, attempted to ignite an insurgency in the Congo against Mobutu's allies, and in 1966 moved with a small group into the Bolivian jungle to attempt the same. The local Communist Party would not cooperate, peasant support failed to materialize, and on 8 October 1967 his column was surrounded by Bolivian rangers trained and accompanied by the CIA at La Higuera. He was captured wounded, executed the next morning by a sergeant under orders from President René Barrientos, and his body displayed for press photographs in Vallegrande. Alberto Korda's 1960 photograph of him in beret — Guerrillero Heroico — became the most reproduced image of the twentieth century and turned him, in death, into the patron saint of every later youth revolt.
