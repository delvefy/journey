Akhenaten

Pharaoh of Egypt, r. c. 1353–1336 BC

Akhenaten, who began his reign as Amenhotep IV, was the son of Amenhotep III and Queen Tiye and one of the most extraordinary figures of the ancient world. A few years into his rule he launched a religious revolution without precedent: he proclaimed the Aten, the disk of the sun, the one true god, and set about suppressing the worship of Amun and the whole crowded pantheon of Egypt. He changed his name to Akhenaten, “effective for the Aten,” and composed hymns of striking beauty to the sole creator whose rays touched all the world.

To house his new faith he built a virgin capital in the desert, Akhetaten, and moved his court there with his celebrated queen Nefertiti, who appears at his side with a prominence given to no other Egyptian consort. The art of his reign broke as sharply with tradition as his religion, portraying the royal family in an intimate, oddly elongated naturalism unlike anything before. But while he gazed upon his god, the empire frayed; the diplomatic letters of his archive show distant vassals begging in vain for help.

His revolution did not outlive him. His successors, his son Tutankhamun among them, restored the old gods, abandoned his city, and later kings struck his name from the records as that of a heretic. Forgotten for three thousand years, he was recovered by modern scholarship and hailed as perhaps the first monotheist in history — an idea that drew Sigmund Freud to make him the hidden source of the faith of Moses. He died about 1336 BC, the strangest and most solitary of the pharaohs.
