Hatshepsut

Female pharaoh of Egypt, r. c. 1479–1458 BC

Hatshepsut was one of the very few women to rule Egypt as pharaoh, and by far the most successful. The daughter of Thutmose I and wife of her half-brother Thutmose II, she became regent on her husband’s death for his young heir, her stepson Thutmose III. But within a few years she took the full titles and regalia of kingship for herself, having herself depicted in the traditional royal forms, even with the ceremonial false beard, and reigned in her own right for some two decades.

Hers was an age of peace and prosperity rather than conquest. Her most celebrated achievement was a great trading expedition to the distant land of Punt, which returned laden with myrrh trees, incense, ivory and gold, an event she recorded in vivid relief. Above all she was a builder: her terraced mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahri, raised against the cliffs by her steward Senenmut, is among the masterpieces of Egyptian architecture, and she set towering obelisks at Karnak.

After her death her stepson at last ruled alone, and in his later years her name and image were systematically erased from her monuments — whether from resentment or to smooth his own son’s succession. The erasure was so thorough that Hatshepsut was all but lost to history until modern scholars pieced her reign back together and, in the twenty-first century, identified her mummy. She is now recognized as one of the great rulers of the New Kingdom.
