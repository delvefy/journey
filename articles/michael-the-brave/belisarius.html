Belisarius

Byzantine general, c.500–565

Belisarius was born about 500 in the Balkan borderlands and rose through the army to become the foremost general of the Emperor Justinian I, the instrument of his dream of restoring the Roman Empire in the West. He first made his name on the eastern frontier, winning a notable victory over the Persians at Dara, and in 532 he helped crush the Nika riots in Constantinople, saving the throne when the mob had all but toppled it.

The next year Justinian sent him against the Vandal kingdom of Africa. In a swift and brilliant campaign he landed near Carthage, routed the Vandals in two battles, captured their king, and extinguished the realm that Gaiseric had founded a century before, returning to celebrate a triumph such as no private citizen had enjoyed for centuries. He then crossed to Italy to reclaim it from the Ostrogoths, taking Sicily, Naples and Rome, holding the old capital through a desperate year-long siege, and entering Ravenna in 540 — where the Goths, in admiration, offered him the Western crown, which he refused out of loyalty.

His later years were harder. Recalled to fight the Persians, sent back to Italy with too few men against a Gothic revival, and once defending Constantinople itself against a steppe raid, he served on with dwindling reward. He fell briefly under the emperor’s suspicion before being restored, and he died in 565. The legend that he ended his days blind and begging in the streets is a later invention, but his campaigns, recorded by the historian Procopius, made him the last great general of classical Rome.
