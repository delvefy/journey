Louis XIII

King of France, 1601–1643

The eldest son of Henry IV and Marie de’ Medici, Louis became king at the age of eight when his father was assassinated in 1610, his mother governing as regent. In 1617 the young king threw off her tutelage and that of her Italian favourite Concini, whom he had murdered, asserting his own authority. He had been married as a boy to the Spanish princess Anne of Austria, a union long strained and childless before it at last produced the future Louis XIV.

Reserved, devout, and often ill, Louis is remembered above all for his partnership with Cardinal Richelieu, whom he raised to power in 1624 and supported unwaveringly against court intrigues, his own mother, and his rebellious brother. Together king and cardinal broke the political power of the Huguenots—Louis personally directing the siege of the Protestant stronghold of La Rochelle—curbed the great nobles, and led France into the Thirty Years’ War against the Habsburgs, laying the foundations of French preponderance in Europe.

A brave if melancholy soldier and a gifted amateur musician, he ruled a kingdom whose real direction lay largely in his minister’s hands. Both men died within months of each other, Richelieu in 1642 and the king in 1643, leaving the throne to a four-year-old son under the regency of Anne of Austria. His reign is the France of cardinals and musketeers, the prelude to the grandeur of the Sun King.
