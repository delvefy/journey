Roger Bacon

English philosopher and friar, c.1219–1292

Roger Bacon was born in England about 1219 and studied at Oxford and Paris, where he was among the first to lecture on the newly recovered works of Aristotle. Shaped by the Oxford tradition of Robert Grosseteste, he came to believe that mathematics and direct experiment — scientia experimentalis — rather than the citing of authorities, were the true road to knowledge of nature. He entered the Franciscan Order and pursued an extraordinary range of studies.

His passion was optics, and here he built directly on the Greeks — Euclid and Ptolemy — and above all on the Arab master of the subject, Ibn al-Haytham. He investigated lenses and mirrors, the rainbow and the workings of the eye, and speculated about instruments that might magnify the distant and the small. When Pope Clement IV asked to see his work, Bacon poured his learning into the encyclopedic Opus Majus and its companion volumes, urging the reform of education, the study of languages and the application of mathematics to every science.

Sharp-tongued, he scorned the ignorance of his contemporaries, and he may have suffered restriction by his order for his boldness. Later ages, half in legend, made him a wizard who built a talking brazen head and a prophet who foresaw gunpowder, flying machines and ships without oars. Stripped of the myths, Roger Bacon remains a striking herald of the experimental spirit, honoured as the Doctor Mirabilis. He died at Oxford about 1292.
