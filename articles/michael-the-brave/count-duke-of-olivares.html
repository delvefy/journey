Count-Duke of Olivares

Spanish royal favourite and statesman, 1587–1645

Gaspar de Guzmán, Count of Olivares and Duke of Sanlúcar—known by the fused title Count-Duke—was the all-powerful favourite of Philip IV of Spain for more than twenty years. Seizing the young king’s confidence on his accession in 1621, he set out to arrest the decline of the Spanish monarchy through tireless reform: cleaning up court finances, attacking corruption, and above all his cherished scheme, the Union of Arms, to make the kingdoms of Aragon, Portugal, and the rest share the military burden borne by Castile.

A man of furious energy and ambition, he renewed the long war against the Dutch and threw Spain’s weight into the Thirty Years’ War, locking himself in a great duel with his French counterpart Cardinal Richelieu. Early in the reign he hosted the future Charles I of England, who came incognito to Madrid in 1623 to court the Infanta. But the endless wars devoured men and money, and his centralising demands provoked the catastrophic revolts of 1640 in Catalonia and Portugal.

With the monarchy seeming to crack apart, Philip dismissed his minister in 1643; Olivares retired in disgrace and died, his mind failing, two years later. A discerning patron, he had drawn the young Diego Velázquez to court and sat for the painter’s famous portraits. History remembers him as the tragic, overreaching reformer who strove to save an empire he helped to overstrain.
