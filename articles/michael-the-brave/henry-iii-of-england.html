Henry III of England

King of England, 1207–1272

Henry III was born in 1207, the eldest son of King John, and inherited the throne in 1216 as a boy of nine, while a French prince held London and rebel barons held half the realm. The aged knight William Marshal governed as regent, drove out the invaders, and reissued Magna Carta in the child king’s name—an act that quietly converted the rebels’ charter into the settled law of the land. Henry’s personal reign, once he came of age, lasted some fifty-six years, among the longest in English history.

It was not a successful one. Pious, artistic and improvident, Henry poured his treasure into rebuilding Westminster Abbey as a Gothic shrine for his patron Edward the Confessor, and into ruinous foreign adventures—most notoriously the “Sicilian Business,” an attempt to buy a Mediterranean crown for his son from the papacy. His reliance on his wife’s Savoyard kin and his own Lusignan half-brothers bred deep resentment among the English baronage.

That resentment hardened into revolution. In 1258 the barons forced upon him the Provisions of Oxford, placing the king under a council; when Henry repudiated them, the realm slid into the Second Barons’ War. His brother-in-law Simon de Montfort captured him at Lewes in 1264 and ruled in his name, summoning in 1265 a parliament that, for the first time, called burgesses from the towns. The tide turned when Henry’s son, the future Edward I, escaped captivity and destroyed Montfort at Evesham. Restored but exhausted, Henry reigned quietly until his death in 1272, and was buried in the great church he had remade.

The road continues to King John.
