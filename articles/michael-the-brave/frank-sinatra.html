Frank Sinatra

American singer and actor, 1915–1998.

Born Francis Albert Sinatra in Hoboken, New Jersey, to Sicilian immigrants, Sinatra worked as a copy boy at the Jersey Observer before resolving, after a Bing Crosby concert, to make his career in song. He sang on local radio, broke into the big bands with Harry James in 1939, joined Tommy Dorsey the same year, and by 1943 had set off the first mass screaming-teenager phenomenon in American popular music — the "Columbus Day Riot" at the Paramount Theatre in New York in October 1944 saw thirty thousand bobby-soxers lay siege to the building.

His film career began in MGM musicals and reached its critical peak in 1953 with the Best Supporting Actor Oscar for From Here to Eternity; the part, which he had lobbied for desperately, rescued him from a slump in which his voice had failed and his recording contract had been cancelled. He returned to the top of the charts at Capitol Records with arrangements by Nelson Riddle — In the Wee Small Hours, Songs for Swingin' Lovers!, Only the Lonely — and founded his own label Reprise. In Las Vegas in the early 1960s he led the "Rat Pack" with Dean Martin, Sammy Davis Jr., Peter Lawford, and Joey Bishop, a self-described summit of cool that turned the Sands into the centre of American showbusiness.

His private life was a turbulent series of marriages — to Nancy Barbato, to Ava Gardner, to Mia Farrow, finally to Barbara Marx — and his loyalties stretched from John F. Kennedy, for whose 1960 campaign he organized fundraisers (and whose mob-connected friend Sam Giancana he shared), to Ronald Reagan, at whose 1981 inauguration he served as gala chairman. Kennedy dropped him after the election to insulate the presidency from the Giancana association, a snub Sinatra never forgave. He sang in public into his eightieth year, and died in Los Angeles in 1998, by then the most imitated and best-remembered voice in twentieth-century American song.

The road continues to Elvis Presley.
