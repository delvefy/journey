Rembrandt

Dutch Golden Age painter and etcher, 1606–1669

The son of a Leiden miller, Rembrandt van Rijn trained under a master who had absorbed the latest Italian ideas and soon set up in booming Amsterdam, where his vivid, dramatic portraits made him the most fashionable painter in the city. He married well, prospered, and built a fine house and a great collection of art and curiosities, studying the grand manner of Rubens and the warm colour of the Venetian Titian to enlarge his own ambitions.

Above all he was the supreme master of light and shadow and of human feeling. His enormous militia group portrait, The Night Watch, broke every convention by setting its figures in dramatic motion; his hundreds of etchings explored every subject with astonishing freedom; and in a lifelong series of searching self-portraits he charted his own face from confident youth to disillusioned age, an autobiography unmatched in art.

Fortune turned against him. He lost his wife Saskia young, outspent his means, and was declared insolvent in 1656, his treasures sold off; later he buried his devoted companion Hendrickje and his only surviving son. Yet adversity deepened his art, and his late works—the tender Jewish Bride, the overwhelming Return of the Prodigal Son—reach a profundity few painters have approached. He died poor and is now revered as one of the greatest artists who ever lived.
