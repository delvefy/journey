Constantine the Great

First Christian Roman emperor, c. 272–337 AD

Constantine was born at Naissus in the Balkans around 272, the son of the officer Constantius and his consort Helena. He rose at the court of the emperor Diocletian, and when his father died at York in 306 the army of Britain proclaimed him Augustus. This drew him into the long civil wars that tore apart the Tetrarchy Diocletian had built to govern the divided empire.

In 312 he marched on Rome against his rival Maxentius. Tradition holds that before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge he saw a sign of the Christian God with the words “by this conquer”; his victory made him master of the West. In 313 he and his eastern colleague Licinius issued the Edict of Milan, granting toleration to Christians. He later turned on Licinius, and by 324 ruled alone as sole emperor of the Roman world.

In 325 he summoned the First Council of Nicaea, the first ecumenical council, which condemned the teaching of Arius and produced the Nicene Creed. He lavished wealth and privileges on the Church, though he himself was baptised only on his deathbed. In 330 he dedicated a new Christian capital, Constantinople, on the site of Byzantium, shifting the empire's centre of gravity eastward.

He also reformed the coinage with the gold solidus, reorganised the army and bureaucracy, and built on Diocletian's administrative foundations. He died in 337, dividing the empire among his sons. Honoured as a saint in the Eastern churches, he is remembered as the ruler who turned Rome toward Christianity and founded the city that bore his name for a thousand years.
