Nicolas Poussin

French classical painter, 1594–1665

Nicolas Poussin was born in 1594 at Les Andelys in Normandy, the son of a minor soldier. He worked his way to Paris and lived for years on the edge of poverty, copying engravings of Raphael and learning what he could from a succession of obscure masters. In 1624 he reached Rome, the city he would call home for nearly all the rest of his life, and entered the orbit of the antiquarian Cassiano dal Pozzo, whose collection of drawings after the antique became Poussin's private school of archaeology.

His first decade in Rome produced ardent, Titianesque mythologies—the Bacchanals, the Triumph of Pan, the Death of Germanicus—painted in saturated colour and warm flesh. From the late 1630s his manner cooled and tightened into the severe, rationally constructed compositions that came to define him: The Israelites Gathering Manna, the two series of Sacraments for his French patron Paul Fréart de Chantelou, Et in Arcadia Ego with its shepherds reading a tomb in a calm Arcadian valley.

Cardinal Richelieu summoned him to Paris in 1640 to oversee the decoration of the Louvre, but Poussin disliked the court and the rival painters who resented him, and within two years he fled back to Rome. He spent his last twenty years in steady, austere production for a small circle of learned patrons, working out his pictures from clay figurines arranged in a peep-box, and writing reflective letters on painting that became a classical creed.

He died in Rome in 1665. His doctrine of measured composition and elevated subject became the dogma of the French Royal Academy and shaped Jacques-Louis David, Ingres, and centuries of art teaching; Cézanne declared his own mission to be "to redo Poussin from nature." Poussin's lifelong friend and fellow Frenchman in Rome, the supreme master of ideal landscape, was Claude Lorrain.
