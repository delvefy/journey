Aelbert Cuyp

Dutch landscape painter of golden light, 1620–1691

Aelbert Cuyp was born in Dordrecht in 1620, the son and pupil of the portraitist Jacob Gerritsz Cuyp. He spent virtually his entire life in his native town, on the Meuse where it meets the Maas, and made his sitters and his subjects out of its quays, its herders, and the broad river skies above it. He almost certainly trained also under Jan van Goyen, whose monochrome tonal landscapes were the foundation of Dutch landscape painting in his generation.

From the late 1640s Cuyp turned away from Van Goyen's grey-brown manner toward the Italianate golden light that the painters of the "Bamboccianti" and Jan Both had brought back from Rome. His pictures from then on are bathed in a low, southern, almost theatrical sun, even when the subject is plainly the Dutch lowland. The Maas at Dordrecht with the great Grote Kerk looming over moored ships, the Herdsmen with Cattle on a Hillside, the View of Nijmegen, the equestrian portraits of young Dutch gentlemen on horseback all share a single warm, level afternoon light.

Cuyp married well in 1658, became a city alderman and a deacon of the Reformed Church, and seems to have painted very little in the last thirty years of his life. He died in Dordrecht in 1691, a prosperous citizen, his pictures little known outside the United Provinces.

In the eighteenth century English collectors discovered him, and his canvases passed in great quantity into British country houses and then into the National Gallery. Joshua Reynolds praised him in his Royal Academy Discourses; Gainsborough learned from him a way of laying golden light along a horizon; and J.M.W. Turner became, by his own confession, his most devoted heir, working out in painting after painting how Cuyp had made the sun itself the centre of a picture. But Cuyp's earliest manner was the dun-coloured river world of his great Leiden predecessor, the painter who founded the realistic Dutch landscape—Jan van Goyen.
