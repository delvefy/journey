Erasmus of Rotterdam

Desiderius Erasmus, Dutch humanist, c. 1466–1536

Erasmus was born at Rotterdam around 1466, the illegitimate son of a priest. Orphaned by plague in his teens, he was placed in the Augustinian house at Steyn and ordained in 1492. He never relished cloistral life; a dispensation from the Bishop of Cambrai let him out as a secretary, and in 1495 he was sent to Paris to study theology. He hated the Sorbonne’s schoolmen and made a friend instead of the Greek-reading English humanists he met on a visit to London in 1499—Thomas More chief among them. From then on his life was a long itinerary between Paris, Louvain, Oxford, Cambridge, Venice, Rome, Basel and Freiburg, financed by patrons, royal pensions and the printing presses that learned to set his Latin.

His books defined the temper of Northern humanism. The Adagia, a great gathering of classical proverbs first published in 1500 and expanded for the rest of his life, made him famous. In Praise of Folly, dictated in a week at More’s house in Bucklersbury in 1509, made him notorious. The Enchiridion Militis Christiani argued for an interior, ethically serious lay piety against the externals of late-medieval religion. His greatest achievement was scholarly: in 1516, with Johann Froben in Basel, he published the first printed Greek New Testament, the Novum Instrumentum, with a fresh Latin translation that quietly corrected the Vulgate at hundreds of points and underlay almost every reformer’s reading of scripture for the next century.

That edition made the Reformation possible, but Erasmus refused to join it. He admired Luther’s early courage, but recoiled from the bitterness of his polemics and from the prospect of schism. In 1524, prodded by his old English patrons, he wrote De libero arbitrio against Luther on free will; Luther’s De servo arbitrio of the next year answered him in such terms that the breach was permanent. He moved to Basel to escape Catholic Louvain, and on to Freiburg to escape Protestant Basel. He died in Basel on 12 July 1536, painted toward the end by Hans Holbein the Younger.
