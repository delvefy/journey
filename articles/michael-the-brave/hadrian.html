Hadrian

Roman emperor, 76–138 AD

Hadrian came from a family of Spanish origin and was a ward and kinsman of the emperor Trajan, whose grand-niece Sabina he married. He rose through military and civil office under Trajan's patronage, and on the emperor's death in 117 he was proclaimed his successor. Where Trajan had pushed the empire to its widest bounds, Hadrian chose consolidation: he gave up the hard-to-hold conquests in the east and devoted himself to securing and defining the frontiers he kept.

The most famous monument to that policy was the great wall he ordered built across northern Britain, still bearing his name. A restless traveller, he spent much of his reign touring the provinces in person, inspecting armies and founding cities. A passionate admirer of Greek culture, he rebuilt the Pantheon in Rome in the form that still stands, raised a vast villa at Tivoli, and adorned Athens with new splendour. When his beloved companion Antinous drowned in the Nile, the grief-stricken emperor had the youth deified and founded a city in his memory.

His reign had a darker side. His decision to refound Jerusalem as a pagan colony and to forbid certain Jewish practices helped ignite the fierce Bar Kokhba revolt, which he suppressed with terrible severity, devastating Judea and renaming it Syria Palaestina. He also gave Roman law a more settled form. As the end neared, Hadrian arranged the succession with deliberate foresight, adopting Antoninus Pius and binding him to adopt in turn the young Marcus Aurelius. He died in 138, and was numbered among Rome's ablest rulers.
