Nostradamus

Michel de Nostredame, French physician and seer, 1503–1566

Michel de Nostredame was born on 14 December 1503 at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence into a family of recently converted Jews. His grandfather, a notary, taught him classical languages and the rudiments of astrology. From 1521 he travelled the south of France as an itinerant apothecary, working among the plague-stricken towns of Provence and Languedoc and earning a reputation for unorthodox treatments that emphasised fresh air, clean water and an oral preparation of rose petals. In 1529 he entered the medical faculty at Montpellier, but was expelled the next year when his earlier work as an apothecary came to light.

He settled in Agen, married, and then lost his wife and two children to a second epidemic. The double blow drove him back onto the road; he visited Italy in the late 1530s, was investigated by the Inquisition on a charge of irreverence, and finally settled at Salon-de-Provence in 1547 with a second wife. From 1550 he published an annual almanac of forecasts, and in 1555 the first installment of Les Prophéties, a sequence of 942 four-line stanzas (the famous quatrains) written in a deliberately obscure mixture of French, Latin, Provençal and Greek. The book was an immediate sensation.

Queen Catherine de’ Medici, an Italian-educated reader of esoterica, summoned him to Paris in July 1556. He cast horoscopes for her children, dined with the king Henri II, and was lodged in the bishop’s palace. The death of Henri II in 1559 in the very manner some of the quatrains seemed to describe—a young lion piercing the old lion’s eye in a cage of gold—cemented his fame. In 1564 the young king Charles IX, on a royal progress through the south, named him counsellor and physician-in-ordinary. He was by then crippled by gout, and died on the night of 1–2 July 1566 in his Salon house, having predicted, accurately, that he would not be found alive the next morning.

The road continues to Henri II of France.
