Hannibal

Carthaginian general, 247–c. 183 BC

Hannibal was the son of the Carthaginian commander Hamilcar Barca, who, by tradition, made the boy swear undying enmity to Rome. Taking command of Carthage's armies in Spain, Hannibal provoked the Second Punic War by storming the Roman-allied city of Saguntum, and in 218 BC he launched one of the most audacious campaigns in military history, marching his army—with its war elephants—over the Pyrenees, across southern Gaul, and through the snows of the Alps into the heart of Italy.

There he shattered one Roman army after another. At the Trebia and at Lake Trasimene he destroyed the forces sent against him, and at Cannae in 216 BC he enveloped and annihilated a far larger Roman host in a battle still studied as the supreme example of tactical encirclement. Yet he could not take the city of Rome itself, and the cautious delaying strategy of Fabius Maximus slowly bled his strength as he ranged Italy for more than a decade.

When the young Roman general Scipio carried the war to Africa, Hannibal was recalled to defend Carthage, and at Zama in 202 BC he met his match and his defeat. Afterward he turned reformer, governing Carthage ably until Roman hostility drove him into exile. He took refuge with Antiochus III and urged him to fight Rome, and when that cause collapsed he fled from court to court, hunted by his old enemy. Cornered at last in Bithynia, he took poison rather than be handed over. Ranked among the greatest commanders who ever lived—Hannibal himself placed only Alexander above his own art—he haunted the Roman imagination for generations.
