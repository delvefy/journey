Ashoka

Mauryan emperor of India, c. 304–232 BCE

Ashoka was the grandson of Chandragupta Maurya, founder of the Maurya Empire, and inherited from his father Bindusara a realm that spanned almost the whole Indian subcontinent. He came to the throne around 268 BCE, and in the early part of his reign he was a forceful and even ruthless ruler, extending and consolidating his vast dominions.

The turning point came with his conquest of Kalinga, on the eastern coast, around 261 BCE. The war was won, but at a terrible cost—by his own account a hundred thousand were slain and many more carried off. Stricken with remorse at the suffering he had unleashed, Ashoka renounced wars of conquest and embraced the teaching of the Buddha.

He devoted the rest of his reign to ruling by dhamma—righteousness and moral duty. He had edicts carved on rocks and polished pillars across his empire, proclaiming non-violence, religious tolerance, care for the poor, the sick, and even animals, and the planting of trees and wells along the roads. These inscriptions, in several languages, are among the oldest decipherable writings of India and a unique record of an ancient ruler's conscience.

A devoted patron of Buddhism, he is said to have built thousands of stupas, convened a great council, and sent missionaries far beyond his borders—including his own son Mahinda to Sri Lanka—helping to turn a regional teaching into a world religion. The lion capital of one of his pillars is today the national emblem of India. Remembered as Ashoka the Great, he became the very model of the benevolent Buddhist monarch.
