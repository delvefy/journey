Gaston de Foix

Duke of Nemours, the Thunderbolt of Italy, 1489–1512

Gaston was born in December 1489 at Mazères in Languedoc, son of Jean de Foix, Viscount of Narbonne, and Marie of Orléans, sister of the future Louis XII. The Foix house held the small Pyrenean kingdom of Navarre on his mother’s side; on his father’s, the high marshal’s baton of southern France. He was raised at the court of his uncle the king, made captain of the gendarme companies of the royal household at sixteen, and on 5 October 1511 was sent to Milan, twenty-one years old, to take command of the French army in northern Italy in place of the unsteady Chaumont d’Amboise. He was made Duke of Nemours at the same hour to give him the rank his command required.

The Holy League of Pope Julius II, Ferdinand of Aragon, and Venice was closing on the French. Gaston broke its winter quarters apart in six weeks. He relieved the citadel of Bologna by a forced march in icy rain on 5 February 1512, the army arriving so unexpectedly that the cardinal-legate Giovanni de’ Medici barely escaped. He turned and stormed Brescia on 19 February, was wounded in the foot at the head of the assault and led the second assault barefoot in the snow, and let his troops sack the city for five days — a Renaissance atrocity that filled the chronicles of Lombardy for a generation. He moved on Ravenna in mid-March, gambled on giving battle before the Spanish-papal army could be reinforced by Swiss pikes coming down from the Alps, and on Easter Sunday, 11 April 1512, drew the league out of its trenches.

Ravenna is recognised as the first battle in which artillery was the decisive arm. The new bronze cannons of Alfonso d’Este of Ferrara, deployed in enfilade across the field, broke the Spanish infantry against its own emplacements; the French heavy horse and the German landsknechts of Gaston’s second line then carried the entrenchment and rolled up the Spanish line. The Spanish viceroy Ramón de Cardona escaped; Gonzalo’s deputy Pedro Navarro and the legate Medici were captured. But as Gaston pursued a knot of fleeing Spanish infantry across a ditch outside the camp, he was unhorsed by a pike-thrust and stabbed to death where he lay, twenty-two years old. The French won the battle of his life and lost Italy within six months for want of him.
