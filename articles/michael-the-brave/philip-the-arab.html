Philip the Arab

Roman emperor, r. 244–249

Marcus Julius Philippus, called Philip the Arab, was born around 204 at Shahba in the Roman province of Arabia, in the volcanic uplands of what is now southern Syria. He rose through the army and became praetorian prefect to the young emperor Gordian III on the Persian campaign. When Gordian died in 244 — by many accounts through Philip’s own scheming — the soldiers raised Philip to the purple, and he hastened to make peace with the Sasanian king Shapur I, buying his withdrawal with a great sum of gold.

Returning west, Philip ruled for five comparatively stable years. His reign reached its summit in 248, when he presided over the Secular Games celebrating the thousandth anniversary of the founding of Rome — a festival of extraordinary splendour, all the more poignant for coming as the empire entered its darkest century. Later Christian writers, recalling his tolerance, would even claim him as the first Christian emperor, though the story is probably no more than legend.

The peace did not last. Revolts flared on the frontiers, and the general Decius, sent to restore order on the Danube, was hailed emperor by his own troops. Philip marched against him and was defeated and killed near Verona in 249. His son and co-emperor was slain soon after, and the brief calm of his reign gave way again to the violence of the age.
