Thomas Carlyle

Scottish essayist and historian, 1795–1881

Born to a stern Calvinist stonemason’s family at Ecclefechan, Carlyle lost his religious faith at university but never its moral fervour, channelling it into a torrential, idiosyncratic prose unlike anything in English. He immersed himself in German literature, translating Goethe’s Wilhelm Meister and corresponding with the aged poet, and in Sartor Resartus dressed his spiritual struggles in elaborate satire.

His masterpiece, The French Revolution, made his reputation; when a friend’s servant accidentally burned the only manuscript of the first volume, Carlyle grimly wrote it again. The book’s vivid, apocalyptic vision of mobs and martyrs deeply marked Charles Dickens, who carried it about with him and drew on it for A Tale of Two Cities. In On Heroes and Hero-Worship Carlyle advanced his “great man” theory of history, and he assailed the cruelties of industrial society and its “cash nexus.”

Revered as the “Sage of Chelsea,” he grew ever more authoritarian and harsh in his later writings, and his reputation suffered for it. His marriage to the brilliant Jane Welsh Carlyle produced one of the great correspondences of the century. He wrote a vast life of Frederick the Great before dying in 1881. The novelist who took fire from his history of the Revolution was Charles Dickens.
