Josephus

Jewish historian, c.37–100

Born Yosef ben Matityahu in Jerusalem about 37, into a family of priests with Hasmonean royal blood, Josephus was schooled in the Jewish law and travelled as a young man to Rome. When the great revolt against Roman rule erupted in 66 he was given command in Galilee, and there his extraordinary life turned. Besieged and beaten at Jotapata, he survived a suicide pact among the last defenders, surrendered to the Roman general Vespasian, and won his captor’s favour by prophesying — correctly — that Vespasian would become emperor.

Freed and rewarded, he took the imperial family name and became Titus Flavius Josephus, a Roman citizen and client of the Flavians. He accompanied Vespasian’s son Titus to the siege of Jerusalem in 70, watched the burning of the Temple, and pleaded in vain with his countrymen to surrender — conduct that branded him a traitor in Jewish memory even as it made him the great chronicler of the catastrophe.

In Rome, under imperial patronage, he wrote in Greek the works that preserve a whole world. The Jewish War recounts the revolt and the last stand at Masada; the vast Antiquities of the Jews traces his people’s history from the creation through Moses and the kings to the age of Herod the Great and the Roman governors. His pages are our chief source for Herodian Judea and for figures such as Pontius Pilate, and they contain the disputed passage on Jesus that Christians would treasure. He recorded too the embassy of the philosopher Philo to the emperor. Josephus died about the year 100, the indispensable witness to the Judea of his age.
