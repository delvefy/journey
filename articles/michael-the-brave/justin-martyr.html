Justin Martyr

Christian apologist and philosopher, c.100–165

Justin was born around the year 100 at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria, of pagan Greek stock, and spent his youth searching through the schools of philosophy — Stoic, Aristotelian, Pythagorean and at last Platonist — for the truth. According to his own account, a chance conversation with an old man by the sea turned him to the Hebrew prophets and to Christ, in whom he believed he had found at last the “true philosophy.” He kept the philosopher’s cloak ever after and opened a Christian school at Rome.

He became the foremost defender of the faith in his century. In his two Apologies, addressed to the emperor Antoninus Pius and the Roman authorities, he argued that Christians were no enemies of the state and explained their worship; in the Dialogue with Trypho he debated a learned Jew over the meaning of the scriptures. His boldest idea was that the divine Logos, fully revealed in Christ, had scattered its seeds throughout humanity, so that those who had lived by reason before Christ — Socrates above all, the philosopher he most admired — were in a manner Christians already.

His learning could not shield him. Around 165, under Marcus Aurelius, he was denounced, tried before the prefect of Rome, and, refusing to sacrifice to the gods, beheaded with several companions — winning the name by which he is forever known, Justin Martyr. He stands as the great bridge between the philosophy of the Greeks and the theology of the Church.
