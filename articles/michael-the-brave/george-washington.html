George Washington

First President of the United States, 1732–1799

Washington was a Virginia planter and surveyor who first won military experience in the French and Indian War. When the American colonies moved toward open conflict with Britain, the Continental Congress chose him to command its army, valuing his steadiness, his imposing presence, and his standing among the southern colonies. For eight hard years he held together an underfed, poorly supplied force against the world’s leading military power.

His genius lay less in winning battles than in not losing the war: he preserved his army through the desperate winter at Valley Forge, struck boldly at Trenton when morale was lowest, and with decisive French help trapped a British army at Yorktown, effectively ending the war and securing American independence. At its close he did the thing that astonished his contemporaries and even his royal adversary, George III: he resigned his commission and returned to private life rather than seize power.

Called back to public service, he presided over the convention that drafted the United States Constitution and was then chosen, unanimously, as the country’s first president. Over two terms he set enduring precedents for the new office, kept the fragile republic out of European war, and then voluntarily stepped down, warning in his Farewell Address against party faction and entangling alliances. A slaveholder who freed his slaves in his will, he became, for later generations, the indispensable founder, revered as the father of his country.
