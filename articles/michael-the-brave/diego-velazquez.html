Diego Velázquez

Spanish painter of the Golden Age, 1599–1660

Born in Seville and trained under Francisco Pacheco, whose daughter he married, Velázquez began with sombre, intensely realistic scenes of everyday life touched by Caravaggio’s naturalism. His talent carried him to Madrid, where at the age of twenty-four he became court painter to Philip IV. For the rest of his life he served that king, recording the royal family, dwarves, jesters, and courtiers with unflinching truthfulness and quiet dignity.

When Peter Paul Rubens visited Madrid in 1628 he urged the young Spaniard to study in Italy, and two journeys there transformed his art, deepening his command of colour learned from Titian and the Venetians. In Rome he painted Pope Innocent X with such ferocious penetration that the sitter is said to have muttered the portrait was “too true.”

His supreme achievement, Las Meninas of 1656, shows the painter himself at his easel before the little princess and her attendants, the king and queen glimpsed in a mirror—an endlessly analysed meditation on illusion, vision, and the act of painting. His loose, optical brushwork, seeming almost careless near to and miraculously alive at a distance, looked centuries ahead to the Impressionists. Ennobled as a knight of Santiago, he was revered by later masters from Goya to Manet, who called him simply “the painter of painters.”
