Alessandro Scarlatti

Founder of the Neapolitan school of opera, 1660–1725

Alessandro Scarlatti was the most influential Italian opera composer of his generation and is remembered as the founder of the Neapolitan school. Born in Palermo in 1660, he was sent as a youth to Rome, where his early gifts brought him to the notice of the exiled Queen Christina of Sweden, who made him her maestro di cappella and launched his career.

In 1684 he moved to Naples as music director to the Spanish viceroy, and for the next four decades he dominated the operatic life of that city, the rising capital of European song. He composed on a vast scale—more than a hundred operas, of which many are lost, alongside some six hundred chamber cantatas, numerous oratorios, and a large body of church music.

His historical importance lies less in any single work than in the forms he refined and handed on. He brought the da capo aria to its mature shape and standardized the three-part Italian overture, the sinfonia, that would later help give birth to the symphony. His operas balanced expressive melody with clear harmonic structure, setting the pattern that composers across the century would follow.

Moving between Naples and Rome, he remained close to the cardinals and academies that patronized Corelli and the young Handel, a central figure in the cosmopolitan musical world of his time. He died in Naples in 1725, his style already being overtaken by lighter fashions. His greatest legacy, however, was personal: his son, who would carry the family name to new heights at the keyboard, Domenico Scarlatti.
