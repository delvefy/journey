Dion of Syracuse

Syracusan statesman, c. 408–354 BCE

Dion of Syracuse was a Sicilian aristocrat and statesman whose career became the great test case of Plato's dream of a philosopher-king. A brother-in-law of the tyrant Dionysius I, he was a man of the first rank at the Syracusan court; but when Plato visited Sicily, Dion fell under his spell and embraced philosophy with a convert's zeal.

On the death of Dionysius I in 367 BCE, Dion hoped to guide the young successor, Dionysius II, toward just and lawful rule, and persuaded him to summon Plato to Syracuse to educate him. The experiment failed: the new tyrant grew jealous of Dion's influence, drove him into exile, and seized his property and his wife.

From exile Dion gathered a small band of mercenaries and, in 357 BCE, made a daring return, landing in Sicily and rousing the Syracusans to expel Dionysius II. For a few years he was master of the city, but his austere, high-handed manner alienated the very people he had freed, and the promised reign of philosophy dissolved into faction and disorder.

In 354 BCE Dion was assassinated by Callippus, an Athenian who had been his companion in the Academy. Plutarch, who wrote his life, paired him with the Roman Brutus as a high-minded man whose noble aims were undone by the harsh realities of power.
