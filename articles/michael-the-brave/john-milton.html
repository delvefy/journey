John Milton

English poet, author of Paradise Lost, 1608–1674

A prodigy of learning, Milton steeped himself in the classics, Italian, and Hebrew, and as a young man wrote exquisite poems—L’Allegro, Il Penseroso, the masque Comus, and the elegy Lycidas—and an epitaph for Shakespeare. On a continental tour he sought out the aged and blind Galileo under house arrest near Florence, an encounter he never forgot.

The Civil War turned him from poetry to polemic. He became the great prose champion of the Puritan and republican cause, defending divorce, a free press in the ringing Areopagitica, and—most dangerously—the execution of Charles I. As Latin Secretary to Oliver Cromwell’s Commonwealth he conducted its correspondence with the powers of Europe, even as his own eyesight failed entirely; he carried on his work wholly blind.

The Restoration of 1660 left him disgraced, briefly imprisoned, and in danger of his life, saved in part by friends among the new regime. Out of this darkness and defeat came his masterpiece. Dictated in his blindness, Paradise Lost (1667) retold the fall of man and the rebellion of Satan in sonorous blank verse of unequalled grandeur, seeking to “justify the ways of God to men.” He followed it with Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes before his death in 1674, leaving English its greatest epic.
