Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

German writer and statesman, 1749–1832

Goethe was the towering figure of German literature and one of the last great universal men. Born in Frankfurt and trained in law, he became famous across Europe while still in his twenties with The Sorrows of Young Werther, a novel of doomed romantic feeling that set off a wave of imitation and even of suicides, and helped launch the stormy Sturm und Drang movement.

Invited to the small court of Weimar by its young duke, he settled there for the rest of his long life, serving as a minister and privy councillor while continuing to write. A journey to Italy renewed his devotion to classical ideals, and his deep friendship and collaboration with Friedrich Schiller produced the golden age known as Weimar Classicism. Their partnership shaped German drama, poetry, and criticism for a generation.

His crowning achievement was Faust, the drama of a scholar who bargains with the devil, which he worked on for most of his life and completed only shortly before his death; it stands among the supreme works of world literature. He also wrote novels, lyric poetry, and an autobiography, and pursued science seriously, advancing studies of plants and a theory of colour. When the conquering Napoleon met him he is said to have exclaimed, “Here is a man!” By the time of his death Goethe was revered throughout Europe as the embodiment of literary and humane genius.
