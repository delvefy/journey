Pedro Álvares Cabral

Portuguese commander, first European to land in Brazil, c. 1467–1520

Pedro Álvares Cabral was born around 1467 in Belmonte, in the Serra da Estrela, the second son of the alcaide-mor Fernão Cabral. The family was well-connected at court; in the 1490s the young Cabral served King John II as a knight of the king’s council, married the wealthy heiress Isabel de Castro, and on John’s death continued in royal service under Manuel I. He had never commanded a fleet when, in 1499, after the triumphant return of Vasco da Gama, Manuel I chose him to lead the second and far larger Portuguese expedition to India.

Thirteen ships and some 1,200 men sailed from Lisbon on 9 March 1500. Cabral followed da Gama’s wide volta do mar but swung further west, and on 22 April his lookout sighted a flat-topped mountain that he named Monte Pascoal. The fleet anchored at what he called Porto Seguro and spent ten days ashore in friendly contact with the Tupiniquim. He sent one ship home with the news, raised a wooden cross, and turned east for the Cape of Good Hope. The chronicler Pero Vaz de Caminha’s letter to King Manuel, written from this beach and locked away in the Lisbon archive until the eighteenth century, is the founding document of Brazilian history.

Off the Cape in May 1500 a sudden storm wrecked four ships including that of Bartolomeu Dias. The remaining vessels reached Mozambique, then Calicut, where Cabral established the first Portuguese feitoria. Within weeks Muslim merchants attacked the post and killed most of its garrison; Cabral retaliated by bombarding Calicut and seizing ten Arab ships. He sailed south to Cochin, signed an alliance with its raja against the Zamorin, and returned to Lisbon in July 1501 with six survivors of his fleet, a hold full of pepper, and a story of accidental empire. He never sailed again. Passed over for the next India command, he retired to Santarém, where he died in obscurity around 1520.

The road continues to Manuel I of Portugal.
