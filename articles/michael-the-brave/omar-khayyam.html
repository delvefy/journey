Omar Khayyam

Persian mathematician, astronomer and poet, 1048–1131

Omar Khayyam was born in 1048 at Nishapur in Khorasan, and made his early reputation as a mathematician of the first rank. In his treatise on algebra he classified cubic equations and solved them by the elegant device of intersecting conic sections, going well beyond the algebra inherited from al-Khwarizmi; and in a searching commentary on Euclid he probed the troublesome parallel postulate, edging toward ideas that would not be fully grasped until the rise of non-Euclidean geometry centuries later.

His talents won the patronage of the vizier Nizam al-Mulk and the Seljuk sultan Malik-Shah, who set him at the head of a team of astronomers at an observatory in Isfahan. There he carried out a reform of the Persian calendar, the Jalali reckoning of 1079, whose measurement of the solar year rivalled and even surpassed the accuracy of the Gregorian calendar devised in Europe five hundred years afterward.

Yet to most of the world Khayyam is remembered not as a geometer but as a poet, the supposed author of the Rubaiyat, a loose gathering of quatrains brooding with wry skepticism on wine, mortality and the silence of the heavens. How many of them he actually wrote is much disputed, but the verses gathered under his name found astonishing fame in the nineteenth century through Edward FitzGerald’s free English rendering, which turned a medieval Persian sage into a household voice of melancholy pleasure. Khayyam died at Nishapur in 1131.
