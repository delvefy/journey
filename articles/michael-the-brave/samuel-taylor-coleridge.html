Samuel Taylor Coleridge

1772–1834, English poet, critic and philosopher

Samuel Taylor Coleridge was born at Ottery St Mary in Devon, the youngest of ten children of a country vicar and grammar-school master. After his father's sudden death he was sent at nine to Christ's Hospital in London, where he met the lifelong friend Charles Lamb. At Jesus College, Cambridge, he distinguished himself by political radicalism, ran up debts, briefly enlisted in the dragoons under a false name, and left without a degree.

With Robert Southey he laid plans in 1794 for “Pantisocracy,” an egalitarian commune on the Susquehanna. The scheme collapsed but left him married to Sara Fricker, Southey's sister-in-law. In 1797, settled at Nether Stowey in Somerset, he met William Wordsworth and his sister Dorothy. The next twelve months produced “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner,” the first “Christabel,” “Frost at Midnight,” and the opium-vision fragment “Kubla Khan,” and the joint Lyrical Ballads with Wordsworth that opened English Romantic poetry.

From 1798 he travelled in Germany, learning the language and immersing himself in the new idealist philosophy of Immanuel Kant and Friedrich Schelling. He translated Schiller's Wallenstein, lectured on Shakespeare and Milton in London, and from 1810 quarrelled with Wordsworth. His later Biographia Literaria (1817), Lay Sermons, On the Constitution of the Church and State, and the Aids to Reflection turned English thought toward German metaphysics and away from John Locke; through them he made deep marks on Edmund Burke's heirs and on later figures from John Stuart Mill to J. H. Newman.

An addiction to laudanum dogged the second half of his life. From 1816 the surgeon James Gillman housed him in Highgate, where he held court with disciples and dazzled visitors among them Lord Byron and the young John Keats. He died there in July 1834, leaving the Notebooks and the Table Talk to be published posthumously.

The road continues to Charles Lamb.
