William Wallace

Guardian of Scotland, c. 1270–1305

William Wallace was born around 1270, probably near Paisley, a younger son of the minor Scottish gentry. He emerges into record abruptly in 1297, after Edward I of England had deposed King John Balliol and placed Scotland under occupation. In May of that year Wallace killed William Heselrig, the English sheriff of Lanark, an act that made him the focus of a popular rising in the south and west while Andrew Moray led a parallel revolt in the north.

The two leaders joined forces, and on 11 September 1297 they destroyed an English army at Stirling Bridge, catching it half across the narrow crossing of the Forth. Moray died of wounds soon after; Wallace was knighted and made sole Guardian of Scotland in the name of the absent Balliol. His authority lasted less than a year. On 22 July 1298 Edward I returned in person and broke the Scottish schiltrons at Falkirk with massed archery, and Wallace resigned the guardianship.

He spent the following years as a fugitive and a diplomat, travelling, it is believed, to the French court and perhaps to the papal Curia to plead Scotland’s case. Betrayed near Glasgow in August 1305, he was taken to London, condemned at Westminster Hall as a traitor—though he denied ever having sworn fealty to the English king—and on 23 August hanged, drawn and quartered, his head set on London Bridge. In failure he became something larger than he had been in life: the enduring emblem of Scottish independence, his memory kept alive by the minstrel Blind Harry and, centuries later, by the wider world.
