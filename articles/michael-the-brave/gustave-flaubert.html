Gustave Flaubert

French novelist, author of Madame Bovary, 1821–1880

Flaubert was the supreme craftsman of the French novel, an obsessive perfectionist who pursued le mot juste—the exactly right word—with monastic devotion. Independently wealthy and famously reclusive, he spent his life at his family home near Rouen, agonising for days over a single sentence, reading his prose aloud to test its rhythm, and grinding out his books at the rate of a few pages a week.

His first published novel, Madame Bovary (1857), the story of a provincial doctor’s wife whose romantic illusions and adulteries lead to her ruin, was prosecuted for offending public morals; the acquittal made him famous overnight. With its cool, impersonal narration and merciless precision, it became a founding monument of literary realism. He laboured for years over the historical novel Salammbô and the bitter Sentimental Education, and a model of the short story in A Simple Heart.

For all his solitude, Flaubert was a generous friend and mentor. He carried on a celebrated correspondence with George Sand, counted the Russian Ivan Turgenev among his intimates, and presided over a Sunday circle of younger writers—Émile Zola, Alphonse Daudet, and his beloved disciple Guy de Maupassant—to whom he passed on his exacting standards. He died suddenly in 1880, revered as a writer’s writer.
