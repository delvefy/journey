Charles Perrault

French author of fairy tales, 1628–1703

Born in Paris into a prosperous bourgeois family, Perrault trained in the law but soon abandoned the bar for letters and administration. His talents brought him into the service of Jean-Baptiste Colbert, Louis XIV’s great minister, who made him a trusted lieutenant in cultural affairs. As controller-general of the king’s buildings he helped shape the artistic policy of the reign, served on the committee that devised inscriptions and devices glorifying the monarch, and was admitted to the Académie française in 1671.

Within the Académie he became the standard-bearer of the “Moderns” in the celebrated Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns. In poems and in his dialogues Parallèle des Anciens et des Modernes he argued that the writers and inventors of his own age had surpassed those of Greece and Rome, drawing the indignant opposition of Boileau and of his old acquaintance La Fontaine, champions of the Ancients.

His enduring fame, however, rests on a slim volume published in 1697, the Histoires ou contes du temps passé, better known by its frontispiece motto as the Tales of Mother Goose. Drawing on popular oral tradition, he gave lasting literary form to Cinderella, Sleeping Beauty, Little Red Riding Hood, Puss in Boots, Bluebeard, and Hop o’ My Thumb, each rounded off with a sly verse moral. In doing so he founded the modern literary fairy tale and bequeathed to the world some of its most beloved stories.

The road continues to Jean-Baptiste Colbert.
