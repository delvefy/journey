Piero della Francesca

Painter and mathematician, c.1415–1492

Piero was born about 1415 at Borgo Sansepolcro, a small upper-Tiber town on the road between Florence and Urbino, the son of a shoemaker and wool dealer. He had a workshop training that he ran in his head as much as in his hands: he was as good a geometer as any in Italy, and his three Latin treatises — De prospectiva pingendi on perspective, the Libellus de quinque corporibus regularibus on the Platonic solids, and the Trattato d’abaco on commercial mathematics — survived as the textbooks his successors learnt from. He met the new Florentine painting in 1439, when as a young assistant he worked beside Domenico Veneziano on the lost frescoes in the choir of Sant’Egidio, the year the Council of Florence brought the Greeks across the Apennines.

His commissions came from the small courts of central Italy. The Misericordia altarpiece at Sansepolcro (1445–62) for his own town set the manner: figures gathered in symmetry around the Madonna, the shadow falling from a single high source, the geometry working under the religion. The Baptism of Christ for the Camaldolese at Sansepolcro, around 1450, is a pellucid Tiber landscape in which the dove and the tilted patens echo the round of the tree-trunks. For Sigismondo Malatesta of Rimini he frescoed a side chapel of the Tempio Malatestiano in 1451. The cycle of the Legend of the True Cross in San Francesco at Arezzo, finished about 1466, with its Solomonic queens and Heraclian battles, is the central work of the quattrocento.

The court of Federico da Montefeltro at Urbino was his last great patron. The Brera altarpiece, with Federico kneeling beside an oddly suspended ostrich egg; the diptych portrait in profile of the duke and his late wife Battista Sforza, with the long landscapes of the Marche behind their heads; the Flagellation, a small panel of unaccountable strangeness — these were painted at Urbino through the 1470s. He went blind in his last years, dictated his treatises to his pupil Marco di Longaro, and died at Sansepolcro on 12 October 1492, the day Columbus stepped ashore on Guanahaní. His mathematical writing passed to Luca Pacioli, his friend, who incorporated much of it in his Divina proportione of 1509, with diagrams by Leonardo.

The road continues to Leon Battista Alberti.
