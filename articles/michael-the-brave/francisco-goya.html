Francisco Goya

Spanish painter and printmaker, 1746–1828

Born to modest circumstances in rural Aragon, Goya rose to become first painter to the Spanish crown, designing tapestry cartoons and producing grand royal portraits—among them a famously unflattering group of the family of Charles IV. He revered the old masters whose works hung in the royal collections, naming Velázquez and Rembrandt, alongside Nature herself, as his only teachers, and he etched copies after Velázquez to learn his secrets.

A devastating illness around 1793 left him stone deaf, and his art turned inward and bitter. In the etched series Los Caprichos he lashed the follies, superstition, and cruelty of his society with savage imagination, giving visual form to the maxim that the sleep of reason produces monsters.

When Napoleon’s armies invaded Spain, Goya witnessed the horrors of the Peninsular War and made of them some of the most powerful protest art ever created: The Third of May 1808, with its terrified victims before a faceless firing squad, and the unflinching prints of The Disasters of War. In old age, alone and embittered, he covered the walls of his house with the nightmarish “Black Paintings,” including the appalling Saturn Devouring His Son. He died in exile in France, a solitary bridge between the age of the old masters and the modern world he seemed to foresee.
