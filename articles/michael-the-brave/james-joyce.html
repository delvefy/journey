James Joyce

Irish modernist writer, 1882–1941

Joyce was born in Dublin and given a rigorous Jesuit education that he would later both honour and reject. Chafing against the Catholic Church and what he saw as the paralysis of Irish life, he left Ireland as a young man with Nora Barnacle and spent the rest of his life in continental exile, in Trieste, Zurich, and Paris. Yet Dublin, rendered with obsessive precision, remained the sole subject of his art.

His early books, the short-story collection Dubliners (1914) and the autobiographical novel A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man (1916), traced the awakening of conscience and vocation in a young Irishman much like himself. They established him as a master of realist detail and of the moment of sudden insight he called the "epiphany."

His masterpiece, Ulysses (1922), compresses a single ordinary day in Dublin into a vast experimental novel whose eighteen episodes are mapped onto Homer’s Odyssey, following the advertising canvasser Leopold Bloom as a modern Odysseus. With its stream-of-consciousness interior monologue and dazzling shifts of style, the book was prosecuted for obscenity yet transformed the possibilities of fiction. He pushed language still further in Finnegans Wake (1939), a dream-narrative built from multilingual puns. More than almost any writer, Joyce remade the modern novel.
