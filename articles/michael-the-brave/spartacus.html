Spartacus

Leader of the great slave revolt against Rome, died 71 BC

Spartacus was a Thracian who served, deserted, and was eventually enslaved and sold to a gladiatorial school at Capua. In 73 BC he led a breakout of fellow gladiators, who armed themselves with kitchen tools and seized weapons as they fled to the slopes of Mount Vesuvius. What began as a desperate escape swelled into one of the most serious uprisings Rome ever faced, as enslaved farmworkers and herdsmen flocked to his banner until the rebel host numbered in the tens of thousands.

For two years Spartacus proved a gifted commander, defeating one Roman force after another, including the armies of both consuls of 72 BC. He led his followers the length of Italy, apparently hoping to break out over the Alps so that they might scatter to their homelands. But the great army turned back south—its motives are debated—and Rome at last took the threat seriously.

Marcus Licinius Crassus was given command and pressed the rebels hard, hemming them into the toe of Italy. In 71 BC Spartacus fell in a final, hopeless battle; his body was never identified. Some six thousand of his captured followers were crucified along the Appian Way, while Pompey, returning from Spain, intercepted survivors and claimed a share of the credit. Rome treated the war as a shameful episode best forgotten, and most of what is known comes from later historians such as Plutarch and Appian. In time, however, Spartacus was remembered very differently—as an enduring symbol of the struggle of the oppressed against their masters.
