Marcel Proust

French novelist, 1871–1922

Proust was born near Paris into a prosperous family; his father was a distinguished physician. Chronically asthmatic and frail, he was as a young man a fixture of the fashionable salons of the capital, observing with a satirist’s eye the aristocrats and social climbers who would later fill his fiction. In these years he wrote elegant sketches and translated the English critic John Ruskin, but seemed to many a mere dilettante.

The death of his beloved mother in 1905 deepened his retreat from society. Withdrawing into a cork-lined bedroom to shut out noise and dust, he poured his remaining years into a single vast work, In Search of Lost Time, published in seven volumes between 1913 and 1927. The first, Swann’s Way, appeared at his own expense after publishers, the novelist André Gide among them, had turned it down.

The novel follows its narrator from childhood to middle age, anatomizing memory, desire, jealousy, art, and the slow social revolution that unseated the old aristocracy. Its most famous passage, in which the taste of a madeleine dipped in tea suddenly resurrects a lost world, gave the language the idea of involuntary memory. The second volume won the Prix Goncourt in 1919, bringing Proust belated fame. He died in 1922 with the final volumes still unrevised, leaving behind one of the supreme achievements of modern literature.
