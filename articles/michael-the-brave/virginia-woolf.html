Virginia Woolf

English modernist novelist, 1882–1941

Born Virginia Stephen in London, she was the daughter of the eminent man of letters Leslie Stephen and educated largely in his vast library. After her parents’ deaths the Stephen children settled in the Bloomsbury district, where their gatherings of artists, writers, and thinkers grew into the Bloomsbury Group. In 1912 she married the writer Leonard Woolf, and in 1917 the couple founded the Hogarth Press, which printed her own books along with works by T. S. Eliot and the English translations of Sigmund Freud.

Woolf became one of the foremost innovators of the modernist novel. In Mrs Dalloway (1925), To the Lighthouse (1927), and The Waves (1931) she abandoned conventional plot for the inner flow of consciousness, tracing the play of memory, perception, and time across her characters’ minds. Orlando (1928), a fantastical biography spanning centuries, was inspired by her intimate friend Vita Sackville-West.

She was also a penetrating essayist and a foundational figure of modern feminism. In A Room of One’s Own (1929) she argued that "a woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction," tracing how women had been shut out of literature by poverty and exclusion. Throughout her life she struggled with recurrent mental illness, and in 1941, fearing a final breakdown, she drowned herself in the River Ouse. Her experimental art and her essays have made her one of the most influential writers of the twentieth century.
