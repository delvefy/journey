Thomas More

English lawyer, humanist, Lord Chancellor, 1478–1535

More was born in Milk Street, London, on 7 February 1478, the son of the judge Sir John More. He was placed as a boy in the household of Cardinal John Morton, sent to Oxford at fourteen, and then back to London to train at Lincoln’s Inn. For a time he lived among the Carthusians at the Charterhouse, debating a vocation, before settling on the law and marriage. By 1504 he was a member of parliament; by 1510, under-sheriff of London; by 1515, on the trade embassy to Bruges and Antwerp from which he came home with the manuscript of Utopia.

Utopia, published in Louvain in 1516 under his old friend Erasmus’s editorial care, gave the English language one of its most durable coinages and the Renaissance one of its sharpest pieces of political imagination: a Latin dialogue describing a commonwealth without private property, with religious toleration and short working days, set against the cruel agrarian England of the enclosures. The book made him European. Henry VIII, who had cultivated him from his accession, drew him further into court service—privy councillor in 1518, knighthood in 1521, Speaker of the House of Commons in 1523. He prosecuted Lutherans and the smugglers of Tyndale’s English Bible with conviction.

In October 1529 the king made him Lord Chancellor in succession to the fallen Cardinal Wolsey. For nearly three years More tried to be a Catholic Chancellor under a king pulling away from Rome over the divorce from Catherine of Aragon. In May 1532 he resigned the great seal rather than subscribe to the bishops’ submission to royal supremacy. He refused the oath demanded by the Act of Succession in April 1534, was imprisoned in the Tower, tried on perjured evidence in July 1535, and beheaded on Tower Hill on the sixth of that month. He told the watching crowd that he died “the king’s good servant, but God’s first.” The Catholic Church canonised him in 1935.
