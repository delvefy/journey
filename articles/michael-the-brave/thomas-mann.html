Thomas Mann

German novelist, Nobel laureate, 1875–1955

Born into a patrician merchant family of Lübeck, Mann turned the decline of just such a house into his first great novel, Buddenbrooks, published when he was only twenty-five and later singled out by the Nobel committee. The tension that runs through all his work—between the comfortable burgher and the doomed, fascinating artist—found its sharpest expression in the novella Death in Venice, the story of a famous writer destroyed by his obsession with beauty.

His monumental The Magic Mountain, set in an Alpine sanatorium, weighed the warring ideas of a Europe about to collapse into war. Conservative and nationalist in his youth, Mann was converted by the experience of the 1920s into an eloquent champion of reason and democracy. When the Nazis came to power he went into exile, settling at last in California, and broadcast defiant messages to his countrymen over the BBC.

In exile he completed the biblical tetralogy Joseph and His Brothers and the dark allegory Doctor Faustus, in which a composer sells his soul for genius as Germany sells hers to barbarism. A lifelong wrestler with the German inheritance of Wagner, Schopenhauer, and Goethe, he drew perhaps most deeply on the philosopher whose vision of art and decadence haunted his pages, Friedrich Nietzsche. Mann died in Zurich in 1955.
