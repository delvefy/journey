Herodotus

Greek historian, c. 484–425 BCE

Herodotus of Halicarnassus, whom Cicero later named the Father of History, wrote the first great prose narrative of the past in the Western tradition. Born around 484 BCE in Halicarnassus, a Greek city of Caria then under Persian rule, he traveled extensively—through Egypt, the Levant, Babylonia, the Black Sea coast, and the Greek world—gathering the materials for his life's work.

That work, the Histories, set out to explain the great conflict between the Greeks and the Persian Empire and to preserve the memory of the deeds of both. He traced the rise of Persia under Cyrus, Cambyses, and Darius, then built toward the climactic invasions: the repulse of Darius's forces at Marathon and the vast expedition of Xerxes, with its battles at Thermopylae, Salamis, and Plataea. Around these events he wove long digressions on the geography and customs of Egypt, Scythia, and many other peoples.

His method—announced as historie, or inquiry—was to collect competing accounts, report them, and often weigh their plausibility, though he freely preserved marvels and hearsay alongside sober fact, which won him both his proud title and the rival charge of being the father of lies. He recited portions of his work in public, spent time in the Athens of Pericles, and was associated with the founding of the colony of Thurii in southern Italy; ancient tradition links him in friendship with the tragedian Sophocles.

By shaping scattered oral traditions into a continuous, cause-seeking narrative on a grand theme, Herodotus made history a literary and intellectual discipline—the very word descends from his Greek. He died around 425 BCE, and his Histories remain our chief source for the Persian Wars and a foundational text of European prose.
