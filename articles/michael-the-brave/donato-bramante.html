Donato Bramante

Architect of the High Renaissance, c. 1444–1514

Bramante was born around 1444 near Urbino, in the duchy then ruled by Federico da Montefeltro, and trained first as a painter in a region shaped by Piero della Francesca and Andrea Mantegna. By the mid-1470s he had moved to Milan, where he spent some twenty years in the service of the Sforza dukes. There he turned decisively to architecture. At Santa Maria presso San Satiro he faked a deep choir on a shallow site with a famous painted perspective, and at Santa Maria delle Grazie he raised the great domed crossing in whose refectory Leonardo da Vinci, his friend and fellow courtier, painted the Last Supper.

When the French drove Ludovico Sforza from Milan in 1499, Bramante went to Rome and immersed himself in the measured study of ancient ruins. The fruit was the Tempietto at San Pietro in Montorio, a small circular temple of perfect gravity that became the textbook emblem of the mature Renaissance manner. Under Pope Julius II he was made the pope’s architect, laying out the vast Cortile del Belvedere to link the Vatican palace to the villa above, and, on 18 April 1506, setting the first stone of an entirely new Saint Peter’s, conceived as a colossal domed Greek cross.

He died in Rome in 1514 with the basilica only begun; its piers and arches stood, but the design passed through Raphael — a kinsman from the Urbino district whom Bramante had drawn to the papal court — and others before Michelangelo recast the dome. Bramante’s authority over proportion, the antique orders, and monumental space set the standard against which the next century of building measured itself.
