John F. Kennedy

1917–1963, thirty-fifth President of the United States

John Fitzgerald Kennedy was born in Brookline, Massachusetts, the second son of Joseph P. Kennedy, a Boston financier and later United States ambassador to London, and Rose Fitzgerald, daughter of the city's mayor. He grew up in a competitive Catholic Irish-American household, attended Choate and Harvard, and turned his senior thesis on British appeasement into the 1940 bestseller Why England Slept. Frail and often ill, he nonetheless commanded the patrol torpedo boat PT-109 in the Solomon Islands in 1943; after a Japanese destroyer cut it in half he led the surviving crew through fifteen hours in the sea to a tiny island, an episode for which he received the Navy and Marine Corps Medal.

The death of his elder brother Joseph Jr. transferred the family's political hopes to him. He won a Massachusetts House seat in 1946, the Senate in 1952, and the Democratic nomination in 1960 after a string of primary victories that proved a Catholic could carry Protestant states. In the first televised presidential debates he edged Richard Nixon on poise alone, and in November he became the youngest man ever elected and the first Catholic president.

His thousand-day presidency was crowded. The Bay of Pigs landing of April 1961 ended in fiasco, but in October 1962 he stared down Nikita Khrushchev during the Cuban Missile Crisis and forced the withdrawal of Soviet rockets in exchange for a secret pullback of Jupiters from Turkey. He launched the Peace Corps, set the country the goal of putting a man on the moon by 1970, signed the Limited Test Ban Treaty, sent federal marshals into Mississippi and Alabama to enforce desegregation, and prepared the Civil Rights Bill that Lyndon Johnson would later sign. On 22 November 1963 he was shot dead in Dallas while riding in a motorcade beside his wife Jacqueline; Lee Harvey Oswald was arrested within hours and himself murdered two days later, leaving an inheritance of unresolved questions that still divides American memory.
