Vincent van Gogh

Dutch Post-Impressionist painter, 1853–1890

Vincent van Gogh was born in 1853 at Zundert in the Dutch Brabant, the son of a Reformed pastor. He drifted through trades—art dealer in The Hague, London and Paris, schoolmaster in England, lay missionary among the Borinage coal miners—before deciding, at twenty-seven, to make himself a painter. His brother Theo, a dealer in Paris, kept him alive financially and by letter for the next decade.

His early Dutch work, culminating in The Potato Eaters of 1885, was dark, peasant in subject, and openly modelled on Jean-François Millet. In Paris in 1886 he met the Impressionists and the Japanese print, and his palette caught fire. He moved south to Arles in 1888 hoping to found an artists' community in the Provencal sun and produced in two short years the canvases on which his fame rests—the Sunflowers, the Bedroom, the Yellow House, the Night Café, the great series of starry nights and wheat fields, all painted at desperate speed in thick, vehement strokes of pure colour.

That autumn Paul Gauguin came to live with him. The visit ended in nine bitter weeks and the celebrated breakdown in which Vincent severed part of his own ear. He committed himself to the asylum of Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, where he painted The Starry Night, then moved north to Auvers-sur-Oise under the care of Dr Gachet, and in July 1890 shot himself in a wheat field. He had sold, in his life, barely a single picture.

The letters to Theo, recovered and published, made him the archetype of the modern artist as visionary outsider, and his colour and brushwork opened the path of the next century. The friend whose visit to Arles ended in catastrophe, and whose own art took an utterly different road, was Paul Gauguin.
