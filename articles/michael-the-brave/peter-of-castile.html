Peter of Castile

King of Castile, “the Cruel”, 1334–1369

Peter, son of Alfonso XI, became king of Castile and León in 1350 and ruled with a severity that earned him from his enemies the name “the Cruel” and from his partisans “the Just.” He struck down nobles and rivals without mercy, set aside his French bride Blanche of Bourbon — who later died in his custody — for his beloved María de Padilla, and so gave France a grievance that would prove fatal. Above all he faced the deadly enmity of his many illegitimate half-brothers, chief among them Henry of Trastámara.

Their quarrel drew in the whole of western Europe and became a front of the Hundred Years’ War. With French gold and the mercenary Free Companies led by Bertrand du Guesclin, Henry drove Peter from his kingdom in 1366. Peter fled to the English in Aquitaine, and Edward the Black Prince took up his cause, marching into Spain and shattering Henry’s army at Nájera in 1367. But Peter could not or would not pay his costly allies; the disillusioned English withdrew, their prince carrying home the sickness that would kill him.

Henry came back at once, backed again by France and Charles V. In 1369, defeated at Montiel, Peter was lured into his rival’s camp and killed by Henry’s own hand, and with him ended the senior line of Castile; Henry II founded the dynasty of Trastámara. Yet Peter’s blood ran on through his daughters, who married John of Gaunt and Edmund of Langley, sons of Edward III of England, carrying a claim to Castile into the English royal house. Chaucer, mourning his fall, called him the “glory of Spain.”

The road continues to Bertrand du Guesclin.
