Gautama Buddha

Founder of Buddhism, c. 5th century BCE

Siddhartha Gautama was born around the sixth or fifth century BCE at Lumbini, in the foothills of the Himalayas, a prince of the Shakya clan. By tradition his father, the ruler Suddhodana, sheltered him from all knowledge of suffering amid the pleasures of the palace. But venturing out, the young prince met an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering ascetic—the “Four Sights”—and was shaken to the core by the realities of age, sickness, and death.

Abandoning his wife, infant son, and royal inheritance, he left to seek liberation. For years he studied under teachers and practised severe self-mortification, until, near starvation, he turned from extreme asceticism to a “Middle Way.” Seating himself beneath a tree at Bodh Gaya, he resolved not to rise until he understood the cause of suffering, and there attained enlightenment, becoming the Buddha, the “Awakened One.”

At the Deer Park near Benares he preached his first sermon, “setting in motion the wheel of the Dharma.” He taught the Four Noble Truths—that life is pervaded by suffering, that suffering arises from craving, that it can be extinguished, and that the way is the Noble Eightfold Path—leading at last to nirvana and the end of rebirth. Around him grew the sangha, a community of monks and nuns.

For some forty-five years he wandered the cities and kingdoms of the Ganges plain, gathering disciples such as Ananda and winning the patronage of kings, teaching a path open to people of every caste. He died around his eightieth year at Kushinagar, passing into final nirvana. From his teaching grew one of the world's great religions, which would spread across Asia and, through patrons such as the emperor Ashoka, far beyond the land of its birth.
