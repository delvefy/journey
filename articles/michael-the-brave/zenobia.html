Zenobia

Queen of Palmyra, c. 240–after 274 AD

Zenobia was the second wife of Odaenathus, the prince of Palmyra who, as Rome reeled from the capture of the emperor Valerian by Persia, had made himself the dominant power in the Roman East. When Odaenathus and his eldest son were assassinated around 267, Zenobia seized control as regent for her young son Vaballathus, and proved a ruler of formidable ambition and ability.

Cultured, multilingual, and said to have claimed descent from the Ptolemies of Egypt, she modelled herself consciously on Cleopatra and gathered learned men about her court, among them the philosopher Cassius Longinus. From Palmyra she extended her power until, by 270, she controlled Syria, much of Asia Minor, and Egypt—the grain supply of Rome itself.

At first she ruled nominally in Rome's name, but soon she cast off the pretence, granting herself and her son the imperial titles of Augusta and Augustus and striking coins in their names. Such open defiance could not be tolerated. In 272 the emperor Aurelian marched east, defeated her armies at Immae and Emesa, and laid siege to Palmyra.

Zenobia fled toward Persia to seek aid but was overtaken and captured near the Euphrates. Aurelian spared her and brought her to Rome, where, by tradition, she was displayed in his triumph of 274, bound in golden chains. Her ultimate fate is uncertain; the kindest account holds that she was pardoned and lived out her days as a Roman noblewoman near Tibur. Brief though her empire was, she became one of the most celebrated queens of antiquity.
