Muhammad Ali

American heavyweight boxing champion, 1942–2016

Muhammad Ali was born Cassius Marcellus Clay Jr. on 17 January 1942 in Louisville, Kentucky, the elder son of a sign painter. The story he liked to tell was that he started boxing at twelve, after a white policeman named Joe Martin found him crying about a stolen bicycle and offered to teach him to fight back. He won six Kentucky Golden Gloves titles and, at eighteen, the light-heavyweight gold medal at the Rome Olympics in 1960.

Four years later, at twenty-two and a seven-to-one underdog, he took the heavyweight championship of the world from Sonny Liston in Miami Beach. Within days he announced that he had joined the Nation of Islam under the name Muhammad Ali. In April 1967 he refused induction for the Vietnam War — “I ain’t got no quarrel with them Viet Cong” — and was stripped of his title and his boxing licence at the height of his powers. The three and a half years of exile that followed cost him what should have been the best fights of his career and made him, more than any prizefighter before him, a political figure.

The Supreme Court overturned his conviction in 1971. The fights that followed remained the standard against which heavyweight boxing measured itself for a generation: the “Fight of the Century” against Joe Frazier in 1971; the “Rumble in the Jungle” against George Foreman in Kinshasa in 1974, where he absorbed Foreman’s punches on the ropes until the champion exhausted himself; and the “Thrilla in Manila” against Frazier in 1975. He became the first man to win the heavyweight title three times. Parkinson’s syndrome closed in on him from 1984; he lit the Olympic flame in Atlanta in 1996, hands trembling, and died at Scottsdale, Arizona, on 3 June 2016.
