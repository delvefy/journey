Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

German polymath, mathematician, and philosopher, 1646–1716

Leibniz was perhaps the last universal genius, contributing to mathematics, philosophy, physics, law, history, theology, and diplomacy. Largely self-taught from his father’s library, he spent his career in the service of German princes, above all the House of Hanover, for whom he worked as councillor, librarian, and historian while carrying on a vast correspondence with scholars across Europe.

Independently of Newton he developed the calculus, and the elegant notation he devised, including the integral sign and the form of the derivative, is the one still used today. The two men’s supporters fought a long and bitter quarrel over who had invented it first. He also worked out the binary arithmetic that underlies modern computing, built a calculating machine, and pursued the dream of a universal logical language in which disputes could be settled by calculation.

In philosophy he held that the universe is composed of simple, indivisible units he called monads, and that God, choosing among all possibilities, had created the best of all possible worlds. This optimism, set out in his Theodicy, was later mercilessly ridiculed by Voltaire. He defended his view of space and time as relations rather than absolutes in a famous correspondence with a follower of Newton. Though he died neglected, with few mourners at his funeral, his ideas in logic, metaphysics, and mathematics proved astonishingly fertile, and later ages recognised him as one of the supreme intellects of the modern world.

The road continues to Voltaire.
