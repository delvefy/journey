Mehmed II

Ottoman Sultan, Fātiḥ — the Conqueror, 1432–1481

Mehmed was born on 30 March 1432 at Edirne, the third son of Murad II by the slave concubine Hüma Hatun. His elder brothers Ahmed and Ali both died young, leaving him the heir, and his father abdicated in his favour during the summer of 1444 only to come back six weeks later to fight the crusaders at Varna. The two abdications, the failed siege of Constantinople by his grandfather Bayezid, and the long humiliation of paying Greek tribute set the obsession of his life. He came to the throne in his own right at Edirne on 18 February 1451; he was nineteen, and the European chanceries, misjudging him, signed renewals of the old treaties on flattering terms.

The conquest of Constantinople occupied the year and a half between. He built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı at the narrowest neck of the Bosphorus in the spring of 1452, cutting Black Sea grain off from the city. The Hungarian gun-founder Orban, refused by the Byzantines on grounds of cost, cast for him the great bombards that pounded the land walls. The siege began on 6 April 1453; the boom across the Golden Horn was bypassed by hauling galleys overland on greased rollers; before dawn on 29 May the assault carried the Saint Romanus gate, and by midday Constantinople was his. He rode to Hagia Sophia, ordered the Friday prayer recited there, and confirmed the Greek patriarch Gennadius II in office as head of a Christian millet under the sultan’s protection.

The next twenty-eight years extended the empire on every front. He absorbed the Latin duchy of Athens and the despotates of the Morea (1460), conquered the empire of Trebizond on the Pontic shore (1461), Bosnia (1463), and the khanate of Crimea as a vassal (1475). His fleets harried Venice the length of the Aegean; the long Ottoman-Venetian war of 1463–79 cost the republic Negroponte and Scutari and ended in tribute. In 1480 his troops landed at Otranto in the heel of Italy. He died on 3 May 1481, perhaps poisoned, at Maltepe on the road into Anatolia for another campaign. Beyond the wars, he codified Ottoman law in the kanunname that bears his name, founded the Topkapı, and patronised the Venetian Gentile Bellini, whose portrait of him with the cap and the great nose hangs in the National Gallery.

The road continues to Constantine XI Palaiologos.
