Julius Caesar

Roman general and dictator, 100–44 BC

Gaius Julius Caesar was born in 100 BC into an old but unremarkable patrician family, and climbed the ladder of Roman office with a mixture of charm, debt and audacity. In 60 BC he bound himself to the two greatest men of the day, the general Pompey and the financier Crassus, in the informal pact later called the First Triumvirate, and used it to win the governorship of Gaul. There, over eight years of relentless campaigning recorded in his own lucid Commentaries, he conquered the whole of Gaul, twice crossed into Britain, and crushed the great revolt of Vercingetorix at Alesia.

His success made him too dangerous for the Senate to tolerate. Ordered to give up his army, he instead led it across the Rubicon in 49 BC—the die, he said, was cast—and plunged Rome into civil war. He defeated Pompey at Pharsalus, pursued the war to Egypt, where he restored Cleopatra to her throne and took her as his lover, and went on to stamp out the last resistance from Asia Minor to Spain, reporting one lightning victory in three words: veni, vidi, vici.

Master of the Roman world, he was made dictator for life. He reformed the calendar into the shape the West still broadly uses, relieved debt, and planned vast public works, but his accumulation of power and the suspicion that he meant to be king turned many against him. On the Ides of March, 44 BC, a conspiracy of senators that included his protégé Brutus cut him down at the foot of Pompey’s statue. His murder did not restore the Republic but destroyed it: out of the wars that followed rose his heir Octavian and the age of emperors. So completely did he embody supreme power that his very name became, in Kaiser and Tsar, a word for it.
