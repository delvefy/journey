Thomas Hobbes

English philosopher, 1588–1679

Thomas Hobbes was the founder of modern political philosophy and the boldest materialist of his century. Born in 1588 as the Spanish Armada approached—he liked to say his mother gave birth to twins, himself and fear—he was educated at Oxford and spent much of his life as tutor and secretary to the aristocratic Cavendish family, which gave him leisure, books, and travel on the Continent.

His mind matured late and dramatically. In his forties he discovered geometry in the pages of Euclid and conceived a lifelong ambition to make politics as certain as mathematics. He visited Galileo in Italy, absorbed the new science of matter in motion, and in Paris joined the circle of Mersenne alongside Gassendi and Descartes, to whose Meditations he wrote a set of sharp objections. He had already translated Thucydides, and would render Homer in his old age.

The English Civil War drove him into French exile, where he tutored the future Charles II and composed his masterpiece, Leviathan, in 1651. In it he argued that the natural condition of mankind is a war of all against all, in which life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short," and that men escape it only by a covenant surrendering their power to an absolute sovereign who alone can guarantee peace.

Denounced as an atheist and embroiled in furious disputes over free will, mathematics, and the vacuum, Hobbes nonetheless reshaped the study of the state. His early years had been spent as assistant to Francis Bacon, and his own social-contract idea would echo on through Locke and Rousseau.
