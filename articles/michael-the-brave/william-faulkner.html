William Faulkner

American novelist of Yoknapatawpha, 1897–1962

William Cuthbert Faulkner was born on 25 September 1897 at New Albany, in northern Mississippi, the eldest of four sons of a country railwayman. The family moved a few years later to Oxford, the seat of Lafayette County, where his great-grandfather — W. C. Falkner, “the Old Colonel” — had been a regional figure of legend; and Oxford, slightly disguised as Jefferson, Mississippi, would furnish almost everything he wrote. He dropped out of high school, enlisted in the Royal Canadian Air Force in the last weeks of the First World War, and attended the University of Mississippi as a special student. In New Orleans in 1924–25 he fell in with Sherwood Anderson, who pushed him toward fiction.

His first novels, Soldiers’ Pay and Mosquitoes, were apprentice work. With Sartoris in 1929 he discovered Yoknapatawpha County, the apocryphal little kingdom he would map with eighty-odd interconnected characters across a dozen books. Within four years he produced the central group — The Sound and the Fury (1929), As I Lay Dying (1930), Sanctuary (1931), Light in August (1932) — and in 1936 the consciously magnificent Absalom, Absalom!, perhaps the most ambitious American novel of its century. He sustained a household at Rowan Oak on the chronic income of intermittent Hollywood work, scripting To Have and Have Not and The Big Sleep for Howard Hawks; he drank heavily, suffered repeated falls, and remained a half-recluse in his hometown.

Recognition came late. The Nobel Prize for Literature in 1949 was given partly for the brief, ringing acceptance speech in which he declared that man would not merely endure but prevail. Two Pulitzers followed, for A Fable (1955) and posthumously for The Reivers (1963). He died of a heart attack on 6 July 1962 at a sanatorium in Byhalia, Mississippi, sixty-four years old.
