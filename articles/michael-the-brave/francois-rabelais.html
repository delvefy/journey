François Rabelais

French Renaissance writer, c.1494–1553

Rabelais was born in Touraine around 1494 and entered religious life, first as a Franciscan and then a Benedictine monk, before his restless curiosity carried him out of the cloister into the wider world of learning. He took up the study of medicine at Montpellier and practised as a physician at Lyon, all the while steeping himself in Greek and Latin and in the new humanist scholarship whose master, Erasmus, he revered as a spiritual father.

Writing under a playful anagram of his name, he poured this vast erudition into the comic chronicles of the giants Gargantua and Pantagruel, published from the 1530s. Bawdy, fantastical, and crammed with jokes, lists, and learning, the books mocked scholastic pedantry, monkish hypocrisy, and blind authority while exalting knowledge, appetite, and liberty, summed up in the rule of his ideal abbey of Thélème: "Do what you will." The theologians of the Sorbonne condemned them as obscene and impious.

From such dangers Rabelais was shielded by powerful friends, the cardinal Jean du Bellay, whom he served as physician on missions to Rome, and King Francis I himself, while the reform-minded Marguerite of Navarre lent her favour. He died in Paris in 1553, leaving a body of work so exuberant that his very name became an adjective; his richly inventive prose helped found the French literary language and was savoured by later readers from Montaigne onward.
