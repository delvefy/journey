Maimonides

Jewish philosopher and physician, 1138–1204

Moses ben Maimon, known to the Latin world as Maimonides and to Jewish tradition by the acronym Rambam, was born in 1138 in Córdoba, then a centre of learning in Muslim Spain. When the puritanical Almohads overran Andalusia and ended its tolerance, his family fled into years of wandering through Spain and North Africa before settling at last in Fustat, near Cairo. There he won fame as a physician, entering the service of the court of Saladin’s great vizier, and as Nagid he became the recognized head of Egypt’s Jewish community.

His learning reshaped Judaism. In the Mishneh Torah he organized the whole sprawling body of Jewish law into a single lucid code, and in his Commentary on the Mishnah he set out the Thirteen Principles of Faith that are still recited. But his most influential book was the Guide for the Perplexed, written for those torn between religion and reason, in which he laboured to harmonize the philosophy of Aristotle with the teachings of the Torah, insisting that scripture be read in the light of demonstrated truth.

Steeped in the Aristotelian tradition transmitted by the Islamic philosophers, Maimonides set the terms for centuries of debate. His rationalism provoked fierce controversy among Jews, yet it also crossed religious lines: Thomas Aquinas cited “Rabbi Moses” with respect in his own reconciliation of faith and reason, and Baruch Spinoza wrestled with him five centuries later. He died in 1204, mourned across the Jewish world, which remembered him in the saying that from Moses to Moses there arose none like Moses.
