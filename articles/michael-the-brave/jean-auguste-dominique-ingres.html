Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres

French Neoclassical painter, 1780–1867

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres was born in 1780 at Montauban in southern France, the son of a minor painter and sculptor. He entered the Toulouse academy at eleven and at seventeen the studio of Jacques-Louis David in Paris, where the doctrine of classical line and Roman moral subject were laid down as commandments. He won the Prix de Rome in 1801 and spent eighteen years in Italy, in Rome and then Florence, supporting himself with severe, almost photographic pencil portraits of French visitors.

Above any modern master he venerated Raphael, whom he revisited obsessively in his own compositions. From Italy he sent back to the Paris Salon canvases that mixed cool sensual surfaces with strange anatomical distortions—the Grande Odalisque of 1814 with her impossibly long back, the Valpinçon Bather, the Oedipus and the Sphinx, the Vow of Louis XIII. Salon critics savaged him as a Gothic regressive and a clumsy draughtsman; he answered with more of the same.

The Apotheosis of Homer of 1827, exhibited at the Louvre, planted the flag of classicism just as Eugène Delacroix's Death of Sardanapalus declared the Romantic cause, and the rivalry between the two became the great public quarrel of French art. Ingres returned to Paris as a national figure, became director of the French Academy in Rome, and held in the second half of his life a near-monopoly on official portraiture, producing the Comtesse d'Haussonville, the Princesse de Broglie, Madame Moitessier and the great late odalisques. He died in Paris in 1867, after a final masterpiece, The Turkish Bath, painted in his eighties.

His cult of line shaped Edgar Degas, late Renoir, and even Picasso's neoclassical drawing of the 1920s. But beyond Raphael, Ingres's own classicism reached back to a still earlier French model who had stood for the same severity in the seventeenth century—Nicolas Poussin.
