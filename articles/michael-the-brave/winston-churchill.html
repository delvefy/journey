Winston Churchill

British Prime Minister and wartime leader, 1874–1965

Born at Blenheim Palace to an aristocratic father and an American mother, Churchill began as a cavalry officer and war correspondent, winning fame by escaping captivity in the Boer War. Entering Parliament in 1900, he held nearly every great office over the next forty years, including First Lord of the Admiralty—a post he left in disgrace after the Gallipoli catastrophe of 1915—and Chancellor of the Exchequer. Through the 1930s he was a lonely, often ridiculed voice warning that Nazi Germany must be confronted.

Vindication came in May 1940, when he became prime minister as France fell and Britain stood alone against Hitler. His defiant oratory—promising nothing but “blood, toil, tears and sweat,” vowing to fight on the beaches, hailing the airmen of Britain’s “finest hour”—stiffened a nation through the Battle of Britain and the Blitz. As one of the “Big Three” alongside Roosevelt and Stalin, he helped forge the grand alliance that destroyed the Third Reich.

Voters turned him out in 1945, even before the war with Japan ended, but he returned as prime minister from 1951 to 1955. He coined the phrase “Iron Curtain” to describe the new division of Europe, and won the Nobel Prize in Literature for his sweeping histories. A painter, bricklayer, and inexhaustible talker, he died in 1965 and was honoured with a state funeral, mourned as the lion who had roared for Britain in its darkest hour.
