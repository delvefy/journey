Henry Clay

American statesman, “the Great Compromiser,” 1777–1852

Virginia-born but made famous as “Harry of the West” in Kentucky, Clay dominated American politics for forty years without ever reaching the presidency he so desperately wanted. As a young and astonishingly powerful Speaker of the House he led the War Hawks who pushed the nation into the War of 1812, then helped negotiate the peace at Ghent. He devised the “American System” of protective tariffs, a national bank, and federal roads and canals to bind the country together.

Above all he was the great broker of the Union. Three times—in the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the resolution of the Nullification Crisis, and the Compromise of 1850—he cobbled together the bargains that postponed civil war over slavery for a generation. Founder and leader of the Whig Party, he made the famous boast that he would rather be right than president.

His alliance with John Quincy Adams in the disputed election of 1824 earned him the lasting enmity of Andrew Jackson, who never forgave the “corrupt bargain.” Clay ran for the White House three times and lost each, most painfully to Polk in 1844. Yet his eloquence and devotion to the Union made him the idol of a young Illinois lawyer named Abraham Lincoln. He died in 1852 with the Union still, for the moment, intact. The man with whom he forged his fateful partnership was John Quincy Adams.
