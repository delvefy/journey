Charles Lamb

1775–1834, English essayist and critic

Charles Lamb was born in the Temple, in London, where his father served as a clerk to one of the benchers. From seven he attended Christ's Hospital, the charity school whose blue gowns and yellow stockings he wore alongside Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and their friendship outlasted everything else in both their lives. His stammer kept him from the university; instead, at fourteen, he took a clerkship at the East India House and stayed there for thirty-three years.

The defining tragedy of his life came in September 1796, when his sister Mary, in a fit of mental illness, killed their mother with a kitchen knife. To spare her the asylum, Lamb pledged the rest of his life to her care. Brother and sister lived together thereafter, with periodic returns of her madness, and in lucid intervals collaborated on the children's classic Tales from Shakespeare (1807), retelling twenty plays in prose.

From 1801 the Lambs held open house in Mitre Court and later in Russell Street, where Wednesday evenings gathered the writers of the new Romantic generation. William Hazlitt, Thomas De Quincey, Leigh Hunt, William Godwin, the young John Keats, and at irregular intervals Lord Byron all sat there over Lamb's pipe and Mary's wafer biscuits. Lamb edited Specimens of English Dramatic Poets, Contemporary with Shakespeare (1808), which helped reopen the Jacobean playwrights to nineteenth-century readers.

From 1820 he wrote for the London Magazine under the pseudonym Elia, and the collected Essays of Elia (1823) and Last Essays of Elia (1833) gave the English personal essay a new domestic voice, full of antiquarian whimsy, urban affection, and quiet pathos. He retired from the India House in 1825 on a generous pension and spent his last years at Edmonton. He died in December 1834, six months after Coleridge, and Mary survived him by another thirteen years.
