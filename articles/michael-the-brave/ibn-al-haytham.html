Ibn al-Haytham

Arab scientist, c.965–1040

Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham, known to the Latin West as Alhazen, was born about 965 at Basra and became one of the greatest scientists of the Islamic golden age. His reputation for learning drew the attention of the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim, who summoned him to Egypt to tame the flooding of the Nile. When Ibn al-Haytham saw that the project — a great dam near Aswan — was beyond the means of the age, he feigned madness to escape the wrath of the volatile ruler, and lived under house arrest until the caliph’s death.

Those confined years were the making of him, for he poured his mind into his masterwork, the Book of Optics. In it he overturned the ancient teaching of Euclid and Ptolemy that the eye sees by sending out rays, demonstrating instead that vision arises from light entering the eye from objects outside. He studied reflection and refraction, lenses and mirrors, the rainbow and the camera obscura, and — crucially — he insisted that claims about nature be tested by controlled experiment, an emphasis that has earned him the title of a father of the scientific method. In his Doubts on Ptolemy he turned the same critical eye on astronomy.

Translated into Latin in the thirteenth century, his optics reshaped European science, guiding Roger Bacon and reaching, centuries on, the work of Johannes Kepler. Ibn al-Haytham died in Cairo about 1040, the founder of the science of light.
