Arthur Conan Doyle

Scottish writer, creator of Sherlock Holmes, 1859–1930

Trained as a physician in Edinburgh, Conan Doyle drew on the uncanny diagnostic powers of his teacher Joseph Bell to invent the most famous detective in literature. Building on the analytical method pioneered by Edgar Allan Poe, he created in Sherlock Holmes—the cold, brilliant reasoner of Baker Street—and his loyal chronicler Dr Watson a partnership that captivated the world. The stories, beginning with A Study in Scarlet and flourishing in the Strand Magazine, made him rich and internationally celebrated.

So weary did he grow of his creation that in 1893 he killed Holmes off at the Reichenbach Falls—only to be forced by public outcry to resurrect him years later. He always preferred his historical romances and considered them his serious work. He was a man of restless energy: a sportsman, a campaigner against injustice who helped clear wrongly convicted men, and a war correspondent and propagandist knighted for his defence of British conduct in the Boer War.

The losses of the First World War turned him to spiritualism, which consumed his final years and led to a famous, bruising friendship and falling-out with the magician Harry Houdini over the reality of mediums. He died in 1930, but his deerstalkered detective long outlived him to become one of the most recognised figures in all of fiction.
