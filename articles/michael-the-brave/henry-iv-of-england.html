Henry IV of England

King of England, first Lancastrian, 1367–1413

Henry Bolingbroke was born on 15 April 1367 at Bolingbroke Castle in Lincolnshire, the eldest surviving son of John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, and Blanche of Lancaster. He was Earl of Derby by 1377 and a leading figure among the Lords Appellant who in 1387–1388 forced reform on the young Richard II. Between Richard's revenge of 1397 and Bolingbroke's banishment, he travelled widely: he crusaded in Lithuania with the Teutonic Order in 1390 and again in 1392, and made a pilgrimage to Jerusalem. Banished for ten years in 1398 over a quarrel with Thomas Mowbray, Duke of Norfolk, he was disinherited the following year when Richard, on Gaunt's death, seized the Lancastrian patrimony.

Henry landed at Ravenspur on the Humber in July 1399 with a small force, swept up the country in Richard's absence in Ireland, and on 30 September accepted the throne in a parliament that deposed Richard and accepted the claim by descent and conquest. Richard was imprisoned at Pontefract and died there in February 1400, almost certainly by starvation. The first half of the new reign was a continuous fight to keep what he had taken. Owain Glyndŵr raised Wales in 1400; the Percies, who had given him the throne, revolted in 1403 — Henry killed Hotspur at Shrewsbury that July; Archbishop Scrope's Yorkshire rising of 1405 ended on the block.

His regime conceded ground to Parliament: extended commons control over taxation, the statute De heretico comburendo of 1401 which authorised the burning of Lollards and conciliated the church, and an audit of household expenditure in 1406. After 1406 Henry was disfigured by a chronic and probably leprous skin disease and increasingly debilitated. He died in the Jerusalem Chamber at Westminster on 20 March 1413, leaving an established but exhausted dynasty to his far more brilliant son.
