Carl Friedrich Gauss

German mathematician and astronomer, 1777–1855

Carl Friedrich Gauss was born in 1777 at Brunswick in the duchy of the same name, the only child of a labourer. His extraordinary precocity was reported almost from infancy—the schoolroom anecdote of his summing the integers from one to a hundred at the age of seven is genuine, if much retold. Duke Karl Wilhelm Ferdinand, hearing of him, paid for his schooling at the Collegium Carolinum and then at Göttingen.

At nineteen he proved that the regular 17-gon could be constructed with compass and straightedge, and from then on he kept a private notebook, the Tagebuch, in which his discoveries far outran what he published. His Disquisitiones Arithmeticae of 1801 founded modern number theory; in the same year, taking just a few of the observations of the lost asteroid Ceres, he computed the orbit by his new method of least squares and predicted accurately where to find it again. The feat made him an international celebrity at twenty-four.

For the rest of his life he held the chair of astronomy at Göttingen and the direction of its observatory. He produced fundamental work on the theory of surfaces ("Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas"), the geodetic survey of Hanover, the theory of electromagnetism with his colleague Wilhelm Weber, and quietly developed—without publishing—an entire non-Euclidean geometry, which Bolyai and Lobachevsky discovered independently. He coined the modern definition of statistical error, the Gaussian curve.

He was an austere, conservative man, deeply religious in a private way, suspicious of mathematical fame, and famously slow to print. He died at Göttingen in 1855. His Newtonian and Eulerian inheritance fed Dirichlet, Dedekind, and through Riemannian geometry the general relativity of Einstein. But the pupil who took up Gauss's most far-reaching idea, the inner geometry of curved surfaces, and turned it into an entire theory of manifolds was Bernhard Riemann.
