Eric Arthur Blair, English essayist and novelist, 1903–1950

Eric Arthur Blair was born on 25 June 1903 at Motihari, in Bengal, where his father served in the Opium Department of the Indian Civil Service. Sent back to England as a child, he won scholarships first to St Cyprian’s and then to Eton, where for a term he was taught French by Aldous Huxley. Rather than read for university, he took the unusual course of joining the Indian Imperial Police, serving in Burma from 1922. The experience left him so ashamed of empire that he resigned in 1927 and set out to make himself a writer.

The years that followed were lean. He tramped in London, washed dishes in Paris kitchens, and produced his first book, Down and Out in Paris and London (1933), under the pseudonym George Orwell. Novels and reportage followed — Burmese Days, A Clergyman’s Daughter, The Road to Wigan Pier — but the turning point was Spain. He arrived in Barcelona late in 1936, enlisted in the POUM militia, and was shot through the throat at Huesca. Homage to Catalonia (1938), his account of how Soviet agents broke the anti-fascist left from within, set the political compass for everything he afterwards wrote.

Excluded from active service by tuberculosis, he worked at the BBC’s wartime Indian Service, then quit to write Animal Farm. The little fable, briefly rejected by T. S. Eliot at Faber, appeared in 1945 and made him famous. From the remote island of Jura, in the Hebrides, dying by inches, he produced Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949). He married Sonia Brownell from a hospital bed three months before tuberculosis killed him, in London, on 21 January 1950. Two essays — “Politics and the English Language” and “Why I Write” — and two words from his last novel — Big Brother — entered the language of every literate country.

The road also leads back to Charles Dickens, Aldous Huxley, T. S. Eliot, Jonathan Swift.
