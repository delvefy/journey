Lorenzo de’ Medici

Florentine statesman, Il Magnifico, 1449–1492

Lorenzo was born at Florence on 1 January 1449, the eldest son of Piero il Gottoso and Lucrezia Tornabuoni. He grew up under the eye of his grandfather Cosimo and was tutored by the humanists who came and went through the Medici palace — Marsilio Ficino, Cristoforo Landino, the Greek John Argyropoulos. In 1466 he and his younger brother Giuliano led the Florentine embassy of congratulation to Pope Paul II; in 1469 he married Clarice Orsini, daughter of a Roman baronial house, in a political alliance that signalled the end of the old Florentine reserve about marrying outside the city. His father died at Christmas the same year and the leading citizens called the twenty-year-old Lorenzo to the head of the regime.

The Pazzi conspiracy of 26 April 1478 — Francesco de’ Pazzi and a clutch of priests and condottieri, with the connivance of Pope Sixtus IV and his nephew Girolamo Riario — caught the brothers at high Mass in the cathedral. Giuliano was knifed to death in the choir; Lorenzo, wounded in the neck, escaped into the sacristy. The two-year war that followed against Sixtus IV and Ferrante of Naples nearly destroyed the regime, until Lorenzo, in November 1479, sailed alone to Naples and, by three months of personal diplomacy in the court of his enemy, came home with a separate peace and the prestige of his maturity. He turned to consolidate: in 1480 a Council of Seventy replaced the broader Cento; the Florentine bank’s losses in the London and Bruges branches were absorbed by drawing on the treasury; the Italian states settled into the so-called “ago della bilancia” balance for which he is remembered.

The patronage was at the heart of his reputation. Botticelli’s Primavera and the Birth of Venus were painted for his cousin Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco; Verrocchio worked steadily on Medici commissions; the young Michelangelo lived at the Palazzo Medici from about 1490 and ate at Lorenzo’s table beside Ficino and Pico della Mirandola. Lorenzo’s own vernacular poetry — the Selve d’amore, the Carnival songs that turned the streets into a perpetual triumph, the Laudi for the Confraternity of the Magi — fills two thick volumes. He died at his villa of Careggi on 8 April 1492, forty-three years old, of gout that had moved to the inner organs; the friar Girolamo Savonarola, summoned to the bedside, blessed him and went out without granting absolution.
