Epicurus

Greek philosopher, founder of Epicureanism, 341–270 BC

Epicurus was born on the island of Samos to Athenian parents and came in time to Athens, where around 307 BC he founded the philosophical community known as the Garden. Unusual for its age, the school welcomed women and slaves as members, and it set great store by friendship and the quiet shared life. From there Epicurus taught a complete philosophy of nature and of how to live, set down in a flood of writings of which only a few letters and collections of maxims survive.

His physics took over and refined the atomism of Democritus: the universe, he held, is made of atoms moving in the void, and everything—including the soul—is material and perishable. From this he drew a liberating ethics. If the gods are remote and indifferent and the soul dissolves at death, then men need fear neither divine punishment nor the grave: "death is nothing to us." The aim of life is happiness, understood not as indulgence but as tranquillity of mind and freedom from pain.

Though his enemies caricatured "epicureanism" as mere pleasure-seeking, his actual counsel was for simple living, modest desires, and the calm that comes of understanding the world. His ideas spread widely through the ancient world and found their greatest voice in the Roman poet Lucretius. Reviled by Stoics and Christians alike yet never forgotten, Epicurus shaped the materialist and humanist currents of thought that would resurface again and again in later ages.
