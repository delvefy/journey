Clodius Albinus

Roman usurper, c.150–197

Decimus Clodius Albinus was born about 150 at Hadrumetum in Roman Africa — the same province that produced his great rival — and rose to command the legions of Britain. In the turmoil of 193 he was the third of the claimants thrown up by the murder of Pertinax and the auction of the empire to Didius Julianus. The shrewdest of the three, Septimius Severus, secured Albinus’s loyalty for a time by granting him the rank of Caesar, so freeing his own hands to march first on Rome and then east against Pescennius Niger.

It was a bargain neither man meant to keep. Once Niger was crushed, Severus set his own young son in Albinus’s place as heir, and Albinus, seeing himself discarded, had his troops proclaim him Augustus. He crossed from Britain into Gaul with a great army, and the two Africans met at last outside Lugdunum, the modern Lyon, in February 197.

The Battle of Lugdunum was among the largest and bloodiest ever fought between Roman armies. After a desperate struggle Severus prevailed; Albinus died, and his body was subjected to every indignity, his head sent to Rome. With his last rival gone, Severus stood as sole master of the empire and founder of the Severan dynasty, and he took a hard revenge on the senators who had favoured the dead pretender. The civil wars of 193 chronicled by Cassius Dio and others were over.
