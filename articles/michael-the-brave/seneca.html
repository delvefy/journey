Seneca

Roman Stoic philosopher and statesman, c.4 BC–AD 65

Lucius Annaeus Seneca was born around 4 BC at Corduba in Roman Spain, the son of a noted rhetorician, and was brought to Rome to be educated in oratory and philosophy. He embraced Stoicism early and rose as a senator and advocate, but his career was nearly cut short under the emperor Caligula and then broken when Claudius banished him to the island of Corsica. After eight years of exile he was recalled by the empress Agrippina to serve as tutor to her young son.

That pupil became the emperor Nero, and for the first years of the reign Seneca, with the praetorian prefect Burrus, effectively guided the government during a period remembered as comparatively just and well ordered. The philosopher grew enormously rich and powerful in the process, a contradiction his enemies were quick to mock in a man who preached the Stoic virtues of restraint and indifference to fortune.

Seneca was the foremost Latin Stoic, and his moral essays and the great sequence of Letters to Lucilius taught how to live well, govern the passions, and meet death without fear, while his blood-drenched tragedies would later shape European drama. As Nero turned to tyranny, Seneca withdrew from court; accused of complicity in a conspiracy against the emperor in AD 65, he was commanded to take his own life and did so with a calm modelled on the death of Socrates. His writings left a deep imprint on Christianity, on Montaigne, and on the moral thought of the West.
