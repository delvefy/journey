Cyrus the Great

Founder of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, r. c. 559–530 BCE

Cyrus II, called the Great, founded the Achaemenid Empire and made Persia the dominant power of the ancient Near East. Born around 600 BCE, he was the son of Cambyses I, a vassal king of Anshan in what is now southwestern Iran; by Herodotus's account his mother Mandane was a daughter of the Median king Astyages. Around 550 BCE he rose against his Median overlords and overthrew Astyages, uniting Medes and Persians under a single crown.

He then expanded with extraordinary speed. In the west he defeated the proverbially wealthy Croesus of Lydia and absorbed Asia Minor with its Greek coastal cities. In 539 BCE he took Babylon, the greatest city of the age, from Nabonidus—an event he commemorated on the Cyrus Cylinder, in which he presents himself as a restorer of order and a patron of the city's gods. The Hebrew scriptures remember him warmly: he permitted the exiled Jews to return from Babylon to Jerusalem and rebuild their Temple, and the Book of Isaiah calls him the Lord's anointed.

Cyrus governed an empire of many peoples with notable tolerance toward local religions and customs, styling himself King of Kings rather than imposing a single creed—an approach to imperial rule that his successors inherited. He died around 530 BCE on campaign in the east; Herodotus relates that he fell fighting the nomadic Massagetae and their queen Tomyris, though other traditions of his death differ. He was buried in a plain stone tomb at his capital, Pasargadae, which still stands and which Alexander the Great is said to have restored two centuries later. Xenophon's Cyropaedia idealized him as the model ruler, and his reputation as a just conqueror has endured for over two millennia.
