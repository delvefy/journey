Giovanni Boccaccio

1313–1375, Florentine writer and humanist

Giovanni Boccaccio was probably born at Certaldo in Tuscany, the illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant attached to the Bardi banking house. As a boy he was sent to Naples to learn his father's business, and instead frequented the cosmopolitan court of King Robert of Anjou, read law, and was taught astronomy by Andalò del Negro. In Naples he fell in love with the woman he addressed in his poetry as Fiammetta, and wrote a series of early works in Italian verse and prose: the Filostrato, the Filocolo, the Teseida.

Family reverses recalled him to Florence by 1340. When the bubonic plague struck the city in 1348 he lost his father and his stepmother. The catastrophe produced his masterpiece, the Decameron (1349–1353), in which ten young Florentines flee the plague to a country villa and tell each other a hundred tales of love, wit, deception and human appetite, from the bawdy to the tragic. Written in a buoyant Tuscan prose, it founded the European art of the short story and made Boccaccio's name from Lombardy to the Levant.

His decisive turn came in 1350 when he met Francesco Petrarch in Florence. Their friendship and correspondence governed the rest of his life, and under Petrarch's influence he turned from vernacular fiction to Latin scholarship and to the recovery of classical antiquity. He compiled De Mulieribus Claris, De Casibus Virorum Illustrium, and the encyclopaedic Genealogia Deorum Gentilium, on the ancient gods, which fed a long line of later poets including Geoffrey Chaucer and, through him, William Shakespeare.

He also became the first great public reader of Dante, lecturing on the Commedia in the church of Santo Stefano in Badia from 1373, writing a Life of Dante, and giving the poem its familiar epithet Divina. He retired to Certaldo in declining health, lived austerely, and died there in December 1375, surviving Petrarch by little more than a year.
