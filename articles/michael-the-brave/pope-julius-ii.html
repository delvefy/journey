Pope Julius II

Giuliano della Rovere, the Warrior Pope, 1443–1513

Giuliano della Rovere was born on 5 December 1443 at Albisola near Savona on the Ligurian coast, the son of a poor relation of the family from which his uncle, the Franciscan friar Francesco della Rovere, had been elected Pope Sixtus IV in 1471. Uncle made him cardinal-priest of San Pietro in Vincoli within months of the election, then bishop of Carpentras, archbishop of Avignon, and legate to France for the negotiation that drew Louis XI into the Italian system. Through the 1480s he was the most powerful man in the curia after the pope. Under the next pontificate, that of his enemy Alexander VI Borgia in 1492, he fled to France, urged Charles VIII into his Italian invasion of 1494, and survived a decade of exile in conspiratorial diplomacy.

Alexander’s death in 1503 and the failure of his brief successor Pius III opened the way. Della Rovere was elected on 1 November 1503 in a conclave that lasted, by report, four hours — the quickest in the history of the institution. He took the name Julius and turned at once to two ambitions: to recover and consolidate the Papal States from the wreckage Cesare Borgia had left across the Romagna, and to expel the foreigner from Italy. In 1506 he led an army in person — the pope in the field with his white beard and his white cloak — and recovered Perugia and Bologna in two months. The League of Cambrai of 1508 partitioned Venice; when Venice yielded, Julius turned the same coalition, redrafted as the Holy League, against Louis XII of France in 1511.

The wars cost France Milan and gave Italy the rallying-cry “Fuori i barbari” — out with the barbarians — which Julius is said to have coined. His Rome was the centre of the new style. Bramante laid the first stone of the new Saint Peter’s on 18 April 1506 to a Latin-cross-on-Greek-cross plan that Michelangelo was eventually to complete. Michelangelo himself was set, in 1508, to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel; Raphael was painting the Stanze of the Vatican apartments. Julius died at Rome on 21 February 1513, the new basilica four pillars high, the ceiling unveiled less than four months before, the empire and France beaten back across the Alps. He was buried in San Pietro in Vincoli; the Moses of his unfinished tomb by Michelangelo remains there.
