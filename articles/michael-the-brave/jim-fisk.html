Jim Fisk

1835–1872, American financier known as “Jubilee Jim”

James Fisk Jr. was born in Pownal, Vermont, the son of an itinerant peddler whose travels he soon joined. As a young man he ran his own peddler's wagon through New England, then prospered during the Civil War by buying southern cotton for northern textile mills and selling Confederate bonds to credulous British investors. The fortune he accumulated in those years carried him to Wall Street in the late 1860s.

There he attached himself to Daniel Drew and Jay Gould, and together the three of them seized control of the Erie Railway in 1868, locking out Cornelius Vanderbilt in the celebrated Erie War. The contest was fought by overprinting Erie stock, decamping to New Jersey with the company's books to escape New York jurisdiction, and buying friendly judges and friendlier Tammany legislators, prominent among them William M. Tweed.

In September 1869 Fisk and Gould tried to corner the gold market in New York, courting President Grant through his brother-in-law Abel Corbin in the hope that the Treasury would stay its hand. When Grant abruptly ordered four million dollars in gold sold on 24 September, prices collapsed in the panic remembered as Black Friday. Gould had quietly sold short; Fisk, who had been the public face of the corner, repudiated his contracts and let the courts protect him.

He spent his last years living conspicuously in Manhattan, financing the Grand Opera House, parading as colonel of the Ninth Regiment of the New York militia, and openly keeping the actress Josie Mansfield. In January 1872 her former lover Edward Stokes shot Fisk on the staircase of the Grand Central Hotel, and he died the next morning at thirty-six. Vast crowds attended his funeral, and ballad-makers turned him into a folk hero almost overnight.
