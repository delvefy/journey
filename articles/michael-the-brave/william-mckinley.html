William McKinley

1843–1901, twenty-fifth President of the United States

William McKinley was born in Niles, Ohio, the seventh of nine children of a small-time ironmaster. He enlisted as a private in the Twenty-third Ohio Infantry in 1861, served under Rutherford B. Hayes through the war, and was breveted major for his conduct at Antietam, where he carried hot rations to the front under fire. He read law in Poland, Ohio, after the war and settled into a quiet practice at Canton.

From 1877 he sat in the House of Representatives almost without interruption for fourteen years, becoming the foremost congressional advocate of protective tariffs. His McKinley Tariff of 1890 raised average rates to nearly fifty percent and cost him his seat. He served two terms as governor of Ohio, and in 1896, managed by the Cleveland industrialist Mark Hanna, secured the Republican presidential nomination on a sound-money, protectionist platform. From the front porch of his Canton home he received delegations while William Jennings Bryan toured the country; the result was a comfortable victory.

His presidency saw the Dingley Tariff of 1897, the annexation of Hawaii in 1898, the gold standard fixed by statute in 1900, and the Open Door notes drafted by his Secretary of State John Hay. After the Maine exploded in Havana harbour, with William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal pressing hourly for war, McKinley reluctantly asked Congress for force. The Spanish-American War of 1898 ended in four months with Cuban independence and American possession of Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines, and McKinley's reluctant decision to suppress the Filipino insurgency under Emilio Aguinaldo.

Re-elected in 1900 with Theodore Roosevelt as his running mate, he was shaking hands at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo on 6 September 1901 when the anarchist Leon Czolgosz shot him twice. He died eight days later. Roosevelt succeeded him, and a new age of progressive Republicanism began.
