Theocritus

Greek pastoral poet, c. 300–260 BCE

Theocritus was born around 300 BCE in Syracuse, the great Greek city of Sicily, and spent his life among the cultivated courts of the Hellenistic age—on the island of Cos, in Sicily, and above all at Alexandria, where he won the favour of King Ptolemy II Philadelphus and a place among the brilliant poets gathered there.

His lasting achievement was the invention of pastoral, or bucolic, poetry. In his Idylls he conjured a vivid, idealised countryside of herdsmen and goatherds piping and singing in friendly contest beneath the Sicilian sun, their loves and quarrels rendered in supple, music-rich verse. Against this rustic world he set the bustle of the city, as in his famous sketch of two Syracusan housewives jostling through the crowds of Alexandria at a festival of Adonis.

He belonged to the Alexandrian school, prizing polish, learning, and small perfect forms over grand epic. He composed encomia for his patrons, praising Ptolemy II and courting the favour of Hiero II of Syracuse, and he moved in the same literary circles as Callimachus and Apollonius of Rhodes.

Though only a modest body of his work survives, his influence was immense. The Roman poet Virgil took Theocritus's shepherds as the direct model for his Eclogues, and through Virgil the pastoral became one of the great recurring modes of Western literature, flowering again in the Renaissance and after. Theocritus stands as the fountainhead of a tradition that has never wholly dried up.
