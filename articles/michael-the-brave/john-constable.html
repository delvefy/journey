John Constable

English landscape painter, 1776–1837

John Constable was born in 1776 at East Bergholt on the Suffolk border, the son of a prosperous miller. He resisted his father's plan that he take over the family business and entered the Royal Academy schools in 1799 as a slow, unfashionable late starter. For the rest of his life he painted the small stretch of country in which he had grown up—the River Stour, the working watermills of Flatford and Dedham, the village church spires, the East Anglian skies.

Where his contemporary J.M.W. Turner reached for the sublime and the catastrophic, Constable wanted instead an exact, scientific record of one place under particular weather. He studied clouds against painted dates and barometer readings, and he carried a long series of full-scale oil sketches—the Six-Footers, including The Hay Wain, The Leaping Horse, and Salisbury Cathedral from the Meadows—through years of revision before exhibiting the finished canvases.

In England his work made slow progress: he was elected to the Royal Academy only in 1829, a year after his wife Maria's death had broken him. In Paris, however, The Hay Wain caused a sensation at the Salon of 1824. Eugène Delacroix was so struck by Constable's broken, flickering greens that he is said to have repainted parts of his Massacre at Chios in answer. From there Constable's example flowed into the next generation of French landscape, into the Barbizon painters and, indirectly, into Impressionism.

He died of heart failure in London in 1837, just past sixty, still feeling himself unrecognised at home. The native English landscape he had recorded was his own subject, but the foreign master on whose Dutch model he had drawn most heavily, and the founder of the realistic landscape of the seventeenth century, was Jacob van Ruisdael.
