Valerian

Roman emperor, r. 253–260

Publius Licinius Valerianus, born about 199, was a senator and general raised to the throne in 253, in the depths of the third-century crisis. Sharing the impossible burden of empire, he at once made his son Gallienus co-emperor, taking the eastern half himself while Gallienus held the West against the Germans. On every side the empire was beset — by invasions, by plague, and by the resurgent Sasanian Persia of Shapur I.

Valerian also turned on the Christians, issuing edicts in 257 and 258 that struck at their clergy and leaders; among those who died in this persecution were the bishop of Rome and Cyprian of Carthage. But it is his own fate that history remembers. In 260, campaigning against Shapur near Edessa, his army was broken and Valerian himself was taken prisoner — the only Roman emperor ever to fall captive to a foreign enemy.

He passed the rest of his life in Persian hands, and Roman writers told, with horror, how Shapur used the captured Augustus as a living footstool to mount his horse, and how after his death his stuffed skin was kept as a trophy — tales perhaps coloured by propaganda, but a measure of the disgrace felt at home. The catastrophe threw the East into chaos, leaving his son Gallienus to rule a fracturing empire alone.
