Henry VIII of England

King of England, 1491–1547

Henry was born at the palace of Placentia in Greenwich on 28 June 1491, the second son of Henry VII and Elizabeth of York. Trained for a clerical career while his elder brother Arthur was raised for the throne, he became heir apparent on Arthur’s death in 1502 and king at not quite eighteen in April 1509. Within weeks he married his brother’s widow, Catherine of Aragon, under a special papal dispensation. The early years of the reign were lavish, glamorous and warlike. Cardinal Thomas Wolsey ran the government; the king invaded France in 1513, watched his armies defeat the Scots at Flodden the same year, and in 1520 met Francis I in the celebrated diplomacy of the Field of the Cloth of Gold.

His response to Luther in the Assertio Septem Sacramentorum of 1521 earned him the papal title Defender of the Faith, which he never relinquished. But by the mid-1520s the failure of Catherine to bear a surviving son, and his passion for her maid-of-honour Anne Boleyn, had set him on the long course of the “king’s great matter.” When Pope Clement VII would not annul the marriage, Henry, advised by Thomas Cromwell and Thomas Cranmer, broke with Rome. The Act of Supremacy of 1534 made him head of the Church in England; Thomas More and John Fisher died for refusing to subscribe; the dissolution of the monasteries from 1536 to 1541 transferred a great part of the Church’s wealth to the crown and to a generation of new gentry.

His later years grew bloated, suspicious and dangerous. He married six times in all; Anne Boleyn and Catherine Howard he beheaded, Anne of Cleves he divorced, Jane Seymour and Catherine Parr survived him, the first by dying in childbirth, the second by outliving him. He executed Cromwell in 1540 when he tired of him. He fought further unprofitable wars with France and Scotland, debased the coinage, and broke his own health with food and tournament wounds. He died at Whitehall on 28 January 1547, leaving three legitimate children—Mary, Elizabeth and the young Edward VI—each of whom would in turn wear his crown.
