Seti I

Pharaoh of Egypt, r. c. 1290–1279 BC

Seti I was the second pharaoh of Egypt’s Nineteenth Dynasty, the son of Ramesses I and the father of Ramesses the Great. His family had risen from the military rather than the old royal blood, his grandfather a soldier and his father a vizier raised to the throne in the unsettled years after the Amarna age. Seti devoted his reign to restoring the strength and the ancient religion of Egypt, which the heretical revolution of earlier kings had thrown into disorder.

He campaigned vigorously in the Levant, recovering territory lost to the Hittites and the unrest of the previous century, and he raised monuments of extraordinary quality. His temple at Abydos, dedicated to Osiris, is among the finest in Egypt, and on its wall he had inscribed a list of the legitimate kings of the past — a list that pointedly passed over the Amarna pharaohs, Tutankhamun among them, as if to erase them from the official memory. He began the colossal Hypostyle Hall at Karnak that his son would complete.

His tomb in the Valley of the Kings, the longest and most beautifully decorated of them all, was discovered in 1817, and his mummy survives in remarkable preservation, the serene face of one of Egypt’s great kings. Seti reigned little more than a decade, but he left a realm restored to confidence and a son prepared to carry it to its zenith. He died about 1279 BC, and Ramesses II succeeded him.
