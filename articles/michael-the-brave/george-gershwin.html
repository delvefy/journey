George Gershwin

American composer and pianist, 1898–1937.

Born Jacob Gershwine in Brooklyn to Russian-Jewish immigrants, Gershwin discovered the piano around twelve when his parents bought one for his older brother Ira, who promptly surrendered it to him. He left school at fifteen to work as a Tin Pan Alley song plugger at the publisher Jerome H. Remick, demonstrating songs all day and absorbing the popular idiom. His first hit, "Swanee" (1919), was made enormous by Al Jolson; through the 1920s a steady stream of musical comedies followed, almost all of them written with Ira's lyrics — Lady, Be Good!, Oh, Kay!, Funny Face, Strike Up the Band, the political satire Of Thee I Sing, the first musical to win a Pulitzer Prize.

What set him apart was a parallel ambition to be taken seriously as a concert composer. In 1924 the bandleader Paul Whiteman commissioned a piece for a "Experiment in Modern Music" concert at Aeolian Hall, and Gershwin wrote, in a hurried few weeks, the Rhapsody in Blue, opening with a clarinet glissando that became one of the most identifiable sounds of the century. He followed it with the Piano Concerto in F, An American in Paris, the Second Rhapsody, and the Cuban Overture, working hard to learn the orchestration trade. He asked both Maurice Ravel and Igor Stravinsky for lessons; both, by the famous stories, declined on the grounds that he should not be made a second-rate Ravel when he was a first-rate Gershwin.

His monument is the 1935 folk opera Porgy and Bess, set in the Black Charleston of DuBose Heyward's novel and bringing into the operatic canon "Summertime," "It Ain't Necessarily So," and "Bess, You Is My Woman Now." He moved to Hollywood in 1936 to write film songs for Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers, and in July 1937, at thirty-eight, collapsed and died of a glioblastoma. The fugal completions, the symphonic ambitions, the operas he had begun planning all died with him.

The road continues to Louis Armstrong.
