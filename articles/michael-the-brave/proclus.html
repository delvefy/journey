Proclus

Neoplatonist philosopher, 412–485

Proclus, called the Successor, was born in 412 and studied in Alexandria before coming to Athens, where under Syrianus he was trained in the philosophy of Plato and the theurgic Neoplatonism of Iamblichus. He rose to head the Platonic Academy and led it for nearly half a century, becoming the last great systematic philosopher of the pagan world. His was a tireless, encyclopedic mind: he poured out commentaries on the dialogues of Plato, hymns to the gods, and the Elements of Theology, in which he set forth the whole Neoplatonic universe in a chain of propositions argued with the rigour of geometry.

That geometric cast was no accident, for Proclus also wrote a celebrated commentary on the first book of Euclid’s Elements, which remains one of our most valuable sources for the early history of Greek mathematics. In philosophy he refined the Neoplatonic hierarchy that descended from Plotinus into its most elaborate form.

His influence outran his pagan faith. His Elements of Theology, reworked into the Arabic and then Latin Book of Causes, was read and commented upon in the medieval schools by Thomas Aquinas; his metaphysics, taken over almost wholesale by the Christian writer who passed as Dionysius the Areopagite, flowed into the mysticism of the Church; and centuries later Hegel saluted him as a summit of ancient thought. Proclus died in Athens in 485, a few decades before the Academy itself was closed.
