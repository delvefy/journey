Michel de Montaigne

French essayist, 1533–1592

Montaigne was born in 1533 at his family’s château in the Périgord, and on his father’s instructions was raised to speak Latin as his mother tongue. Trained in the law, he served for years as a magistrate at Bordeaux, where he formed with Étienne de La Boétie a friendship so perfect that his friend’s early death left a wound he mourned for the rest of his life.

In 1571, weary of public affairs, he retired at thirty-eight to the tower library of his estate to read and reflect, and there he created an entirely new kind of writing. He called his pieces "essays," meaning trials or attempts, and in them he turned his searching gaze upon himself and upon every human subject, death, friendship, education, custom, cruelty, and the newly discovered peoples of America. Saturated in Plutarch, Seneca, and the ancients, his book circled always back to the sceptic’s question, "What do I know?"

Though he prized his solitude, Montaigne could not escape the wars of religion tearing France apart; he served as mayor of Bordeaux and was trusted as a moderate by both the king and Henry of Navarre, whom he helped reconcile. His candid self-portrait, declaring "I am myself the matter of my book," founded the essay as a literary form and left its mark on Shakespeare, Pascal, and the whole tradition of introspective modern thought. He died in 1592.
