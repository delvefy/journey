Napoleon Bonaparte

Emperor of the French, 1769–1821

Born on Corsica the year after France acquired the island, Napoleon trained as an artillery officer and rose with astonishing speed amid the chaos of the French Revolution. A dazzling campaign in Italy made his name, and after an expedition to Egypt he seized power in the coup of 1799, becoming First Consul and then, in 1804, crowning himself Emperor of the French in the presence of the Pope.

He was among the greatest commanders in history, winning crushing victories such as Austerlitz and bending most of the continent to his will through conquest, alliance, and the placing of relatives on European thrones. Yet he was also a formidable ruler, and his enduring monument is the Napoleonic Code, a clear and rational body of law that reshaped legal systems far beyond France. His attempt to strangle British trade through the Continental System, however, drew him into ruinous commitments.

His overreach proved fatal. A long, draining war in Spain bled his armies, and the catastrophic invasion of Russia in 1812 destroyed the Grande Armée in the snows of the retreat from Moscow. A coalition of his enemies drove him to abdicate in 1814 and exiled him to Elba. He escaped for a last gamble, the Hundred Days, which ended in final defeat at Waterloo by the armies of Wellington and Blücher. Banished to the remote island of St Helena, he died there in 1821, leaving a legend and a transformed Europe behind him.
