James Monroe

Fifth President of the United States, 1758–1831

A Virginian like the presidents before him, Monroe left college to fight in the Revolution and was badly wounded leading troops at Trenton. He studied law under Thomas Jefferson, who became his lifelong mentor, and entered politics with the states’-rights convictions of the early republic, serving in the Senate before turning to diplomacy.

His diplomatic career reached its height in 1803, when Jefferson sent him to Paris to assist Robert Livingston. There the two men seized an unexpected offer from Napoleon’s government and concluded the Louisiana Purchase, buying a vast territory that doubled the size of the United States at a stroke. Monroe went on to serve as minister to Britain and as Secretary of State—and briefly of War—under James Madison during the War of 1812.

Elected president in 1816, Monroe presided over the “Era of Good Feelings,” a period of one-party calm in which Florida was acquired from Spain and the Missouri Compromise patched over the growing quarrel about slavery. His most enduring legacy was the doctrine that bears his name, proclaimed in 1823 and shaped with his Secretary of State John Quincy Adams: a warning that the Americas were closed to further European colonisation. In 1824 he welcomed the aged Marquis de Lafayette on a triumphal tour of the nation he had helped to free.
