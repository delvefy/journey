Nicholas of Cusa

German cardinal and philosopher, 1401–1464

Nicholas of Cusa was born in 1401 at Kues on the Moselle, the son of a prosperous boatman, and studied law and the liberal arts at Heidelberg, Padua and Cologne. He first made his mark at the Council of Basel, where he argued in De concordantia catholica for a reformed and unified Church, before shifting his allegiance to the papacy. In 1437 he was sent to Constantinople and helped escort the Byzantine emperor John VIII Palaiologos and the Greek delegation to the Council of Florence, in the great if short-lived attempt to reunite the Eastern and Western Churches.

Rewarded with a cardinal’s hat and the bishopric of Brixen, he served as papal legate across Germany, preaching reform, and remained throughout the close friend and adviser of Enea Silvio Piccolomini, who as Pope Pius II leaned on his learning. But it is as a thinker that Cusa endures. In On Learned Ignorance he argued that the infinite God is the coincidence of all opposites, beyond the reach of reason, which can only know that it does not know; and he speculated boldly that the universe has no fixed centre and no circumference, and that the Earth itself is in motion.

These daring conjectures, advanced a century before the new astronomy, made him a forerunner of modern cosmology: Nicolaus Copernicus would displace the Earth from the centre, and bolder spirits still would imagine an infinite cosmos. His metaphysics of the infinite later drew the admiration of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. A mathematician who attempted to square the circle and a churchman who dreamed of concord, Cusa died at Todi in August 1464, only days before the pope he had served.
