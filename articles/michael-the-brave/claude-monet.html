Claude Monet

French Impressionist painter, 1840–1926

Monet was the heart and soul of Impressionism, the most faithful to its founding aim of capturing the fleeting play of light and atmosphere on the eye. Encouraged as a youth by the seascape painter Eugène Boudin to paint outdoors, he carried the practice to revolutionary lengths. It was his hazy harbour view Impression, Sunrise that, mocked by a critic in 1874, gave the whole movement its name.

With his friends Renoir and the other independents he abandoned the brown studio tradition for broken touches of pure colour laid down rapidly before the motif. Increasingly he painted the same subject again and again under changing conditions—haystacks, poplars, the façade of Rouen Cathedral—to record the very passage of light and time across a surface. The series became his signature method.

In his last decades he created a watery paradise of his own at Giverny, diverting a stream to make the famous lily pond and Japanese bridge. There, his eyesight clouding with cataracts, he painted the vast, dissolving canvases of Water Lilies that hover at the edge of abstraction. Urged on by his friend the statesman Georges Clemenceau, he gave the great mural cycle to France, installed in Paris after his death in 1926. He had outlived nearly all his fellow Impressionists, the movement’s last and greatest survivor.
