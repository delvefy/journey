Franz Liszt

Hungarian composer and piano virtuoso, 1811–1886

A child prodigy from the Hungarian borderlands, Liszt studied in Vienna with Czerny, himself a pupil of Beethoven, and as a boy was said to have been kissed on the brow by the great master. Settling in Paris amid the Romantic ferment, he was electrified by hearing the demonic violinist Paganini and resolved to become the Paganini of the piano. He succeeded beyond all rivalry: his tours provoked a frenzy contemporaries dubbed “Lisztomania,” with women fighting over his gloves and handkerchiefs.

Behind the showman stood a genuine innovator. Liszt invented the single-movement symphonic poem, pioneered the “transformation of themes,” and pushed piano technique and harmony toward the music of the future. His Hungarian Rhapsodies, his B minor Sonata, and his transcendental studies remain pillars of the repertoire, and his transcriptions carried Beethoven’s symphonies and Schubert’s songs to audiences across Europe.

As music director at Weimar he championed the daring works of others, above all those of Wagner, who would marry his daughter Cosima. Generous to a fault, he taught a whole generation of pianists without fee. In later life he took minor holy orders and was known as the Abbé Liszt, dividing his time between Rome, Weimar, and Budapest. He died at Bayreuth in 1886. Among the dearest friends of his Paris years was the Polish composer Frédéric Chopin.
