Epaminondas

Theban general and statesman, c. 418–362 BCE

Epaminondas was the Theban general and statesman who broke the long military supremacy of Sparta and made Thebes, for a brief decade, the leading power in Greece. Schooled in a Pythagorean tradition that prized discipline and integrity, he was admired even by his enemies for his poverty, his learning, and his incorruptibility.

His moment came at the battle of Leuctra in 371 BCE. Facing a larger Spartan-led army, he abandoned the conventional even line of battle and massed his best troops many ranks deep on his left wing, holding back the rest—an innovation later known as the oblique order. The reinforced wing crushed the Spartan right, where the king Cleombrotus fell, and the myth of Spartan invincibility died with him.

Epaminondas pressed his advantage with strategic boldness. He led Theban armies deep into the Peloponnese, liberated the long-subjected region of Messenia from Spartan rule and freed its helots, and fostered new cities such as Megalopolis to hem Sparta in. At a stroke he reduced Sparta from the arbiter of Greece to a second-rate power confined to its own valley.

In 362 BCE he sought a decisive victory at the battle of Mantinea. His army was winning when he was mortally wounded, and with his death Theban hegemony collapsed as swiftly as it had risen, for no one of his stature remained. Ancient writers ranked him among the greatest generals and finest men that Greece produced.
