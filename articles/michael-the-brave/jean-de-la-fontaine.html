Jean de La Fontaine

French fabulist and poet, 1621–1695

Born at Château-Thierry and trained without enthusiasm in the law, La Fontaine drifted into minor office and the life of a poet under the protection of Nicolas Fouquet, Louis XIV’s wealthy superintendent of finances. When the king disgraced and imprisoned Fouquet, La Fontaine loyally pleaded for his fallen patron in verse, an act of fidelity that earned him the lasting coolness of the crown.

His fame rests on the Fables, published from 1668 onward. Taking the old stories of Aesop and other ancient and Eastern sources, he retold them in supple, witty French verse, filling a whole society of talking animals—the flattering fox, the improvident grasshopper, the wolf who needs no good reason to devour the lamb—with all the folly, cruelty, and shrewdness of humankind. Read by children and savoured by adults, they became one of the treasures of the French language; he also wrote the more mischievous verse Contes.

La Fontaine belonged to a celebrated circle of “four friends” with Molière, Racine, and the critic Boileau. Elected to the Académie française only after the king let his candidacy wait, he took the side of the Ancients in the great literary Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns against Charles Perrault. He died in 1695, his fables long since woven into the imagination of his nation and the wider world.
