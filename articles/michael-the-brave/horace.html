Horace

Roman poet, 65–8 BCE

Horace was, with his friend Virgil, the greatest poet of the age of Augustus, and perhaps the most quietly influential lyric voice in all of Latin literature. Born in 65 BCE in southern Italy, the son of a freed slave who spent everything to give him the finest education, he studied in Rome and Athens and seemed destined for no settled career.

The civil wars swept him up: he fought at Philippi in 42 BCE as an officer in the republican army of Brutus, on the losing side, and returned to Italy pardoned but impoverished. Poetry was his salvation. His talent won him the friendship of Virgil, who introduced him to the great patron Maecenas; Maecenas gave him a farm in the Sabine hills and the leisure to write, and drew him into the circle of the emperor Augustus himself.

From this security came his enduring work: the conversational Satires and Epistles, the biting Epodes, and above all the Odes, in which he naturalized the meters of the Greek lyric poets into Latin with unmatched polish. His verse coined phrases that still circulate—carpe diem, "seize the day," and dulce et decorum est pro patria mori—and his Ars Poetica served as a handbook of literary taste for centuries.

He died in 8 BCE, within weeks of his patron Maecenas, and was buried near him. Calm, urbane, and self-aware, Horace bequeathed to European poetry an ideal of measured craftsmanship that writers from the Renaissance onward strove to match.

The road continues to Maecenas.
