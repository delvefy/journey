Ptolemy I Soter

Founder of Ptolemaic Egypt, c. 367–282 BCE

Ptolemy was a Macedonian nobleman, a boyhood companion and one of the most trusted generals and bodyguards of Alexander the Great, whom he followed throughout the conquest of the Persian Empire. When Alexander died in 323 BCE, Ptolemy secured for himself the richest prize of the dismembered empire: the satrapy of Egypt.

He ruled Egypt first as governor and, from 305 BCE, as king, founding a dynasty that would endure nearly three centuries, until the death of his descendant Cleopatra. To legitimise his rule he seized Alexander's body as it was being carried for burial and enshrined it in Egypt, eventually in a great tomb in his new capital, Alexandria. He beat off the attempts of rival successors—Perdiccas and Antigonus—to wrest Egypt from him.

Shrewd and moderate, Ptolemy earned the surname Soter, “Saviour.” He made Alexandria the cultural capital of the Greek world, founding the famous Museum and Library that drew scholars from across the Mediterranean; the mathematician Euclid taught there in his reign. He promoted the cult of Serapis to bind together his Greek and Egyptian subjects, and he himself wrote a sober and valuable history of Alexander's campaigns, now lost but used by later writers.

Late in life he passed over his eldest son and named a younger son, Ptolemy II Philadelphus, as his successor, securing a peaceful transfer of power. He died around 282 BCE, having proved the ablest and most enduring of all Alexander's heirs, and the founder of the wealthiest of the Hellenistic kingdoms.
