Pierre Gassendi

French philosopher and astronomer, 1592–1655

Pierre Gassendi was a French priest, astronomer, and philosopher who revived the atomism of antiquity for the age of the scientific revolution. Born in Provence in 1592, he became provost of the cathedral of Digne and later professor of mathematics at the Collège Royal in Paris, combining a churchman's life with tireless inquiry into nature.

As an observer he won lasting fame in 1631, when he made the first recorded sighting of a transit of Mercury across the face of the Sun, confirming a prediction of Kepler and demonstrating the power of the new astronomy he shared with Galileo, whom he admired and defended. He also performed careful experiments in mechanics to vindicate the idea of inertia.

His central ambition, however, was philosophical: to rescue the system of Epicurus, known above all through the poem of Lucretius, and to reconcile its atoms-and-void physics with Christian belief. Against the entrenched followers of Aristotle he had launched his first book, and he became the most searching critic of Descartes, composing the Fifth Objections to the Meditations and insisting, against the doctrine of innate ideas, that all knowledge springs from the senses.

A central figure in the Paris circle of Marin Mersenne, friend of Thomas Hobbes, and teacher of the young Molière and Cyrano de Bergerac, Gassendi spread a sober empiricism whose influence ran on through Locke and the corpuscular science of Newton.
