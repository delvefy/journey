Porphyry

Neoplatonist philosopher, c.234–305

Porphyry was born about 234 at Tyre in Phoenicia, bearing originally the Semitic name Malchus, “king,” which he turned into the Greek Porphyrios. After studying at Athens under the critic Longinus, he came to Rome and became the closest disciple of Plotinus. It was Porphyry who, after his master’s death, gathered the scattered treatises and arranged them into the six sets of nine that we know as the Enneads, and who wrote the Life of Plotinus that preserves nearly all we know of him.

A scholar of vast range, he wrote on logic, religion, vegetarianism and Homer, and composed a life of Pythagoras. His most enduring work was the Isagoge, a short introduction to the Categories of Aristotle; translated into Latin by Boethius, it became the first book of logic studied in the medieval schools and set the terms of the long debate over universals.

But Porphyry was also the most formidable pagan critic of the new religion. His fifteen books Against the Christians assailed the scriptures and the faith with such learning that the Church answered him for generations — Eusebius and Augustine among his opponents — and at last had his work condemned to the flames. He guided Neoplatonism after Plotinus, though his own pupil Iamblichus would turn it toward ritual and the gods. Porphyry died around 305, the great systematizer and defender of the last pagan philosophy.
