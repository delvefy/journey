Pliny the Elder

Roman naturalist and author of the Natural History, 23–79 AD

Gaius Plinius Secundus was born to an equestrian family at Como in northern Italy and served as a cavalry officer in Germany before turning to law and letters. Under the suspicious eye of Nero he kept to safe scholarly subjects, but the rise of his friend Vespasian opened a career of trust: he held a series of imperial procuratorships and was given command of the Roman fleet at Misenum. A man of legendary industry, he begrudged every hour not spent reading or being read to, and filled his nights and journeys with study.

The fruit of this labour was the Natural History, a vast encyclopaedia in thirty-seven books that he dedicated to the future emperor Titus. Ranging over astronomy, geography, the animal and plant kingdoms, medicine, minerals, and the arts, and drawing on the work of Aristotle and hundreds of other authors, it was the largest single work to survive from the ancient world and remained the West's chief storehouse of natural knowledge for fifteen centuries.

His curiosity cost him his life. When Vesuvius erupted in 79, Pliny put to sea, partly to observe the great cloud and partly to rescue those trapped along the shore. Landing near Stabiae, he was overcome by the fumes and ash and died upon the beach. The scene was preserved in vivid detail by his nephew and adopted son, who had stayed behind and who would carry on both his name and his devotion to letters.
