Marsilio Ficino

Florentine philosopher, translator of Plato, 1433–1499

Marsilio was born at Figline Valdarno on 19 October 1433, the son of a physician in the service of Cosimo de’ Medici. Cosimo, who had come back from the Council of Florence inflamed by the Plato of Gemistos Plethon and frustrated by the disappearance of every Greek text more demanding than a school grammar, marked the studious boy out for the task. He was educated at Cosimo’s expense in Latin, philosophy, and Greek; in 1462 Cosimo settled on him the small villa of Careggi above Florence, gave him a salary, and put into his hands the Greek manuscripts of Plato that had been collected through the agency of Niccolò Niccoli and the Greek refugees. Ficino was twenty-nine.

He worked on Plato for fifteen years. The first complete Latin translation of the Platonic dialogues was finished in 1469 and printed at Florence in 1484–85 — the year Pico della Mirandola turned twenty-one — and the rest of his life’s scholarly work followed. He translated Plotinus complete (1492), the Corpus Hermeticum (1471), the works of Iamblichus, Porphyry, Proclus, and the pseudo-Dionysius. His own Theologia Platonica de immortalitate animarum (1474), in eighteen books, attempted to reconcile Plato with Christian doctrine on the immortality of the soul; his De vita libri tres (1489), with its careful instructions on the medical, dietary, and astrological care of the scholar, became a much-reprinted handbook of Renaissance learning.

The villa at Careggi was the seat of a working academy as much as the symbolic centre of Florentine Platonism. He took priest’s orders in 1473, was given a canonry of the cathedral, and held weekly meetings of his friends — Cristoforo Landino, the young Pico, Lorenzo and Giuliano de’ Medici, Angelo Poliziano, Giovanni Cavalcanti, with the painter Botticelli in occasional attendance — at which the dialogues of Plato were read and translated as exercises in living philosophy. The Renaissance idea of platonic love is essentially his commentary on the Symposium. He died at his Florentine house on 1 October 1499, a year after Savonarola, of whose movement he had been at first a sympathetic observer and then a denouncer. Ficino was buried in the cathedral, the bust by Andrea Ferrucci above his tomb.
