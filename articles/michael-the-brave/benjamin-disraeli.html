Benjamin Disraeli

1804–1881, twice Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, 1st Earl of Beaconsfield

Benjamin Disraeli was born in London, the eldest son of the Sephardic-Jewish literary historian Isaac D'Israeli. A quarrel with his synagogue led his father to baptize the children as Anglicans in 1817, opening the parliamentary career otherwise barred to professing Jews. Disraeli was apprenticed to a solicitor, lost a small fortune speculating in South American mining stocks, and at twenty-one published the novel Vivian Grey to repay his debts. Through the 1830s he produced further novels — The Young Duke, Henrietta Temple, Venetia — while four times standing unsuccessfully for Parliament.

He entered the Commons in 1837 as a Tory and made his reputation by ferocious attacks on Sir Robert Peel during the Corn Law crisis of 1846. Out of the Tory wreckage that followed he led the protectionist remnant for twenty years alongside Lord Derby. As Chancellor of the Exchequer in three short Derby ministries he was the party's chief debater; in 1867 he carried Derby's Second Reform Bill, almost doubling the borough electorate, and in February 1868 succeeded his chief as Prime Minister.

The general election later that year handed power to William Gladstone, and the two contended through the next thirteen years for the soul of British politics. Disraeli returned in 1874 with a working majority and produced the great imperial and social ministry: the Public Health Act and Artisans' Dwellings Act at home, the purchase in 1875 of the Suez Canal shares from the Khedive of Egypt, and the proclamation of Queen Victoria as Empress of India in 1876. At the Congress of Berlin in 1878 he and Otto von Bismarck rolled back the Russian settlement imposed on the Ottomans at San Stefano, and Disraeli returned, in his own phrase, with “peace with honour.”

The novels Coningsby (1844), Sybil, or The Two Nations (1845) and Tancred (1847) had defined his “Young England” conservatism; his very late Lothair and Endymion enjoyed great commercial success. He was the queen's favourite minister, defeated by Gladstone in 1880, and died of bronchitis the following April. Victoria had primroses sent to his grave at Hughenden.
