Darius the Great

Achaemenid King of Kings, 550–486 BCE

Darius I, called the Great, seized the throne of the Persian Empire in 522 BCE amid the chaos following the death of Cambyses II, putting down a usurper and a wave of rebellions across the realm. He recorded his victory and his right to rule in the great trilingual inscription carved high on the cliff at Behistun, a monument that would later furnish the key to deciphering cuneiform.

A brilliant organiser, Darius gave the sprawling empire its lasting shape. He divided it into provinces called satrapies, each under a governor watched by royal inspectors—the “king's eyes and ears”; he standardised weights, measures, and a gold coinage, the daric; and he built the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, along which couriers sped royal orders across vast distances. He raised the magnificent palace-city of Persepolis and adorned Susa.

Under him the empire reached its greatest extent, stretching from the Indus valley in the east to Thrace and the edge of Europe in the west, and embracing Egypt and Babylon. He cut a canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea and sent expeditions to probe his frontiers.

His ambitions in the west brought Persia into collision with the Greeks. When Athens aided a revolt of the Greek cities of Ionia, Darius resolved to punish her, and in 490 BCE he launched the first Persian invasion of Greece—only to see his army beaten on the plain of Marathon. He was preparing a greater expedition when he died in 486, leaving the unfinished war, and his vast empire, to his son Xerxes. Heir to the realm founded by Cyrus the Great, Darius had made it the mightiest power the world had yet seen.
