Ovid

Roman poet, 43 BCE–c. 17 CE

Ovid was one of the greatest poets of Augustan Rome, a master of elegant, witty, and inexhaustibly inventive verse on the themes of love and myth. Born in 43 BCE, the year after Julius Caesar's murder, to a well-off family of Sulmo, he abandoned a public career for poetry and quickly became the most fashionable writer in Rome.

His early work was playful and erotic—the Amores, the Heroides (letters in the voices of mythical heroines), and above all the Ars Amatoria, a mock-didactic "art of love" whose cheerful immorality would later cost him dearly. His masterpiece, the Metamorphoses, is a vast hexameter poem gathering the myths of gods and mortals into a single flowing narrative of transformation, from the creation of the world to the deification of Caesar; it became the great storehouse of classical mythology for all later European art and letters.

At the height of his fame, in 8 CE, the emperor Augustus abruptly banished him to Tomis, a bleak town on the Black Sea, for what Ovid called "a poem and a mistake"—the poem his scandalous Ars, the mistake an offense he never names. From exile he poured out the mournful Tristia and Letters from Pontus, pleading in vain to be recalled.

He died in exile around 17 CE, never seeing Rome again. Yet his influence proved immortal: Dante, Chaucer, and above all Shakespeare drew on him endlessly, and the Metamorphoses remains one of the most beloved and widely imitated poems ever written.
