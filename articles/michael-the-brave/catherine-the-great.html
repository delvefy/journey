Catherine the Great

Empress of Russia, 1729–1796

Catherine II, called the Great, was the German-born princess who became Russia's longest-reigning empress and one of the most formidable rulers of the eighteenth century. Born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst in 1729 to minor nobility, she was brought to Russia as a girl to marry the heir to the throne, the future Peter III. She embraced her new country wholeheartedly, converting to Orthodoxy, mastering Russian, and biding her time in an unhappy marriage.

In 1762, months after her erratic husband became tsar, she seized power in a palace coup backed by the imperial guard; Peter was deposed and soon killed. For the next thirty-four years she ruled as autocrat. A self-styled enlightened sovereign, she corresponded with Voltaire, took Diderot under her patronage and bought his library, and composed her Nakaz, a reforming instruction to a great legislative commission, drawn largely from Montesquieu—though in practice she changed little, and after Pugachev's vast peasant rebellion she bound the serfs more tightly than ever.

Abroad she was relentlessly successful. Two wars against the Ottoman Empire won Russia the northern shores of the Black Sea and the annexation of the Crimea, work in which her favourite Grigory Potemkin played a leading part, while three partitions of Poland, shared with Prussia and Austria, wiped that kingdom from the map and pushed Russia deep into Europe.

A great collector who founded the Hermitage and corresponded with the leading minds of the age, Catherine turned conservative in horror at the French Revolution near the end of her life. She saw herself always as the heir of the tsar who had dragged Russia toward Europe, and dedicated his famous equestrian statue, the Bronze Horseman, "to Peter the First from Catherine the Second."
