Cornelius Nepos

Roman biographer, c. 110–24 BCE

Cornelius Nepos was a Roman writer of the first century BCE, remembered as the earliest Latin biographer whose work survives. Born around 110 BCE in Cisalpine Gaul, he settled in Rome and moved in the brilliant literary circle of the late Republic, counting among his friends the orator Cicero, the cultured millionaire Atticus, and the poet Catullus, who dedicated his book of verses to him with the famous question of to whom he should give his charming new little book.

His major work was a vast collection of biographies, On Famous Men, arranged in parallel books that set Romans and foreigners side by side—generals, historians, kings, and poets. Most of it is lost, but the surviving book On Eminent Foreign Commanders preserves short lives of figures such as Miltiades, Themistocles, Alcibiades, Lysander, Epaminondas, Pelopidas, and Hannibal, while two further lives, of Cato the Elder and of his friend Atticus, survive from the Roman series.

Nepos wrote also a universal chronicle, the Chronica, and collections of moral anecdotes, all now lost. His surviving Lives are brief, fond of moral example, and not always critical in their handling of sources, yet they are precious: they are the oldest Latin biographies we possess and a direct ancestor of the later, greater work of Plutarch and Suetonius.

He died around 24 BCE, having helped to invent a literary form that would shape how the West remembers its great men ever after.
