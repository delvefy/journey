Catullus

Roman lyric poet, c. 84–54 BCE

Catullus was one of the greatest lyric poets of ancient Rome, a young writer of the late Republic whose intense, personal verse broke sharply with the grand public poetry that came before. Born around 84 BCE to a prominent family of Verona in Cisalpine Gaul, he came to Rome and joined a set of brilliant young "new poets" who took as their models the polished, learned art of the Greeks rather than the old Roman epic.

His surviving book of poems ranges from the tender to the obscene, from the grief-stricken to the savage. At its heart is his tormented love affair with a woman he calls Lesbia—a name chosen in homage to the poet Sappho of Lesbos, and now generally identified with the aristocratic Clodia. Across a sequence of poems he charts the whole arc of passion, jealousy, and bitter disillusion, including the famous two-word cry odi et amo, "I hate and I love."

He could also be ferociously political, aiming obscene squibs at Julius Caesar and his favorite Mamurra; Caesar is said to have been stung but, after an apology, to have dined with the poet's father as if nothing had happened. Catullus dedicated his collection to the biographer Cornelius Nepos.

He died young, probably around 54 BCE, barely thirty years old. Yet his slender book, rediscovered in the Middle Ages, made him a lasting model of the personal lyric, admired and imitated from the Augustan poets to the present day.
