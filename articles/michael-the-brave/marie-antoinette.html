Marie Antoinette

Queen of France, 1755–1793

Born an archduchess of Austria, the youngest daughter of the Empress Maria Theresa, Marie Antoinette was married at fourteen to the future Louis XVI of France to seal the alliance between the Habsburg and Bourbon dynasties. A foreigner at the formal and intrigue-ridden court of Versailles, she became queen in 1774 and soon attracted criticism for her pursuit of pleasure, fashion, and gambling, and for her retreat into a private world at the Petit Trianon.

As royal finances deteriorated, she was blamed for the crown’s extravagance and nicknamed Madame Déficit, though the famous remark about eating cake was almost certainly never hers. The Affair of the Diamond Necklace, in which her name was used by swindlers without her knowledge, further blackened her reputation despite her innocence. To a public turning against the monarchy she came to symbolise its excess, and her Austrian birth bred suspicion that she worked against France.

When revolution came she counselled resistance and intrigued for foreign help. The royal family’s attempted flight in 1791 ended in humiliating capture at Varennes and destroyed what trust remained. After the monarchy fell, her husband was tried and guillotined early in 1793; months later she too was brought before a revolutionary tribunal, condemned on charges both political and slanderous, and executed. She went to the scaffold with a composure that won a measure of sympathy even from her enemies, and her tragic end made her one of the most famous queens in history.
