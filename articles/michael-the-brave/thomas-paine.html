Thomas Paine

Anglo-American revolutionary writer, 1737–1809

The son of an English corset-maker, Paine failed at trade after trade until a chance meeting with Benjamin Franklin in London changed his life. Armed with Franklin’s letter of introduction, he sailed for Philadelphia in 1774 and within two years had become the most influential pamphleteer of the age.

His pamphlet Common Sense, published anonymously in 1776, tore through the colonies, arguing in plain and fiery prose that independence from Britain was not only justified but inevitable. It sold in the hundreds of thousands and helped turn colonial grievance into outright revolution. When the war went badly, his series The American Crisis—opening “These are the times that try men’s souls”—was read aloud to George Washington’s shivering troops before they crossed the Delaware to victory at Trenton.

Paine carried his convictions back to Europe. When Edmund Burke attacked the French Revolution, Paine answered with Rights of Man (1791), a defence of popular sovereignty that became a bestseller and saw him outlawed in Britain. Fleeing to France, he was elected to the National Convention though he spoke little French; he opposed the execution of Louis XVI and was himself imprisoned during Robespierre’s Terror, narrowly escaping the guillotine. His later assault on organised religion, The Age of Reason, made him many enemies, and he died poor and largely shunned in New York, his vast influence unhonoured in his own time.
