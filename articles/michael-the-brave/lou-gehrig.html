Lou Gehrig

American baseball player, 1903–1941.

Heinrich Ludwig Gehrig was born in Yorkville, Manhattan, to German immigrants from the Palatinate — his father a sheet-metal worker, his mother a housekeeper who cleaned for a Columbia University fraternity. He starred as a football lineman and baseball player at the High School of Commerce, and on a Columbia scholarship he pitched and batted with such power that the New York Yankees signed him in 1923. After a season and a half at Hartford in the Eastern League he was called up for good in June 1925, replacing the regular first baseman Wally Pipp, who had a headache, and from that day did not miss a game for fourteen years.

Batting behind Babe Ruth in the line-up of 1927's "Murderers' Row," Gehrig drove in 175 runs that season and won the American League Most Valuable Player award. He was a quiet, methodical, immensely strong hitter, more reliable than Ruth and almost as dangerous — .340 lifetime average, 493 home runs, 1,995 runs batted in, six World Series titles, two MVP awards, the only player to hit four home runs in a single game in the 1932 season. The 2,130 consecutive games he played from 1925 to 1939 stood as the major-league record for fifty-six years, until Cal Ripken Jr. of the Baltimore Orioles broke it in 1995.

By the spring of 1939 his hitting had fallen off catastrophically and he was struggling to field routine balls. The Mayo Clinic that June diagnosed amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, a degenerative motor-neuron disease that the public has called Lou Gehrig's disease ever since. On Lou Gehrig Appreciation Day at Yankee Stadium on 4 July 1939, in a hatless speech of two minutes, he told 62,000 fans that he considered himself "the luckiest man on the face of the earth"; the line passed at once into American memory. He died at home in the Bronx in June 1941, thirty-seven years old, having retired with his wife Eleanor and the Yankees' uniform number 4, the first to be retired by any major-league team. Gary Cooper played him three years later in The Pride of the Yankees.
