Sigmund Freud

Austrian founder of psychoanalysis, 1856–1939

Born in Moravia and raised in Vienna, Freud trained as a neurologist and studied the treatment of hysteria under Charcot in Paris. Working with Josef Breuer on patients whose symptoms eased when they spoke freely of their pasts, he conceived the “talking cure” and from it built an entirely new account of the mind. He argued that human beings are driven by unconscious wishes, that childhood and sexuality shape the adult psyche, and that dreams are the disguised fulfilment of repressed desires.

In a stream of bold and controversial works—The Interpretation of Dreams, his theory of the Oedipus complex, and the division of the psyche into id, ego, and superego—he made psychoanalysis a movement, gathering disciples around him in Vienna. The most gifted of them quarrelled and broke away, but his vocabulary of repression, neurosis, and the unconscious seeped into the whole of twentieth-century thought, literature, and art. He even exchanged open letters with Einstein on the roots of war.

A Jew, Freud saw his books burned by the Nazis and, after the annexation of Austria in 1938, was allowed to escape to London, where the Gestapo had first interrogated his daughter Anna. Suffering from a long and painful cancer of the jaw brought on by his beloved cigars, he died in London in 1939. Few thinkers have been so fiercely disputed or so deeply influential. His chosen heir, who later founded a rival school, was Carl Jung.
