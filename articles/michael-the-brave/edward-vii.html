Edward VII

1841–1910, King of the United Kingdom and the British Dominions

Albert Edward, known in his family as Bertie, was born at Buckingham Palace, the eldest son and second child of Queen Victoria and Prince Albert. His parents, fearing him slow, imposed a stifling educational regime; tutors put him through Greek, Latin and Aristotle, while Albert's death in 1861 was attributed by the bereaved Victoria in part to the chill caught visiting Bertie at Cambridge over a barracks-scandal at the Curragh. She blamed him for it for forty years and kept him at distance from the business of monarchy.

In 1863 he married Princess Alexandra of Denmark, and as Prince of Wales he became the standard-bearer of fashionable London. He toured the empire, opened hospitals and bridges, and presided over Marlborough House and its smart, sometimes raffish circle, his mistresses including Lillie Langtry, Daisy Greville, Countess of Warwick, and the much younger Alice Keppel. The Tranby Croft baccarat scandal of 1890 put him in the witness box, the only Prince of Wales to give evidence in a civil court since the seventeenth century, and confirmed Victoria's worst expectations.

He inherited the throne on Victoria's death in January 1901, almost sixty years old. The reign, only nine years long, gave its name to the Edwardian age. He played little part in the legislation of his ministers under Lord Salisbury, Arthur Balfour and Herbert Asquith, but transformed the personal diplomacy of the monarchy. State visits to Paris in 1903 prepared the Entente Cordiale with France; a return visit to Reval in 1908 helped clear the way for the Anglo-Russian agreement with his nephew Nicholas II. His nephew Wilhelm II of Germany, who detested him, blamed him through the war for encircling the Reich.

He smoked twenty cigarettes and twelve cigars a day and was repeatedly bronchitic. He died at Buckingham Palace in May 1910 of pneumonia and heart failure, sixty-eight years old; his son Bertie, who took the throne as George V, found him on his deathbed and was told the Liberal government's last act would be the Parliament Act curbing the Lords.

The road continues to George V.
