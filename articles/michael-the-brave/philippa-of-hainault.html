Philippa of Hainault

Queen of England, consort of Edward III, c. 1314–1369

Philippa was born about 1314 in the county of Hainault, a daughter of its count, and her marriage was a matter of high politics. The bargain that brought her to England in 1328 as the wife of the young Edward III had been struck to win Hainault’s soldiers and ships for the invasion that had just toppled Edward II. From that calculated beginning grew one of the happiest of royal marriages and the reign of a queen long remembered with affection as “Good Queen Philippa.”

She bore Edward III a great brood of children, among them Edward the Black Prince, Lionel of Antwerp, John of Gaunt and Edmund of Langley, whose descendants would one day tear England apart in the Wars of the Roses. Gracious and steady, she often travelled with the king on campaign, and the chroniclers credited her with rallying the realm’s defence against a Scottish invasion in 1346. Her most famous moment came the next year at Calais: when Edward meant to execute six burghers who had surrendered the starved city, Philippa, heavy with child, knelt before him and begged their lives, and the king relented.

She was a patroness of learning — an Oxford college was founded in her honour — and a friend to poets and clerks, including the young Jean Froissart, who served in her household and never forgot her kindness. Her death in 1369 was widely mourned, and the long reign she had graced slid afterward into decline.
