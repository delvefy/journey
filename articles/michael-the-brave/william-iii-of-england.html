William III of England

William of Orange, Stadtholder and King, 1650–1702

William III, Prince of Orange, was the Dutch ruler who became king of England and made the resistance to Louis XIV the central business of his life. Born at The Hague in 1650, a posthumous son whose mother was a daughter of Charles I of England, he grew up sidelined by the republican party that distrusted his princely house.

His hour came in 1672, the Dutch “year of disaster,” when France and England fell upon the Republic at once. Raised to the offices of stadtholder and captain-general, William rallied a broken country, opened the dikes to flood the invader's path, and emerged as the indispensable leader of every European coalition against French power. In 1677 he married his cousin Mary, elder daughter of the future James II.

When James, a Catholic, alienated his Protestant subjects and a Catholic heir was born, English leaders invited William to intervene. He landed with a Dutch army in 1688; James fled, and Parliament offered the crowns to William and Mary as joint sovereigns, bound now by a Bill of Rights that curbed the monarchy for good. In 1690 he broke the deposed king's cause at the Battle of the Boyne in Ireland.

Mary died in 1694, and William ruled alone, austere and asthmatic, never loved by his English subjects but respected as the architect of the alliance that checked France. He died in 1702 from a fall when his horse stumbled, leaving no children, and the throne passed to his sister-in-law Anne. The king he had driven into exile was his own father-in-law, James II of England.
