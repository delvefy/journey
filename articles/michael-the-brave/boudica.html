Boudica

Queen of the Iceni, British war-leader, died c. AD 61

Boudica was the wife of Prasutagus, a client king of the Iceni in eastern Britain who had kept his throne by alliance with Rome. On his death he left his kingdom jointly to his daughters and to the emperor Nero, hoping to protect his family; instead the Romans seized everything. According to Tacitus, the queen was flogged and her daughters violated, and the leading Iceni were stripped of their lands. Out of that outrage came one of the fiercest revolts the empire ever faced in the west.

In AD 60 or 61, while the governor Suetonius Paulinus was campaigning far away in Wales, Boudica united the Iceni, the Trinovantes, and other tribes and fell upon the symbols of Roman power. Her host destroyed the colony of Camulodunum, where the hated temple of the deified Claudius stood, then burned Londinium and Verulamium, putting tens of thousands to the sword.

Suetonius Paulinus hurried back and, though heavily outnumbered, chose his ground well; in a single pitched battle the discipline of his legions broke the vast but disordered British army with terrible slaughter. Boudica did not long survive the defeat, dying—by poison, says Tacitus—soon after. Her rising failed, but the memory of the warrior queen who burned Roman London endured. Preserved in the pages of Tacitus and Cassius Dio, she rose again in later ages as a national heroine of Britain, celebrated in monument and verse.
