Alexios IV Angelos

c. 1182 – 1204, Byzantine co-emperor with his father Isaac II, 1203–1204

Alexios was born about 1182, the only son of the emperor Isaac II Angelos and his first wife Eirene, the daughter of Andronikos Doukas Kamateros. He grew up at the court of his father between 1185 and 1195 as the obvious heir to the throne, a boy of twelve when his uncle Alexios deposed and blinded Isaac at Kypsella in Thrace. The new emperor, his namesake, did not kill him, possibly because he was a child, possibly because Euphrosyne Doukaina Kamatera, the empress, took a hand in his protection; he was, however, kept under close watch in modest apartments of the Great Palace and given no station, no household, and no military training. He passed the next six years in this confinement, growing from a boy into an adolescent of nineteen with no formal position and a memory of his father in the prison of the Anemas tower a few streets away.

In the autumn of 1201 he escaped. The chroniclers disagree about the means, but the most plausible account, in Niketas Choniates, has it that he was smuggled out of the city in an empty wine cask aboard a Pisan ship sailing for Ancona; one version adds that he had his hair cut short on board to pass for a deck hand. He landed in Italy, made his way overland through the Po valley, and presented himself before Christmas 1201 at the court of his half-sister Eirene Angelina and her second husband Philip of Swabia, the king of Germany and the head of the Hohenstaufen interest in Europe. Philip was preparing his own war for the imperial title against Otto of Brunswick and could not march east in person, but he wrote to the Pope and to the leaders of the Fourth Crusade then assembling in Venice, urging that the boy’s claim be taken up. In May 1202 Alexios joined his brother-in-law’s ambassadors at the court of the marquess Boniface of Montferrat, who had been elected leader of the crusade in the previous summer; by the spring of 1203 he was on the Venetian island of Corfu, on board the assembled fleet, with a written agreement from the crusader princes and the Doge of Venice, Enrico Dandolo, to restore him and his father to the throne of Constantinople.

The terms he had signed at Zara in January 1203 were ruinous. In return for the diversion of the crusade he undertook to pay the crusaders 200,000 silver marks, to provision their army for a year, to supply ten thousand Byzantine soldiers to the expedition to Egypt, to maintain five hundred knights in the Holy Land at imperial expense for the rest of his life, and — the clause that mattered most in Rome — to bring the Greek Church under the obedience of the Pope. He had no authority to promise any of these things; he was a fugitive with no money, no court, no army, and no consent from his subjects. He believed, or had been persuaded to believe, that as soon as the fleet appeared off Constantinople the population of the city would rise in his favour and that the wealth of the imperial treasury would meet the bills.

It was the central miscalculation of his short life. The fleet arrived in the Bosphorus on 23 June 1203 and the boy was paraded under the sea walls in a galley with a herald shouting his name; the population of Constantinople, who had not seen him in two years and who blamed him for the foreign army at their gates, was silent. The crusaders forced a landing at Galata, broke the harbour boom of the Golden Horn, and on 17 July assaulted the Blachernae quarter. That night Alexios III fled with the treasury; on the following morning the courtiers who remained in the city opened the prison of the Anemas tower, brought out the blind Isaac, and acclaimed him emperor in Hagia Sophia. The Latins demanded that the boy be crowned alongside his father; on 1 August 1203 Alexios IV was anointed co-emperor by the patriarch in Hagia Sophia, his blind father seated beside him on the throne. He was twenty.

The six months that followed were the slow ruin of the bargain he had made. The treasury was almost empty — his uncle had taken most of what remained — and even the melting of imperial plate and the stripping of icons of their gold and silver revetments produced only a fraction of the sum he had promised. To pay the crusaders he raised confiscatory taxes on the merchant houses, levied a forced contribution on the Latin quarter, and ordered Hagia Sophia stripped of its silver lamps; each measure deepened the hostility of the populace and of the Greek church without satisfying the army outside the walls. He travelled in person with a small body of Latin knights into Thrace in the autumn of 1203, partly to compel his uncle to surrender, partly to raise money from the provinces; the campaign was inconclusive. While he was away, on 19 August, a brawl between Latin sailors and a small Muslim community in the city set off the Great Fire of Constantinople of 1203, which burned for two days and nights and destroyed a swathe of the city from the Golden Horn nearly to the Sea of Marmara. The blame fell on him.

By December the city was in open revolt. A noble faction at court, led by the protovestiarios Alexios Doukas, nicknamed Mourtzouphlos for his joined eyebrows, took over the day-to-day government and made it clear that the agreement with the Latins would not be honoured. On the night of 27–28 January 1204 Mourtzouphlos broke into the imperial apartments, told the young emperor that the people were storming the palace, hurried him out a back stair with the promise of escape, and conducted him to a cell where he was strangled with a bowstring. The official notice spoke of a sudden illness. The body was buried discreetly; the blind Isaac, told of his son’s death, died himself within days of grief, fever, or violence. Mourtzouphlos was crowned emperor as Alexios V the next morning. Three months later, on 12 April 1204, the crusader fleet stormed the walls and the city fell. The Latin Empire of Constantinople, the lasting consequence of the boy’s desperate journey from the wine cask to the throne, was proclaimed five weeks after that.
