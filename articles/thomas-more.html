Thomas More

1478 – 1535, English humanist, Lord Chancellor of England, and Catholic martyr

Thomas More was born in London on 7 February 1478, the son of Sir John More, a London lawyer who rose to become a judge of the King's Bench, and Agnes Graunger. He was sent first to St Anthony's School in Threadneedle Street, the best Latin grammar school in the city, and at about thirteen was placed as a page in the household of John Morton, Cardinal Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Chancellor under Henry VII. Morton was a politician of the first rank and a careful judge of boys; he told visitors that the page who waited on his table would prove a marvellous man. From Morton's household More was sent in 1492 to Canterbury College at Oxford, where he learned Greek under the early English Hellenists Thomas Linacre and William Grocyn. After about two years his father, impatient with humanist studies and intent on a legal career for his son, called him back to London to study law at New Inn and from 1496 at Lincoln's Inn.

While reading law, More entered into an intense religious experimentation. From about 1500 to 1504 he lived in or beside the London Charterhouse, the Carthusian monastery just outside the city wall, sharing in the monks' prayers and disciplines and considering taking the habit himself. He also weighed the Franciscan Observants. In the end he decided, as he later told Erasmus, that he could not in conscience promise himself a life of chastity, and chose marriage instead; but he kept the habit of wearing a hair shirt under his clothes for the rest of his life. He married Jane Colt in 1505 and had four children with her, the eldest his beloved daughter Margaret. Jane died in 1511, and within a month More had married a widow, Alice Middleton, older than himself and notoriously sharp-tongued, who managed his expanding household at Bucklersbury and later at Chelsea.

His friendship with Erasmus, formed when the Dutch humanist first visited England in 1499, became the central intellectual relationship of his life. It was while staying at More's London house in 1509 that Erasmus composed the Praise of Folly, dedicating it to him in a Latin pun on his name — Moriae Encomium. The two men corresponded for the next quarter century, and More acted as Erasmus's chief patron and protector in England. With John Colet, the dean of St Paul's, and Cuthbert Tunstall they formed the core of the early English Christian humanist circle.

More's own most famous book grew out of a diplomatic embassy. In 1515 he was sent to Bruges to negotiate a wool-trade dispute, and during a lull in the talks he visited Peter Giles in Antwerp and began the Latin dialogue that became Utopia, published at Louvain in 1516. Cast as a traveller's tale told by the fictional Raphael Hythloday, the work describes an island commonwealth where property is held in common, religious toleration is general, war is despised, and the daily life of the citizens is regulated by reason rather than honour or appetite. Its purpose has been argued over ever since: at the least it is a savage satire on the European order of his day, particularly its enclosures, its hereditary idleness, its capital punishment for theft, and its dynastic wars. He also worked in these years on a Latin and English History of King Richard III, written probably between 1513 and 1518 and never quite finished, which became Shakespeare's principal source for the play of that name and one of the foundational works of English humanist prose.

From 1517 More was drawn into the personal service of Henry VIII, who delighted in his company and walked with him in the gardens at Chelsea with an arm around his neck. He served as Master of Requests, then as a member of the king's council, was knighted in 1521, became Under-Treasurer, and in 1523 was elected Speaker of the House of Commons, where he defended the privilege of free speech against Cardinal Wolsey's attempt to overawe the house. He was made Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster in 1525 and, on Wolsey's fall in 1529, Lord Chancellor of England — the first layman to hold the office in living memory.

By that point the king's matrimonial crisis had begun. Henry had decided that his marriage to Catherine of Aragon was invalid and that he must marry Anne Boleyn; he had decided also, when the pope would not grant the annulment, that he must break with Rome. More had accepted the chancellorship on the express understanding that he would not be required to involve himself in the king's "great matter". As chancellor he prosecuted heretics, particularly the Lutheran underground in London, with a vigour that has troubled his admirers ever since — six men were burned during his tenure — and he wrote enormous polemics in English against William Tyndale and Simon Fish. But he refused to support the king's claim to be supreme head of the church in England, and on 16 May 1532, the day after the clergy submitted to the king, he resigned the great seal.

For nearly two years he lived in retirement at Chelsea, writing devotional treatises, increasingly isolated. He refused to attend the coronation of Anne Boleyn in June 1533, and in April 1534 he was summoned with John Fisher, Bishop of Rochester, to swear the oath required by the Act of Succession, which not only acknowledged the children of Anne as legitimate heirs but in its preamble repudiated the authority of the pope. He was willing to swear to the succession but not to the preamble. The distinction was not accepted. On 17 April 1534 he was committed to the Tower of London.

He remained in the Tower for fifteen months, allowed at first books and visits from his daughter Margaret Roper, then deprived of both. He wrote there the Dialogue of Comfort against Tribulation and a meditation on Christ's agony in Gethsemane, the De Tristitia Christi, in his own neat humanist hand — works of an extraordinary calm. On 1 July 1535 he was tried at Westminster Hall on the charge of treasonably denying the king's supremacy, on the evidence, almost certainly perjured, of the Solicitor General Richard Rich. He was convicted and condemned to death. On 6 July 1535 he was beheaded on Tower Hill, declaring himself the king's good servant, but God's first. His head was set on a pole on London Bridge; Margaret, by tradition, retrieved it.

His reputation was contested from the moment of his death. Erasmus, hearing the news in Basel, said that in More's death he himself had died. The English Reformation had no use for him for centuries. The Catholic Church beatified him in 1886 and canonised him in 1935, on the four-hundredth anniversary of his execution. In the modern period he has been read as the patron of conscience against the state, as the prophet of a more humane political economy, and, by his critics, as the burner of Protestants who refused others the freedom of conscience he claimed for himself. The contradictions have not made him smaller. He remains, with Erasmus, the human face of the Christian humanism that the religious wars destroyed.
