Ludwig IV, Holy Roman Emperor

1282 – 1347, Ludwig the Bavarian, Wittelsbach duke of Upper Bavaria, Holy Roman Emperor, the great antagonist of the Avignon papacy

Ludwig of Wittelsbach was born on 1 April 1282 at Munich, the second son of Duke Louis II of Upper Bavaria and Mathilda of Habsburg, the daughter of King Rudolf I. He was thus through his mother a nephew of Albert I of Germany, the Habsburg king, and through his father a member of the Wittelsbach line that had ruled Bavaria since 1180. He was raised at the Viennese court of his Habsburg uncle as the close companion of his cousin Frederick, the future Frederick the Fair, with whom he would later contest the empire by arms. He inherited Upper Bavaria jointly with his elder brother Rudolf in 1294 and ruled it more or less alone from 1301 after a partition. The first political success of his reign was at the Battle of Gammelsdorf on 9 November 1313, where with a small army of Bavarian and Salzburg vassals he defeated his cousin Frederick and forced a temporary peace, and the guardianship of the children of the dead Stephen I of Lower Bavaria.

The death of Emperor Henry VII of Luxembourg in 1313 opened a contested imperial election. Four of the seven electors — the King of Bohemia (John of Luxembourg, son of the late emperor), the Archbishop of Mainz, the Margrave of Brandenburg, and the Duke of Saxony — chose Ludwig on 20 October 1314. The remaining three plus a disputed fourth voted on the same day for Frederick of Habsburg. There were thus two simultaneous coronations the next month, Ludwig at Aachen on 25 November and Frederick at Bonn on the same day; both used the proper insignia (Frederick the imperial crown, Ludwig the regalia of the Empire); both were anointed by the proper bishops. The war that resulted was long and inconclusive, fought across Swabia and Bavaria for eight years. It was settled by the Battle of Mühldorf on the Inn on 28 September 1322, where Ludwig, with the support of John of Luxembourg and the Burgrave Frederick IV of Nuremberg, captured Frederick along with thirteen hundred of the Austrian nobility. He kept his cousin imprisoned at Trausnitz Castle in the Upper Palatinate for three years.

The reconciliation with Frederick is the most curious episode of his reign. The Treaty of Trausnitz (1325) released Frederick on his oath to renounce the empire and bring his Habsburg kindred to submission; when Frederick's brother Leopold refused, Frederick returned voluntarily to captivity. Ludwig, moved by the gesture, offered him a joint kingship at the Treaty of Munich on 5 September 1325 — a formal arrangement under which the two cousins would rule as co-kings of the Romans, an unprecedented constitutional novelty. The pope refused to ratify it; the electors and the imperial princes were divided; Frederick withdrew, broken in health, and died at Gutenstein on 13 January 1330. Ludwig was henceforth sole king.

The papacy in this period was the Avignon papacy, and from 1316 it had been in the hands of John XXII, the fierce Cahors jurist who held the strongest theory of papal supremacy of any pope since Boniface VIII. The collision was structural and unavoidable. John XXII held that no king of the Romans could exercise the imperial dignities without explicit papal approbation; Ludwig, having been validly elected by the German princes and having defeated his rival in battle, held that he held the empire directly from God. The first crisis came in October 1323, when John demanded that Ludwig present his title for confirmation; Ludwig refused, John declared him deposed in March 1324 and excommunicated him in July. Ludwig answered with the Sachsenhausen Appellation of May 1324, declaring John XXII a heretic for his theological positions on the poverty of Christ, and appealing to a general council of the church for his deposition.

The doctrinal apparatus of his quarrel with John XXII was supplied, with extraordinary intellectual force, by refugees from Avignon and from the Spiritual Franciscans. Marsilius of Padua published the Defensor Pacis in 1324, a treatise so radical in its rejection of papal authority — locating sovereignty in the people and limiting clergy to spiritual functions only — that Marsilius fled north when its authorship became known and arrived at Ludwig's court in Munich in 1326. He was followed in 1328 by William of Ockham, the great English Franciscan, who escaped from house arrest at Avignon over the question of evangelical poverty and presented himself to Ludwig at Pisa with the famous offer: "Defend me with the sword and I will defend you with the pen." Ockham produced, over the following two decades at Munich, a stream of polemical treatises on the limits of papal authority that remained foundational to the conciliar movement and, indirectly, to the Reformation.

In 1327 Ludwig descended into Italy with a small army, was crowned with the Iron Crown of Lombardy at Milan on Pentecost, marched south through the lands of the Ghibelline lords of central Italy, and on 17 January 1328 entered Rome. There, in Saint Peter's, he was crowned emperor by Sciarra Colonna acting as captain of the Roman people — not by any pope, but by the formal representative of the Roman commune. It was the first imperial coronation in centuries to dispense with papal participation, and a deliberate constitutional point. Three months later, on 18 April 1328, Ludwig formally declared John XXII deposed for heresy and on 12 May had the Spiritual Franciscan Pietro Rainalducci elected as antipope Nicholas V by a small synod of obedient clerics. The antipope was a dignified figure but a political dead end; he was abandoned by Ludwig when the Roman populace turned against the German garrison, surrendered to John XXII at Avignon in 1330, and ended his life as a pensioned prisoner in the papal palace there.

Ludwig withdrew from Italy in 1330 and never returned. The next decade and a half were spent in steady territorial consolidation: he acquired the March of Brandenburg from the extinct Ascanian line for his son in 1323, the County of Holland and Hainaut for another son in 1345, and most controversially the County of Tyrol for his son Louis the Brandenburger by the divorce, on his own imperial authority, of Margaret Maultasch from her Luxembourg husband in 1342 — a precedent in matrimonial law deeply offensive to canon lawyers and to the Luxembourg family. The Declaration of Rhense of 16 July 1338, by which the six lay and ecclesiastical electors of the empire jointly declared that the king of the Romans elected by them required no papal confirmation, was the constitutional answer to John XXII and to John's successor Benedict XII; the imperial law Licet Iuris, promulgated by Ludwig at the Diet of Frankfurt in August 1338, made it law.

His last years were dominated by the slow rise of his old supporter's son. John of Luxembourg, who had backed Ludwig at Mühldorf, was estranged by the Tyrol affair; John's son Charles, raised at the French court, succeeded as Charles IV of Bohemia in 1346. With Pope Clement VI's active backing, the five anti-Wittelsbach electors met at Rhense on 11 July 1346 and chose Charles as anti-king. Ludwig prepared to fight; he died on 11 October 1347 at Puch near Fürstenfeld during a bear hunt, struck by what was apparently a stroke, at the age of sixty-five. Charles IV, his lifelong opponent, succeeded uncontested. The Wittelsbach Empire, the only imperial dignity ever held by that house until the eighteenth century, ended with him.

Ludwig's reputation has been mixed. To imperial-Ghibelline tradition and to later Protestant historiography he is the model of secular resistance to papal overreach, the patron of Marsilius and Ockham and the legal author of imperial autonomy. To papal and Guelf tradition he is the German tyrant who set up an antipope, divorced a Tyrolese countess on his own authority, and harboured every heretic and rebel friar of his age. The Wittelsbachs of Bavaria honoured him as the founder of their dynasty's imperial dignity; the Habsburgs, descendants of his rival Frederick, never quite forgave him.

The road continues to Pope John XXII.
