Constance of Sicily

1154 – 1198, heiress of the Norman kingdom, empress of Henry VI, mother of Frederick II

Constance was born on 2 November 1154 in the royal palace of Palermo, the posthumous daughter of Roger II of Sicily and his third wife Beatrix of Rethel. Her father, the great Hauteville king who had welded the Norman conquests of southern Italy into a single kingdom, had died on 26 February 1154 of what the court physicians described as exhaustion at the age of fifty-eight, leaving Beatrix in the eighth month of her pregnancy. The court of Palermo through which the child grew up was the most extraordinary in twelfth-century Europe: a state with a Greek-speaking Sicilian peasantry, an Arab-speaking artisan and commercial class in the towns, a Latin Norman feudal nobility, a Jewish administrative quarter, a clergy of three rites, and a chancery in which charters could be issued in any of four languages. Her elder half-brother William I, called the Bad by his enemies, ruled in succession; after his death in 1166 the throne passed to his eldest son, her nephew William II, called the Good, only a year older than herself.

Her place in the dynastic order was peculiar from the start. William II reached his majority in 1171 and married Joanna of England, sister of Richard the Lionheart, in 1177; the marriage produced no surviving children. As long as the king was young, the position of his unmarried aunt Constance was indeterminate: she had revenues but no obvious public role, lived in retirement at Palermo, and was given an unusually thorough Latin education for a princess of the Norman court. The chroniclers of the next generation, prompted by the disasters that followed her marriage, reported a prophecy by Joachim of Fiore that "her marriage would bring ruin to the kingdom of Sicily", and various legendary embellishments: that she had been placed in a convent for the safety of her royal nephews, that she had been compelled out of religious life only when the marriage was unavoidable. The actual evidence places her at the lay court, unmarried but not consecrated, throughout her thirties.

By 1184 William II had been ten years married to Joanna without children and was openly preparing for a contested succession. The natural alternative was his half-cousin Tancred of Lecce, an illegitimate son of his uncle Roger of Apulia — politically energetic but compromised by his birth. The other was the unmarried aunt Constance. To secure her inheritance against the local opposition that an illegitimate cousin would in any case be raised to provide, William entered into negotiations with Frederick Barbarossa, who needed a corresponding alliance in the south to balance the Italian communes. In October 1184, at the Diet of Augsburg, the marriage of Constance to Barbarossa's son and heir Henry was agreed. She left Palermo in August 1185 with a great household of Sicilian officials and a treasure of unprecedented dowry, was met at the Brenner Pass by the imperial escort, and married Henry at the cathedral of Milan on 27 January 1186. She was thirty-one, eleven years her bridegroom's senior, and at the same ceremony was crowned queen of Italy by the patriarch of Aquileia.

The five years that followed she spent mainly in Germany, learning German enough to function in the imperial household, supervising the small Sicilian establishment that accompanied her, and producing no children, an outcome that was beginning by 1188 or 1189 to be discussed by both courts. On 18 November 1189, in his royal bed at Palermo, William II died at thirty-six of an undiagnosed illness, after a final fever of two days. The Sicilian magnates, gathered at the cathedral in council, declined to acknowledge the German connection of the absent Constance and her absent husband and elected Tancred of Lecce as king. The position of the new empress — Barbarossa had died in the Saleph in June 1190 and Henry had succeeded as emperor — depended now on the recovery of her kingdom by armed force.

Henry's first expedition of 1191 took her south as far as the imperial siege lines outside Naples, where the German army was defeated by a malarial epidemic. He sent her with a small German garrison to hold the city of Salerno against Tancred while he himself fell back north. The Salernitans, on Henry's withdrawal, declared for Tancred and surrendered Constance to him with the keys of the citadel. She was held at the royal castle at Palermo for some months, treated by Tancred with the formal courtesy due to her rank, and finally sent in 1192 toward Rome to be transferred to the custody of Pope Celestine III as part of an understanding by which Tancred sought papal recognition. The papal escort that took her north of Frosinone was intercepted by imperial partisans, and Constance was conducted to Germany with a haste that disclaimed any responsibility for the rescue.

The kingdom of Sicily came to her at last only at almost forty. Tancred died on 20 February 1194; the boy heir William III could not hold the magnates; Henry, with a great army and the treasure of Richard the Lionheart's ransom, swept down the peninsula in the summer and autumn, and on 20 November 1194 entered Palermo without resistance. Constance, who had crossed the Alps with him as far as Apulia and stopped there because her late pregnancy had made further travel unwise, was at the small town of Jesi in the Marches when the imperial herald reached her with the news of the surrender of Palermo on 24 November. On 25 December Henry was crowned king of Sicily in the cathedral that her father had built. On 26 December, in the market square of Jesi, in a great open tent set up for the purpose, with the women of the surrounding towns invited in as witnesses, Constance gave birth to her only child Frederick. The public delivery, organised at her own insistence, was intended to silence the rumours that would otherwise inevitably attach to a male heir born to a woman of forty after eight years of childless marriage. Both the date and the manner became among the most famous facts of European history of the next century.

The two years between the birth and her husband's death were the politically darkest of her life. Henry treated Sicily as a conquered country, kept her at the Apulian courts of Bari and Foggia rather than at Palermo, where she would have been the focus of the Norman party; she was given no formal share in the administration of the kingdom that was in name still hers, and was not consulted on the suppression of the great conspiracy of 1197 in which a number of her own kinsmen were among the victims. When Henry died at Messina on 28 September 1197, Constance acted with a decisiveness that the political class on both sides of the Alps had not expected from her. She secured Frederick at Foggia, brought him to Palermo, sent the most prominent German officials of the kingdom out of Sicily with a substantial escort and an order that they were not to return, dismissed the cousin Markward of Anweiler whom Henry had set up as imperial regent, and asserted the regency of the kingdom in her son's name. She did not take the boy to Germany when she might have; the choice committed Frederick to being a Sicilian king of the Norman line first, and a German king or emperor only thereafter, and proved the decision that shaped the next half-century of European politics.

She had the three-year-old crowned king of Sicily at Palermo on 17 May 1198. She came to terms with the new pope Innocent III, who had succeeded Celestine III in January, by sacrifices that the chroniclers of the next generation reckoned heavy: she gave up the apostolic legateship of Sicily, a privilege the Norman kings had held since Urban II's grant to Roger I in 1098, which had given the Sicilian crown an authority over its own church that no other Latin kingdom enjoyed. She accepted Innocent III's overlordship of the kingdom, paid the back tribute, and agreed in her will to commit Frederick to his guardianship. Her health, never strong since the late pregnancy of 1194, broke down in the autumn of 1198. She made her testament in the first week of November, with the cardinals of the Roman party at the bedside. She died at Palermo on 27 November 1198, aged forty-four, fourteen months after her husband and the day after sending her last letter to the pope of Rome. She was buried in the cathedral that her father had built, in the porphyry sarcophagus beside Henry's and beside Roger's, where she lies among the kings of her line.
