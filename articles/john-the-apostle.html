John the Apostle

fl. c. AD 30 – c. 100 · fisherman of Galilee, gospel-writer, exile of Patmos

John, son of Zebedee, was a Galilean fisherman called by Jesus while mending nets with his brother James on the shore of the lake near Capernaum. He is named, with that brother, in the inner circle of Peter, James, and John whom the gospels show closest to Jesus at the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor, at the raising of the daughter of Jairus, and at the agony in the garden of Gethsemane. The fourth gospel and three short letters of the New Testament have been ascribed to him from the second century onward, as has the apocalyptic book of Revelation written from the Aegean island of Patmos. He is the only one of the Twelve who, according to a tradition as old as the writings themselves, did not die a martyr but lived into extreme old age in the city of Ephesus on the coast of Asia Minor.

The synoptic gospels describe him and his brother as the sons of Zebedee, a fisherman of some means whose boats employed hired servants and whose wife, perhaps named Salome, was among the women who followed Jesus from Galilee. Jesus is said to have surnamed the two brothers Boanerges, sons of thunder — a nickname that fits the moment when they ask, after a Samaritan village refuses Jesus hospitality, whether they may call down fire from heaven, and the moment when, alone among the Twelve, they ask Jesus to seat them on his right and left in the kingdom and are rebuked.

The fourth gospel, which never names its principal disciple but refers to him repeatedly as the disciple whom Jesus loved, places this anonymous figure at the Last Supper, leaning on Jesus's breast and asking who would betray him; at the foot of the cross, where Jesus entrusts his mother to him with the words "Woman, behold thy son … Son, behold thy mother"; at the empty tomb, outrunning Peter to it on Easter morning but waiting at the entrance; and on the lakeshore in Galilee, where he is the first to recognize the risen Jesus on the beach at dawn. The early church identified that disciple with John the apostle, and the closing chapter of the gospel hints at a community gathered around him in old age, expecting his survival until Christ's return.

In the book of Acts he appears at first as Peter's near-constant companion. The two go up together to the Temple to pray and heal the crippled man at the Beautiful Gate; they are arrested and brought before the Sanhedrin of Annas and Caiaphas, who, finding them unlettered and ordinary men, can do nothing but warn them and let them go; they are sent later to Samaria to lay hands on the new believers there. After the death of his brother James at the hands of Herod Agrippa I in AD 44 — the first of the Twelve to be martyred — John continues to appear in the narrative as one of the pillars of the Jerusalem church, alongside Peter and James the brother of Jesus, whom Paul met on his visit in the same period.

After about AD 50 the New Testament records his movements no more. The early tradition, attested by Polycrates of Ephesus in the second century and by Irenaeus, who claimed to have heard the report from Polycarp of Smyrna, who had known him personally, is that John settled in Ephesus, the great Roman city of Asia, and lived there into the reign of the emperor Trajan, dying around the year 100. From Ephesus, the same tradition says, he wrote the fourth gospel and the three letters that bear his name, and from there he was banished, under the emperor Domitian, to the rocky Aegean island of Patmos. There, the opening of the book of Revelation reports, he received in a long sequence of visions the apocalyptic images that became the closing book of the Christian Bible: the seven churches, the throne of God, the four horsemen, the woman clothed with the sun, the beast and his number, the millennium, the new Jerusalem descending out of heaven.

Modern scholarship has dispersed this single figure into perhaps several. The hand of the fourth gospel and the three letters seems to be one author or one tightly bound school; the Greek of Revelation is so different in style and grammar that many historians attribute it to a separate writer, sometimes called John the Elder, also based in Asia and possibly conflated in tradition with the apostle. The early church, however, knew only one John of Ephesus, and his memory was carried into the next generations by men who had heard him speak: Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, martyred in his eighties, who said he had sat at the feet of John; and through Polycarp to Irenaeus of Lyons, who carried the line into the late second century.

The most arresting story preserved about his old age comes from Jerome and Cassian, who report that when he had grown too feeble to walk into the assembly at Ephesus he was carried in, and could no longer manage a sermon, he would repeat the single phrase, "Little children, love one another." Asked why he said no more, he answered that this was the Lord's commandment, and if it alone were kept, it would be enough. Another tradition, attested by Clement of Alexandria, has him pursuing a young convert who had fallen into banditry and embracing him at the head of a robber band to recall him to faith.

He is buried, according to the same Ephesian tradition, on a hill above the harbor at Selçuk, where a small church marked his tomb in the second century and where Justinian raised a great six-domed basilica in the sixth, its ruins still legible on the slope. The man whom the gospels show as a young fisherman calling for fire from heaven on a village of Samaritans is remembered in the closing scenes of his life as an old man asking only that his hearers love one another — the arc of a life across some seventy years that began at a lakeshore in Galilee and ended on a hill in Asia Minor.
