Francis I of France

1494 – 1547, King of France, victor of Marignano, prisoner of Pavia, patron of the French Renaissance

Francis was born at Cognac on 12 September 1494, the son of Charles, Count of Angoulême, of a junior Valois branch, and of Louise of Savoy, then only seventeen years old. His father died when he was less than two. Louise, intelligent, ambitious, and fiercely focused, raised him with his sister Marguerite at Amboise and Cognac, where she had him groomed, against considerable improbability, for the French throne. The reigning Louis XII had no son. In 1514 Francis was married to Louis's elder daughter Claude, securing the inheritance of Brittany; on 1 January 1515 Louis died and Francis became king at twenty.

His first act as king was to march into Italy. Louis XII had been driven from Milan in 1513 by the Swiss; Francis crossed the Alps in the summer of 1515 by a difficult mountain pass with thousands of pioneers cutting roads ahead of him, and on 13–14 September fought, near Marignano outside Milan, a two-day battle against the Swiss pikemen of the Sforza. The French heavy cavalry and Italian artillery wore the Swiss down at frightful cost. It was the first defeat of Swiss infantry in a generation and was treated in Europe as a feat of personal courage by the young king, who was knighted on the field by the Chevalier Bayard. Milan was his. He concluded with Pope Leo X the Concordat of Bologna in 1516, which gave the French crown the right to nominate bishops and abbots and was, in practical terms, the foundation of the Gallican church for the next three centuries.

From the heights of Marignano his fortunes turned. In 1519 the Emperor Maximilian died, and Francis stood for the imperial election against Charles of Habsburg, Charles V. The German electors took bribes from both but chose Charles, whose territories now surrounded France on three sides: Spain to the south, the Netherlands and Franche-Comté in the north and east, the Italian dependencies of Naples and Milan. Francis spent the rest of his reign trying to break the encirclement. In 1520 he met Henry VIII at the Field of the Cloth of Gold, a vast and intricate festival of tents, banquets, and wrestling near Calais, designed to win England as an ally and failing to do so. War with Charles V began in 1521 and would continue, in four phases, for the next twenty-five years.

The disaster came on 24 February 1525, Charles V's twenty-fifth birthday, outside the walls of Pavia. The French army that had been besieging the city was caught between the walls and a relieving imperial force; the French heavy cavalry, charging the Spanish arquebusiers, was destroyed; the king himself was unhorsed, surrounded, and taken prisoner. Francis was carried in stages to Madrid, where Charles kept him for a year in conditions of increasing severity until he signed the Treaty of Madrid in January 1526, surrendering Burgundy and renouncing his Italian claims. The treaty was unenforceable. Within weeks of crossing the Pyrenees he had repudiated it, formed with Pope Clement VII the League of Cognac, and resumed the war.

The League's war went badly. In May 1527 the imperial army, mutinous for unpaid wages, sacked Rome and held the pope prisoner; Francis was forced eventually to the Peace of Cambrai in 1529, called the Ladies' Peace because his mother Louise and Charles's aunt Margaret of Austria negotiated it. He kept Burgundy but renounced Italy and ransomed his two young sons, who had been hostages in Spain for three and a half years. The peace lasted a few years. He invaded again in 1536 and 1542. In the second of these later wars he scandalised Christendom by allying openly with Suleiman the Magnificent: an Ottoman fleet under Khair-ed-Din Barbarossa wintered in 1543–1544 at Toulon, the cathedral was converted into a mosque, and a joint Franco-Ottoman force took Nice. The diplomatic logic was unanswerable; the moral cost was very high.

At home he was the prince of a court that resembled none before it. He brought to France in 1516 the aged Leonardo da Vinci, lodged him at the Clos Lucé near the royal château of Amboise, paid him a pension, and is said by Vasari to have held the painter in his arms at his death in 1519. He bought from Italian merchants and from Leonardo's heir the Mona Lisa, which has been in the royal and then the national collection of France ever since. He invited Andrea del Sarto, Rosso Fiorentino, Francesco Primaticcio, and Sebastiano Serlio; under their direction Fontainebleau was transformed into the showcase of a new French classicism. He began Chambord in 1519, the most ostentatious château ever built in Europe, with its double-helix staircase that some attribute to Leonardo's drawings. He bought the manuscripts and printed books that became the nucleus of what is now the Bibliothèque nationale, made a fixed copy-deposit obligation on French printers, and in 1530 founded the lecturers in the trilingual languages who became the Collège de France, in conscious imitation of the humanist colleges of Louvain and Alcalá.

His religious policy was nervous and contradictory. He was personally orthodox but tolerant of his sister Marguerite of Navarre, who patronised reformers; he tolerated also, for political reasons, his German Lutheran allies. The Affair of the Placards in October 1534, in which Reformed Protestants posted broadsheets attacking the Mass in Paris and on the door of his own bedchamber at Amboise, broke his patience: persecution intensified, and from then on the French Reformation was a marked, and eventually a hunted, movement.

His last years were sombre. His eldest son and heir the Dauphin Francis died in 1536, possibly of natural causes, possibly poisoned; the succession passed to the silent and resentful Henry. His mother Louise had died in 1531, Claude in 1524; his second wife, Eleanor of Austria, Charles V's sister, was a political marriage from the Cambrai peace and gave him no children. He grew bitter, ill, and exhausted. He died at the château of Rambouillet on 31 March 1547, two months after Henry VIII and less than two months before Charles V's great victory of Mühlberg.

He left France stronger as a state than he had found it, with the royal authority more concentrated, the army modernised, the territory enlarged by Brittany and stabilised in the east; he left it also entangled in the Italian and the religious questions that would, in his sons' reign, plunge the country into civil war. To later French memory he became the founder-king of the Renaissance: the patron of Leonardo and Cellini, the builder of Chambord and Fontainebleau, the prince who made his court the cultural centre of northern Europe. The defeats and the Ottoman alliance are remembered too, but at a discount.
