Duns Scotus

c. 1266 – 1308, the Subtle Doctor, Scottish Franciscan, master of haecceitas and the early champion of the Immaculate Conception

John Duns Scotus was born about 1266 in or near the village of Duns in Berwickshire on the Scottish side of the eastern border, in a family of small landowners; the surname "Duns" is his place-name, "Scotus" his identification as a Scot among Latin schoolmen, and "John" his Christian name, by which he was almost never called in the records. He entered the Order of Friars Minor as a boy, perhaps at the Dumfries convent or at Haddington, and was ordained priest on 17 March 1291 in the parish church of Saint Andrew at Northampton by the bishop of Lincoln, Oliver Sutton — the only fixed date in his early biography, and the basis from which his birth date is conjectured back. He was sent for higher studies to the Franciscan studium at Oxford and remained there, with interruptions, for most of the next decade.

The Oxford in which Scotus learned and taught was at the height of its medieval intellectual brilliance, an English university that had absorbed the Aristotelian corpus a generation earlier under masters like Robert Grosseteste and Roger Bacon, and that under its Franciscan and Dominican studia was now producing scholastic theology of a sophistication to rival Paris. The standard exercise of the advanced theology student was the lecture-course on the four books of the Sentences of Peter Lombard, and Scotus delivered such a course at Oxford about 1298–1299. The lectures, preserved in the version known as the Lectura, were revised and expanded over the rest of his life into the great Ordinatio, the unfinished masterwork on which his theological reputation principally rests; a second version, the Reportatio Parisiensis, records his Parisian re-delivery a few years later, and the Quodlibetal Questions from his Parisian regency complete the corpus of his major works.

The metaphysics he developed at Oxford and Paris is among the most intricate of any medieval thinker. The central commitment is to the univocity of being: the term "being", when predicated of God and of creatures, has, on Scotus's view, a single common meaning — in opposition to Thomas Aquinas, who had held that "being" is predicated of God and creature only analogically. From this single methodological choice flow consequences for natural theology (in which the existence of God can be demonstrated more rigorously than Aquinas had held), for the doctrine of God (where the divine attributes are not, as for Aquinas, merely human ways of thinking about a simple essence but rather formally distinct features of the divine), and for metaphysics generally. The famous Scotist "formal distinction" — a real distinction less than the distinction between separate things but more than a merely conceptual distinction — is the technical tool that holds the system together; it is what Ockham, his greatest critic, principally attacked.

The doctrine for which he is best remembered, and the one most often invoked in his name in later philosophy, is haecceitas — the "thisness" of an individual. Scotus rejected the older Thomist view that matter is the principle of individuation: for Scotus, individual things are individuated by a positive principle inherent in each, a final formal determination that makes Socrates Socrates and not merely a particular instance of human nature. Haecceitas is therefore not a thing or a property but a metaphysical feature peculiar to each existing individual. The concept supplied the late medieval world with a vocabulary for talking about individuality with a precision the ancient and patristic traditions had not had, and the term itself, once almost extinct, was revived by Charles Sanders Peirce in the nineteenth century and by Anglo-American metaphysicians in the twentieth.

His theology was as influential as his metaphysics. He was the first major scholastic systematically to defend the Immaculate Conception of the Virgin Mary — the doctrine, repudiated by Bonaventure, Aquinas, and the dominant Dominican tradition, that the Mother of God had been preserved free from original sin from the moment of her conception. Scotus's argument, "preservative redemption" — the contention that Christ had redeemed his mother in advance, by preventing the contraction of sin rather than by removing it — supplied the Franciscan order with the doctrinal apparatus for what became, over the next five centuries, the dominant Latin position, fixed at last as defined dogma by Pius IX in 1854. Scotus also held, against the still-strong Augustinian-Thomist tradition of intellectualism, that the will is the higher faculty of the rational soul and that beatitude consists chiefly in an act of love rather than in an act of knowledge — a position which subtly reshaped Franciscan spirituality and which exercised long influence on devotional theology.

He was at Paris by 1302 as a bachelor of theology, working towards the doctorate, and was lecturing on the Sentences when the political crisis broke. King Philip IV of France, in his second great quarrel with Pope Boniface VIII, demanded in June 1303 that all the masters and bachelors of theology at the University of Paris sign an appeal calling for the deposition of the pope by a general council. Of the secular and Dominican theologians the great majority signed; of the eighty-six Franciscan friars at the Paris convent only eighty signed, and Scotus was among the six who refused. The refusers were expelled from France within three days. Where exactly Scotus spent the year of his exile is not certain — possibly at Oxford, possibly at Cambridge, possibly at a continental Franciscan house. He returned to Paris in the spring of 1304 after the death of Boniface and was admitted to the doctorate later that year. He held the Franciscan chair of theology at Paris from 1305 to 1307.

In the summer of 1307 he was transferred by his order to the studium generale of the Franciscans at Cologne, a centre comparable to Paris and Oxford in the German lands. He arrived in October, taught for about a year, and died there on 8 November 1308 in his early forties, of an unrecorded cause — the medieval life of a hard-working master was a short one. He was buried in the choir of the Franciscan church at Cologne, and his tomb remained a centre of veneration through the centuries. He was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1993 after seven centuries of unofficial cult.

The legacy of Duns Scotus has had a strange double life. Within the medieval Franciscan order he was the dominant theologian for two and a half centuries; the late medieval distinction of philosophical schools was between Thomism (the Dominican line of Aquinas), Scotism (the Franciscan line), and the via moderna of Ockham and his successors. In Reformation polemic the Scotists were caricatured as the type of useless hair-splitters, and the English word "dunce" — from his name — passed into the language as a term of derision. Modern philosophy rehabilitated him slowly: Charles Sanders Peirce and Martin Heidegger took him seriously; the twentieth-century Catholic philosophical revival, by way of Étienne Gilson and Maurice de Wulf, restored him to first rank; analytic philosophers of religion have returned to his arguments for the existence of God as the most rigorous of the medieval proofs. The Subtle Doctor outlived "dunce" by enough to recover his subtlety.
