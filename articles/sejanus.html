Sejanus

c. 20 BC – AD 31 · praetorian prefect of Tiberius, would-be successor

Lucius Aelius Sejanus, born about 20 BC at Volsinii in Etruria and dead at Rome on 18 October AD 31, was the praetorian prefect of the emperor Tiberius for nearly half of his reign and the second-most-powerful man at Rome from about AD 23 onward. His rise from the equestrian order to the brink of a place in the imperial succession was, in scale and in speed, without precedent in the Roman political class; his fall in a single afternoon at the Senate, on a letter from the emperor at Capri, was the most theatrical political execution of the early principate. The Roman public, by Juvenal's account a generation later, dragged his statues through the streets the same day. He left behind two adolescent children executed in their cells on the order of the Senate, a former wife whose suicide note revealed the conspiracies that had defined his career, and a regime so badly shaken that the treason trials he had begun ran on for the rest of Tiberius's life.

He was the son of Lucius Seius Strabo, an equestrian of Italian provincial background from Volsinii, and was adopted in adolescence into the gens Aelia, a senatorial line, by a maternal relation. His father was the praetorian prefect under Augustus and at Augustus's death in AD 14 was confirmed in the office by Tiberius, who promoted the young Sejanus as a junior colleague of his father in the same year. In AD 15 the elder Strabo was sent as governor to Egypt; Sejanus, then about thirty-five, became sole prefect of the praetorian guard.

He used the post systematically over the next ten years. His first great administrative achievement was the consolidation, in AD 23, of the nine cohorts of the praetorian guard, which had previously been quartered in lodging-houses scattered through Rome, into a single fortified barracks built on the Viminal at the edge of the city — the Castra Praetoria — which would remain the headquarters of the guard for the next three centuries. The political effect was immediate. The praetorian prefect now commanded a concentrated military force of about nine thousand men in the city of Rome itself. Sejanus, by his prefecture, was the only man in Italy with troops under arms. Tiberius's letters of the period speak of him as "the partner of my labors"; the emperor erected statues to him in public places at Rome, which was the closest a contemporary equestrian came to imperial honors.

He had married Apicata, a daughter of an obscure Roman house, around AD 15; they had three children. About AD 20 he began a relationship with Livilla, the granddaughter of Livia and Mark Antony, sister of Germanicus and the wife of Tiberius's son Drusus the Younger. The relationship was, by Tacitus's account, conducted through the household physician Eudemus and the slave Lygdus; in AD 23 the three of them — Sejanus, Livilla, Eudemus — administered the poison that killed Drusus the Younger over a period of weeks. Tiberius did not learn of the cause of death until eight years later. Drusus's death removed Tiberius's natural heir and opened the succession to the sons of Germanicus and Agrippina the Elder, with whom Sejanus, as Tacitus puts it, was already planning his second campaign.

From AD 25 Sejanus moved to break the Agrippina party. He cultivated Tiberius's distrust of Germanicus's widow and her sons, used the network of informers attached to the guard to surveil her household, and engineered, over the next five years, the destruction of Agrippina the Elder herself, her eldest son Nero Julius Caesar — exiled to Pontia and dead by AD 31, possibly by forced suicide — and her second son Drusus Caesar, imprisoned in a cellar of the Palatine and starved to death there in AD 33. The only one of Agrippina's children Sejanus did not move against, perhaps because he was the youngest and seemed politically negligible, was the boy Gaius — the future Caligula — who survived his mother and brothers to inherit the empire.

In AD 26 Tiberius withdrew from Rome to Capri. Sejanus, who had been with him at a dinner at the cave of Sperlonga earlier that year when the roof collapsed and had thrown himself over the emperor to shield him from the falling stones, was the effective administrator of Rome for the next five years. He filtered every approach to the emperor through his own correspondence; he placed his clients in the consulships and on the bench of the Senate; he was given, in AD 31, the consulship as colleague to Tiberius himself — the first equestrian colleague of an emperor in the office. He requested permission to marry Livilla, by then a widow of eight years' standing. Tiberius politely deferred the request, with a letter so carefully ambiguous that historians still read it for sense. Sejanus then requested, and was granted, an alternative betrothal to Livilla's daughter Julia, a step that placed him at the threshold of the imperial family by a side door.

In the autumn of AD 31 Antonia the Younger, alarmed by the marriage negotiations and by signals from her household freedman Pallas that the next step would be a move against her grandson Caligula, sent a long private letter to Tiberius at Capri laying out the entire history — the affair with Livilla, the poisoning of Drusus the Younger, the plans for the succession. Tiberius, on receiving it, summoned the prefect of the watch Macro to Capri, named him the new praetorian prefect, gave him a sealed letter to the Senate, and sent him to Rome on the night of 17 October.

On the morning of 18 October the Senate met at the temple of Apollo on the Palatine. Sejanus arrived expecting, as Macro had told him, that the long letter from the emperor would name him as tribune of the people, the constitutional preparation for the succession. The letter, read aloud, began with general matters, wandered, took back various honors he had been given, and ended with a clear order for his arrest. The Senate, sensing the shift, condemned him in a single vote. He was led from the temple to the Mamertine prison, strangled at the foot of the rock, and his body thrown out for the Roman mob, which dragged it through the city for three days before tipping it into the Tiber. His elder son was executed the same evening; his daughter, too young by Roman law to be put to death as a virgin, was raped by the executioner and then strangled in the same cell.

His ex-wife Apicata, who had survived in obscurity for eight years, on hearing of her children's deaths wrote out a long account of the poisoning of Drusus the Younger and the names of all those involved — Livilla, Eudemus, Lygdus, Sejanus himself — sent it to Tiberius at Capri, and killed herself. The letter named Livilla. Tiberius handed his niece to her own mother Antonia for whatever punishment Antonia might choose; Antonia locked her daughter in a room in her own house and starved her to death.

The trials Sejanus had begun ran on for the rest of Tiberius's reign. The Senate, faced with the question of what to do about the long string of senators who had taken oaths and made dedications and named their children for the dead prefect, settled into a routine of denunciations and arrests that did not end until the emperor himself died at Misenum five and a half years later. The empire's distrust of Tiberius, in the verdict of Tacitus, dated from Sejanus's prefecture; the boy Caligula, whose family Sejanus had destroyed and whose own survival was an accident of imperial inattention, would, on his accession in AD 37, be welcomed by the Roman crowds as the long-awaited son of Germanicus — the alternative house that Sejanus had failed to break.

The road continues to Drusus the Younger.
