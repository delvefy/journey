Catherine of Aragon

1485 – 1536, Infanta of Spain, first Queen Consort of Henry VIII, the discarded wife whose refusal precipitated the English Reformation

Catherine, in Spanish Catalina, was born at Alcalá de Henares on 16 December 1485, the youngest surviving child of Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile. She grew up in a court still on campaign — she was seven when Granada fell and her parents made their triumphal entry into the conquered Alhambra in January 1492 — and was educated to a higher standard than was usual for the daughters even of great houses. She read Latin fluently, knew the classical poets and the church fathers, was trained in canon law and dynastic history, and was instructed by her mother in the practical business of government. By treaty signed in 1489, when she was three years old, she was betrothed to Arthur, the elder son of Henry VII of England. The match was the keystone of an Anglo-Spanish alliance against France.

She sailed for England in the summer of 1501 and was married to Arthur, Prince of Wales, on 14 November in Old St Paul's. The young couple were sent to Ludlow on the Welsh Marches, where Arthur was nominally to learn government. In April 1502, after less than five months of marriage, both fell ill of the sweating sickness or some related epidemic; Arthur died on 2 April. Whether the marriage had been consummated — the question that would later remake European Christendom — Catherine swore until her death it had not. She was sixteen years old, a widow in a country whose language she did not yet speak well, with no clear position.

The seven years that followed were the worst of her life. Henry VII, having received only half of her dowry, refused to send her home; her father refused to send the rest of the dowry until she was provided for. She was passed between Durham House and Croydon, then for long periods kept at the Bishop of Salisbury's house, in something close to genteel poverty, dismissing servants she could not pay, pawning her plate. In 1503 she was betrothed to her brother-in-law Henry, then twelve years old, on the strength of a papal dispensation granted by Julius II in 1504 explicitly overriding the Levitical impediment of affinity. The boy was made to repudiate the betrothal at fourteen on his father's instructions, then to embrace it again on his accession.

On 11 June 1509, seven weeks after his father's death, Henry VIII married Catherine privately at Greenwich, and on 24 June they were crowned together at Westminster. The first years of their marriage were the high tide of her happiness. She was twenty-three, beautiful, pious, formidably educated — Erasmus, who praised few queens, called her a model of female learning — and visibly devoted to her young husband. She bore him a daughter in January 1510 who lived only weeks, a son on New Year's Day 1511 who died in February, several other children stillborn or short-lived, and in February 1516 the princess Mary, the only child who survived to adulthood.

In the summer of 1513, while Henry was campaigning in France, Catherine was appointed Governor of the Realm and Captain General of the king's forces. When James IV of Scotland invaded the north in support of the French, she rode in armour to Buckingham to rally reinforcements; the army she had organised, under the Earl of Surrey, destroyed the Scots at Flodden on 9 September. James IV was killed. She sent the bloodied surcoat south to Henry as a trophy. It was her one moment as effective regent of England, and her contemporaries did not forget it.

By the mid-1520s the failure of male issue had become a public problem and the king's affections had wandered. Henry began in 1525 to set up his bastard son Henry Fitzroy with royal honours; by 1526 he was pursuing Anne Boleyn. In 1527 he asked Cardinal Wolsey to obtain an annulment from Rome, on the ground that his marriage to his brother's widow had been invalid ab initio and that the dispensation of Julius II had not had the power to make it lawful. Catherine refused to cooperate. She refused to enter a convent, which would have spared everyone trouble; she refused to admit that her first marriage had been consummated; she refused, with extraordinary tenacity, to give up the title of queen. At the legatine court convened by Cardinal Campeggio at Blackfriars in June 1529 she rose, crossed the hall, knelt at her husband's feet, and addressed him directly, asking what she had done to deserve repudiation and reminding him that she had come to him a true maid without touch of man. She then walked out and refused to recognise the court's authority. The legation was eventually adjourned to Rome and never reconvened.

Her nephew was the Emperor Charles V, who in May 1527 had sacked Rome and now held Pope Clement VII in something close to captivity. The pope could not in practice grant Henry's annulment without breaking with the emperor. Henry, denied his answer from Rome, in the end made his own answer. By the Act of Supremacy of 1534 he became Supreme Head of the Church in England, and Archbishop Cranmer pronounced his marriage to Catherine void. She was demoted from queen to "Princess Dowager of Wales" (Arthur's widow), separated from her daughter Mary, whom she would never see again, and moved successively to The More, Buckden, and finally to Kimbolton Castle in Huntingdonshire.

She refused to recognise any of it. She continued to sign herself Catherine the Queen, to keep a small court of Spanish servants, to hear Mass according to the Roman use, to correspond with the imperial ambassador Eustace Chapuys, and to write to her daughter exhorting her to obedience to God before obedience to the king. Her health collapsed in the autumn of 1535. She died at Kimbolton on 7 January 1536, possibly of a cancer of the heart found at the autopsy, possibly of poison as rumour had it, certainly of long sorrow. She was buried in Peterborough Cathedral. Henry, hearing the news, dressed in yellow and held a joust; Anne Boleyn would be dead within five months.

Her reputation in England survived the Reformation that her case had triggered. The common people had always loved her, and the chroniclers, even the Protestant ones, treated her with a respect they rarely accorded her husband. Her daughter Mary brought England briefly back to Rome in the next reign. To Spain she remained an exemplary Infanta and to her great-nephew Philip II a symbol of an old wrong. In the longer history she stands as the wife whose refusal to acquiesce in her own destruction broke the medieval English church and made the modern English nation possible.
