Margaret of Anjou

1430 – 1482, Queen consort of Henry VI, the Lancastrian she-wolf, last campaigner of her line

Margaret was born on 23 March 1430 at Pont-à-Mousson in Lorraine, the second daughter of René of Anjou, titular King of Naples, Jerusalem, and Sicily, and Isabella, Duchess of Lorraine in her own right. The Angevin house in which she grew up was richer in claims than in revenues: her father held a string of nominal kingdoms in southern Italy and the Mediterranean that he was rarely able to make good, and a working duchy of Anjou whose principal value lay in its proximity to the French crown. Her uncle on her mother's side was Charles VII of France, then in the long second half of the Hundred Years' War driving the English out of Normandy and Guyenne. Her education in Lorraine and Anjou was that of a French princess of the second rank, in the cultivated half-Italian court her father kept at Angers and Saumur.

Her marriage was negotiated as part of the truce of Tours in May 1444, by which the English government of Henry VI, exhausted by the war, sought a breathing space from France. The bride brought no dowry; her family ceded the counties of Anjou and Maine to her uncle Charles VII as part of the settlement, a concession that was kept secret from the English political nation and contributed materially, when it became known, to the disasters of the following decade. Margaret was fourteen when she crossed the Channel in the spring of 1445; she was married to the twenty-three-year-old Henry VI at Titchfield Abbey on 22 April and crowned at Westminster on 30 May.

Henry VI was a gentle, otherworldly, pious young man without the temper of a fifteenth-century king. The court that received Margaret in 1445 was dominated by William de la Pole, Duke of Suffolk, who had brokered the marriage, and by Edmund Beaufort, Duke of Somerset, who would shortly take command of what was left of Lancastrian France. Margaret attached herself to Suffolk's party from the beginning; when Suffolk was impeached in 1450 over the surrender of Maine and the loss of Normandy and was murdered on his way into exile, she transferred her support to Somerset, who returned from France a hated man. The opposition coalesced around Richard, Duke of York, the king's nearest adult kinsman in the male line and a man passed over for the regency to which his birth entitled him.

In August 1453 Henry VI suffered a mental collapse that lasted for eighteen months and from which he never wholly recovered. In October Margaret bore the only child of the marriage, a son named Edward of Lancaster, whose paternity, given the king's condition, was at once a subject of rumour. The protectorship in the king's incapacity went to York; on Henry's partial recovery in early 1455 it was withdrawn; in May at the first battle of St Albans Yorkist troops under Warwick killed Somerset and broke the king's party in the streets. From that moment the queen, not the king, was the political head of the Lancastrian cause. She moved the court out of London, gathered her party in the Midlands, and built around her son's claim a faction whose hardness shocked the gentry.

The decisive turn came at Wakefield on 30 December 1460, where a Lancastrian army loyal to her killed the duke of York and his second son Edmund of Rutland. The duke's head, in a paper crown, was set on Micklegate Bar at York at her order. Two months later, at the second battle of St Albans on 17 February 1461, her army defeated Warwick and recovered the captive Henry VI. But she failed to follow up the victory by entering London, which closed its gates to her northerners; Edward of York, the eighteen-year-old son of the dead duke, marched into the city, was acclaimed king on 4 March as Edward IV, and destroyed her army at Towton on Palm Sunday, 29 March, in driving snow.

The remainder of her life was the war's afterlife. She fled with her son and her household to Scotland, then to France, where she was kept on a small pension by Louis XI. She raised three small expeditions in the 1460s, all of them defeated; she lived for nearly nine years on the kindness of her relations and on the small revenues of Lorraine. The political opening, when it came, was the quarrel of Edward IV with Warwick the Kingmaker. In the summer of 1470 at Louis XI's court she received Warwick, the man who had killed her allies and stripped her crown, and, after a long and humiliating reconciliation, agreed to ally with him on the condition that her son marry his younger daughter Anne Neville. The marriage was solemnised at Angers on 13 December 1470. Warwick crossed to England, drove Edward out, restored Henry VI; Margaret waited in France through the winter for the situation to settle.

She landed at Weymouth on 14 April 1471, the day Warwick was killed at Barnet, and marched west to gather strength among the Lancastrian gentry of the south-west. At Tewkesbury on 4 May her army was destroyed. Her seventeen-year-old son Edward, on whose claim her whole career had turned, was killed in the battle or in its immediate aftermath. She was taken prisoner. Henry VI, returned to the Tower of London, was murdered there on the night of 21 May. Within three weeks every man and cause to which she had been attached for thirty years was dead.

She lived for eleven more years as a prisoner of Edward IV, lodged first in the Tower, then more loosely at Wallingford, then released in 1475 under the terms of the Treaty of Picquigny to her cousin Louis XI for a ransom of fifty thousand crowns. He extracted from her in return a formal renunciation of all her claims in the Angevin inheritance. She passed her last years at the Château de Souzay near Saumur on a small pension from the French crown, dependent and impoverished. She died on 25 August 1482, aged fifty-two, and was buried in Angers Cathedral beside her parents; her tomb was destroyed in the Revolution.

Margaret of Anjou was the most active queen consort in the political history of medieval England. Shakespeare's "she-wolf of France" is the long shadow of a real character: a foreign-born princess in a kingdom whose government, in the absence of her husband's capacity, she undertook to direct, in a society that had no constitutional vocabulary for what she was doing. Her party lost, and the historians who wrote the first long accounts of her career wrote them for her enemies. The Wars of the Roses, when they ended in 1485 with Henry Tudor's marriage to Edward IV's daughter, joined the two roses through her enemies' line, not hers.

The road continues to Henry VI of England.
