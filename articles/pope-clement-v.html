Pope Clement V

c. 1264 – 1314, Bertrand de Got, the pope who moved the curia to Avignon and dissolved the Templars

Bertrand de Got was born about 1264 at Villandraut in the diocese of Bordeaux, the son of a minor Gascon noble family. He studied the arts at Orléans and the civil and canon law at Bologna, and rose through the church on the influence of his uncle Bertrand de Got the elder, archbishop of Lyon. He was canon of Bordeaux and Tours, then chaplain to Boniface VIII, bishop of Saint-Bertrand-de-Comminges in 1295, and in 1299 archbishop of Bordeaux — the principal ecclesiastical office in Gascony, which was then the duchy held in fief from the French crown by Edward I of England. The archbishop of Bordeaux had to be acceptable to both kings; Bertrand contrived to be so for six years.

The conclave that elected him was the longest of the century to that date. Benedict XI, the Dominican successor to Boniface VIII, died at Perugia on 7 July 1304 after a pontificate of eight months. The cardinals, divided between the supporters of the late Boniface and the Colonna and French parties, deadlocked for almost a year. The compromise emerged at last on 5 June 1305 in the person of Bertrand de Got, who was not a cardinal, who was a Frenchman acceptable to Philip IV, and who was a former familiar of Boniface VIII acceptable to the Italians. He took the name Clement V and refused to come to Rome for the coronation: he summoned the cardinals to Lyon, where on 14 November 1305 he was crowned in the presence of King Philip IV. During the coronation procession through the city a wall collapsed on the cavalcade, killing the duke of Brittany and unhorsing the king and the pope; the pope's tiara fell off and a great carbuncle was lost. The Italians took it as an omen.

Clement never set foot in Rome as pope. He kept the curia in France — first in Bordeaux, then in Poitiers from 1307, then from March 1309 at Avignon, a town belonging to the kings of Naples and lying on the imperial bank of the Rhône, technically outside French sovereignty but a few miles from the kingdom. The choice was provisional; it became the seat of the papacy for the next seventy years. The cardinals he created were almost all French, many of them his own kin — the nepotism of the early Avignon papacy was the most extreme of any age — and the administrative weight of the papal court, with its financial chamber, its judicial tribunals, and its enormous patronage network, settled into the new city around the cardinal-palaces built on its slopes.

The Gascon pope owed his election to Philip IV and paid the debt at length. The first instalment was the rehabilitation of the king and of Guillaume de Nogaret for the attack on Boniface VIII at Anagni; the second was the absolution of all those involved in the violence; the third, demanded by Philip and resisted for years by Clement, was a formal posthumous trial of Boniface VIII himself on charges of heresy, sodomy, and simony. The trial dragged through 1310 and 1311 with the testimony of witnesses procured by Nogaret; Clement, who could not openly condemn his predecessor without destroying the legitimacy of his own succession, played for time and finally extinguished the proceedings at the Council of Vienne in 1311–1312 by an artful compromise: the trial was discontinued, Boniface's bulls against Philip were annulled, and the king publicly accepted that no heresy had been proved. In substance Philip had won.

The fourth and largest instalment was the suppression of the Order of the Temple. On Friday 13 October 1307 Philip's officers arrested every Templar in France in a coordinated operation kept secret from the curia. Clement protested at the violation of papal jurisdiction; he suspended the inquisitorial proceedings against the order in February 1308, then, after pressure and the production of confessions extracted under torture, agreed in November 1308 to a parallel ecclesiastical inquiry. He interrogated seventy-two Templars at Poitiers in mid-1308 and was apparently shaken by their testimony. The Council of Vienne, summoned in 1310 and meeting from October 1311, considered the question for seven months and showed no enthusiasm for outright condemnation. Clement therefore acted by his own apostolic authority and not by conciliar decree: by the bull Vox in Excelso of 22 March 1312 he suppressed the order, not as condemned but as so defamed as to be incapable of recovery, and by Ad Providam of 2 May 1312 he transferred its property to the Hospitallers of Saint John — though Philip kept most of the cash and the Iberian kings absorbed the local commanderies into new orders of their own. The Grand Master Jacques de Molay and the senior Templar officers, reserved to papal judgment, languished for two more years; when in March 1314 they publicly retracted their confessions, Philip seized them as relapsed heretics and burned them on the Île aux Juifs in the Seine without further appeal to the pope.

Outside the great affair of the Templars, Clement's pontificate had its compensating successes. He proclaimed and convoked the Council of Vienne, the fifteenth ecumenical council, which besides the Templar business legislated against the Spiritual Franciscans, condemned the Beguine and Beghard heresies, and decreed the foundation of chairs of Hebrew, Arabic, Greek, and Chaldean at Paris, Oxford, Bologna, Salamanca, and the papal court — the legal foundation of academic Orientalism in Europe. He compiled the Constitutiones Clementinae, promulgated by his successor in 1317, the fourth great collection of medieval canon law. He crowned, in absentia at the Lateran by his legate in June 1312, the emperor-elect Henry VII of Luxembourg, the last serious attempt to revive imperial authority in Italy; Henry died a year later on the road south to Naples, and the dream of a universal empire died with him.

Clement's health was wretched throughout the pontificate, an intestinal cancer that his contemporaries called a flux. He set out in the spring of 1314 to return to Gascony to die at home, but got only as far as Roquemaure on the Rhône, where on the night of 20 April he died, less than six weeks after the burning of Jacques de Molay and seven months before Philip IV. The legend that de Molay from the pyre had summoned both pope and king to God's judgment within the year arose immediately, and Dante — who hated Clement as he hated Boniface, and put him too in the pit of the simoniacs in advance, while he still lived — gave it imaginative permanence.

Clement's body, conveyed homewards from Roquemaure, lay in an undefended church at Uzeste in Gascony, where in 1577 a band of Huguenot soldiers burned it. His reputation, never high in his lifetime, has remained that of a weak and venal pope who broke the universal papacy to the convenience of the French crown and presided over an act of judicial murder against the Templars; modern historians have been gentler about his motives but not about his results. The Avignon papacy, which he had improvised as a temporary expedient on the road home to Gascony, outlived him by sixty-three years and broke into the Schism six years after that. The road from Rome to Avignon, and from Avignon to Constance, ran through his pontificate.

The road continues to Jacques de Molay.
