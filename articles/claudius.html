Claudius

10 BC – AD 54 · fourth emperor of Rome

Tiberius Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus, born at Lugdunum in Gaul on 1 August 10 BC and dead at Rome on 13 October AD 54, was the fourth emperor of Rome and the first born outside Italy. He was the younger son of Drusus the Elder and Antonia the Younger; he was the brother of Germanicus, the uncle of Caligula, the great-uncle and stepfather and predecessor of Nero. He was sixty-four when he died, of a poisoned plate of mushrooms administered, by the unanimous account of every ancient source, by his last wife and niece Agrippina the Younger, who succeeded in seating her own son Nero on the throne in place of Claudius's natural son Britannicus and thus closed the dynastic line that her uncle had restored. He had reigned for thirteen years. He had become emperor only by an accident more improbable than the principate had yet produced.

He was born in early August of 10 BC at the new colony of Lugdunum on the confluence of the Rhone and the Saone, where his father Drusus the Elder was wintering between the campaigns of his German command. His mother Antonia, then twenty-six, had travelled out with the household. His elder brother Germanicus was a small child at the time and his sister Livilla a toddler. He was a year old when his father was thrown from a horse east of the Rhine in 9 BC and died of his injuries; he was raised, like his brother and sister, in Antonia's house at Rome under the eye of his grandmother Livia and the protection of his great-uncle Augustus.

He was, from childhood, in poor health. The ancient sources record a stammer, an unsteady walk, a noisy laugh, a tendency to slobber, a weak digestion, and frequent illnesses; modern medical historians have variously diagnosed cerebral palsy, polio, or some other early childhood neurological insult. Augustus, who was not unkind, wrote letters to Livia about the boy debating whether he could be brought into public functions at all without exposing the imperial family to embarrassment; the surviving letters quoted by Suetonius are remarkable for the careful affection with which Augustus tried to find some role for him. The family solution was to keep Claudius out of public life. He was given no military commands, no civil magistracies of the first rank, no place in the cursus honorum. He was permitted to give himself instead to the scholarly antiquarianism that he loved.

He spent his youth and middle age — from his teens to his late forties — at his books. He wrote histories: a twenty-book Etruscan history, an eight-book Carthaginian history, a forty-three-book Roman history covering the period from the death of Caesar onward, of which his patron and teacher Livy may have been the model, and an autobiography. None of these works survives. He was an Etruscan-language scholar of probably the last generation that could still read inscriptions in that language with any accuracy. He was a public speaker of more competence than his family expected; he gave the funeral oration for his brother Germanicus in AD 19 and for his grandmother Livia in AD 29. He was married twice in this earlier period, first to Plautia Urgulanilla, with whom he had two children — one died as a baby of choking on a pear, the other was repudiated as illegitimate when the marriage broke down — and then to Aelia Paetina, with whom he had a daughter, Claudia Antonia. Neither marriage figures much in the political record.

The accident that made him emperor took place on the morning of 24 January AD 41. The praetorians, who had just murdered his nephew the emperor Caligula in a colonnade beneath the Palatine palace, were looting the imperial apartments when, by the famous and possibly apocryphal account of Suetonius and Josephus, one of them discovered Claudius hiding behind a curtain. The man dragged him out by the feet, recognized him, and saluted him as emperor on the spot. The other guardsmen, looking for a way to preserve their own positions and the prerogatives of the corps after the assassination of an emperor, took up the idea. They carried Claudius to the praetorian camp and acclaimed him while the Senate, debating constitutional alternatives in the Capitol, was overtaken by events. Claudius was fifty.

His reign was a far more capable administration than the family had ever predicted. He reorganized the civil service of the empire by creating powerful freedman secretariats — the ab epistulis under Narcissus, the a rationibus under Pallas, the a libellis under Callistus — which became the standing professional bureaucracy of the imperial center. The loss of senatorial dignity in this development was the source of much of the senatorial historians' hostility to him. He extended Roman citizenship aggressively to provincial elites, particularly the Gauls; the long speech in the Senate of AD 48, preserved in part in the surviving bronze Lyon Tablet, in which he argued for the admission of leading Gauls to the Senate, is one of the few authentic samples of Claudian Latin we possess and is a remarkable piece of political reasoning. He undertook the conquest of Britain in AD 43, sending his general Aulus Plautius with four legions to take the south of the island, and going himself, in a brief sixteen-day visit, to be present when the British king Caratacus's brother was brought in and to enter Camulodunum on an elephant. He built the great new harbor at Ostia, the Claudian aqueducts that brought water to Rome from the Anio valley, and the artificial drainage of the Fucine Lake in central Italy. He annexed Mauretania, Thrace, Lycia, Pamphylia, and Judaea — the last under his old friend Herod Agrippa I, whom he had known in Antonia's household as a boy and who died in AD 44.

His private life was a disaster. His third wife Valeria Messalina, married to him in AD 38 or 39 when she was very young, gave him his only surviving son Britannicus in February AD 41 and a daughter Octavia the year before; she conducted in his last years what was either, in the senatorial sources, the most spectacular and reckless sexual scandal of the imperial age, or in modern historical readings the desperate political maneuvering of a woman who saw her position eroded by the powerful freedmen of the household. In the autumn of AD 48, while Claudius was at Ostia on the harbor works, she went through a public ceremony of marriage in Rome with the senator Gaius Silius. Narcissus, by Tacitus's account, intercepted Claudius on his return from Ostia, informed him of the marriage, and arranged for both Messalina and Silius to be killed in the gardens of Lucullus before Claudius could change his mind. Claudius was at supper when news of his wife's death reached him; he asked for more wine and went on eating.

He married, the following year, his niece Agrippina the Younger, the daughter of his brother Germanicus, who had been a widow since AD 47 and the mother of an eleven-year-old son by her first marriage to Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus — the boy Nero. Agrippina obtained from Claudius the formal adoption of Nero as his son in AD 50 and the marriage of Nero to her own daughter Octavia in AD 53; she progressively displaced Britannicus, who was three years younger and constitutionally Claudius's heir, from the political account. On the evening of 12 October AD 54, by Tacitus's and Suetonius's account, Claudius was poisoned at supper by a plate of mushrooms prepared by his food-taster Halotus, in which the poisoner Locusta had introduced a slow toxin; he died on the morning of 13 October. Nero, sixteen, was acclaimed emperor by the praetorians the same day. Britannicus, fourteen, was poisoned at a dinner by Nero himself the following spring.

The Senate, somewhat to its own astonishment, deified Claudius. He was the first emperor since Augustus to be made a god by formal decree. The court satirist Seneca, who had returned from exile under Claudius's reign and had no reason to be grateful to him, wrote on the deification the savage prose-and-verse parody called the Apocolocyntosis, in which Claudius is pumpkinified rather than deified, fails to gain admission to Olympus, and is sent to the underworld to take dictation at the trial of his own freedmen. The piece is the cruellest contemporary verdict on the reign and one of the most enjoyable Latin works of its century. The judgment of modern scholarship has been much more favorable. Claudius was, despite the family verdict and the senatorial hostility, the most administratively capable emperor between Augustus and Vespasian, the architect of the imperial bureaucracy, and the man who closed the long Augustan project of British annexation and opened the Roman center to the provinces in a way that decisively shaped the next three centuries of the empire.
