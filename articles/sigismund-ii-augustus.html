Sigismund II Augustus

1520 – 1572, last Jagiellonian King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania

Sigismund II Augustus was the only one of the children of Sigismund I the Old and Bona Sforza to reach adulthood, and the weight of an exhausted dynasty fell on him in his cradle. He was born on 1 August 1520 at Kraków, in the long, glittering reign of his father; his mother, the Milanese princess Bona, decided very early that the boy would not be left to chance. She had him crowned as junior king alongside his father in 1530, when he was nine, on the principle of vivente rege — election in the lifetime of the reigning monarch — that the Polish nobility regarded with suspicion and would never tolerate again. She had him sent to Lithuania for a separate apprenticeship as grand duke. She arranged his marriage. She managed his money. She gathered his court. In his political education she imprinted on him an Italian conception of statecraft that he never quite shed and never quite acted on.

His first wife, Elizabeth of Habsburg, was the daughter of Ferdinand I and was joined to him in 1543 in a treaty marriage that pleased the Habsburg connection his mother despised. She was an epileptic, miserable in the Lithuanian winters, ignored by the husband she had been told would adore her, and dead within two years. The court at Vilnius received instead an attractive young Lithuanian widow, Barbara Radziwiłł, of the most powerful Calvinist magnate family in the grand duchy. He fell in love with her. He married her in secret, perhaps in 1547. He acknowledged the marriage publicly in 1548, immediately after his father's death and his accession to sole rule. The Polish nobility responded with rage that lasted for years; his own mother conspired against the queen consort; the bishops refused to crown her until 1550. Barbara died, almost certainly of cervical cancer, in May 1551, in great pain. Sigismund accompanied the funeral cortège from Kraków to Vilnius on foot. Bona, suspected of poisoning her daughter-in-law, retired to her Italian estates in 1556, taking with her a great fortune in cash and jewels, and died in Bari the next year, possibly poisoned in turn.

His third marriage in 1553 to Catherine of Habsburg, Elizabeth's sister, was loveless, childless, and, after a few years of attempted reconciliation, separated. The dynasty's failure to perpetuate itself became the unstated political fact of the reign.

Around it Sigismund built the legal architecture of what came after. The Livonian Order, dissolving under the pressure of the Lutheran Reformation and Russian invasion, transferred its last surviving polities into Polish-Lithuanian hands in 1561, when its last master Gotthard Kettler took Courland and Semigallia from him as a hereditary duchy and submitted to Polish suzerainty. Livonia proper became a joint condominium of the Commonwealth, and the long Livonian War with Ivan IV was now Sigismund's war as much as Ivan's. He could not finish it militarily — the Commonwealth lacked a standing army — but he held its diplomatic ground.

The greater achievement of his reign was institutional. The Union of Lublin, signed on 1 July 1569, took the personal union of Poland and Lithuania that the Jagiellons had embodied since the late fourteenth century and turned it into a single Commonwealth with a single elected king, a common Sejm, a shared foreign policy, and a single currency, while leaving Lithuania its own laws, treasury, army, and central offices. The Lithuanian magnates, who feared incorporation, were brought to the table partly by the king's own steady pressure and partly by the threat of land annexations to Poland in the Ruthenian provinces of Volhynia and Kiev, which Sigismund detached from Lithuania and joined to the Crown by royal decree as the price of agreement. It was the most consequential constitutional act of any Polish reign. The Commonwealth that came out of it was the largest contiguous state in continental Europe and would survive in something like that form until the late eighteenth century.

In religion he was a Catholic of decided personal piety and unusually broad tolerance. The decades of his reign were the years when Lithuanian and Polish nobles in great numbers turned Protestant: Calvinists in Lithuania, more diverse antitrinitarians and Unitarians in Poland, with Lutheran towns scattered across the western provinces. Sigismund did not persecute. He treated the religious choice of his nobles as their own, used the bishops as ministers without taking their counsel on the conscience of the laity, refused to bring the Inquisition into the kingdom, and laid the practical groundwork for what would become, in 1573 — the year after his death — the Confederation of Warsaw, Europe's first general statutory guarantee of religious freedom for the nobility. Whether this was conviction or simply the prudence of a king with no heir is debated, but the result was that the Commonwealth in his time was the most religiously plural large polity in Christendom.

His health failed early. He suffered chronic intestinal complaints, attempted cures at Knyszyn and elsewhere, and was rumoured throughout his last decade to be impotent or sterile, the rumour itself a political fact at a court whose succession depended on his progeny. He died on 7 July 1572 at Knyszyn, unmarried again by then — Catherine of Habsburg had been sent home in 1566 and died in 1572 a few months before her husband — and without legitimate issue. The Jagiellonian dynasty, which had ruled in Kraków since 1386 and had at various times worn the crowns of Poland, Lithuania, Bohemia, and Hungary, ended with him.

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth he left behind held its first royal election in 1573 — Henry of Valois, briefly — and a second in 1576: Stephen Báthory of Transylvania, married to Sigismund's sister Anna Jagiellon. The continuity of the Jagiellonian line was over; the elective monarchy that replaced it was the system Sigismund himself had set up. He was buried in the Wawel Cathedral in Kraków, between his parents. The Renaissance court he and his mother had built — Italian musicians, Italian gardens, Italian humanists working in Polish — survived him in fragments and gave the Commonwealth a cultural imprint visible for the next century.
