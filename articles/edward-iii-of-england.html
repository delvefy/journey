Edward III of England

1312 – 1377, fifty-year king of England, victor of Sluys, Crécy, and Poitiers, founder of the Order of the Garter

Edward was born at Windsor Castle on 13 November 1312, the eldest son of Edward II of England and Isabella of France, daughter of Philip IV. His birth was politically necessary for his father, whose intermittent struggles with his magnates — in particular with the Despenser favourites and against them with Thomas of Lancaster — were just then in one of their crises. The boy was created Earl of Chester in his cradle and brought up in the conventional schooling of a Plantagenet heir, in Latin, in French, in arms, and in the hunting field, by tutors whose names — Richard de Bury, later bishop of Durham, the bibliophile, was one — suggest the seriousness of the cultivation.

The crisis of his youth was his mother's revolution. In 1325 Isabella had been sent to Paris to negotiate a settlement of the Anglo-French dispute over Gascony; she did not return, and from late 1325 lived openly at the French court with Roger Mortimer, a Marcher lord who had escaped from imprisonment in the Tower the year before. In September 1326 the two of them, with the young Edward in their train, landed in Suffolk at the head of a small Hainaulter force; the regime of his father and the Despensers collapsed within weeks; the king was deposed in January 1327 by an unprecedented act of parliament and the fourteen-year-old prince was crowned at Westminster on 1 February. His father was murdered at Berkeley Castle in September. The new king's first three years were the regency of his mother and Mortimer.

In October 1330, seventeen years old, he carried out his own coup. With a small group of friends — William Montagu, the future Earl of Salisbury, was the principal — he entered Nottingham Castle by a secret passage, arrested Mortimer in his bedchamber, and took the regime in hand. Mortimer was hanged at Tyburn in November; Isabella was retired in dignified confinement, in which she lived another twenty-eight years. From that moment Edward governed in his own right, and would do so for another forty-seven years.

His early reign was made by Scotland. In July 1333 he destroyed a Scottish army under Sir Archibald Douglas at Halidon Hill outside Berwick-upon-Tweed in the first English archery victory of the century, the model for the great French battles to come; he restored Edward Balliol to the Scottish throne as his client; he kept Scotland off balance for thirty years, capturing King David II at Neville's Cross in 1346 and holding him for eleven years until ransom. The Scottish war prepared his armies, his commanders, and his fiscal machinery for the larger war he meant to fight.

The French war — the Hundred Years' War, as nineteenth-century historians would call it — opened in 1337 with his formal claim to the French throne by right of his mother Isabella, the only surviving child of Philip IV, against his cousin Philip VI of the cadet Valois line. The claim was a diplomatic instrument as much as a personal one: it absolved his Flemish allies, whose wool trade depended on English supply, from the imputation of treason against their lawful king. The first decade of the war was indecisive and ruinously expensive; the English crown went bankrupt in 1340, taking with it the Florentine banking houses of Bardi and Peruzzi. The pattern broke in June of that year at Sluys, when the English fleet destroyed the assembled French invasion fleet in the harbour; the war thereafter was fought in France.

The six years from 1346 to 1356 were the high summer of his reign. At Crécy in Picardy on 26 August 1346 the English longbowmen of the king's army destroyed a French chivalric host three times their size; among the dead was John, the blind King of Bohemia, whose three feathers were taken by Edward's son, the Black Prince, as the badge of the Prince of Wales. Calais fell to him in August 1347 after a year-long siege; he kept it as an English town for two hundred years. In 1356 his son, in independent command, captured the French king John II at Poitiers and brought him back to live in luxurious captivity in England. The treaty of Brétigny in 1360, the high-water mark of the medieval English position in France, gave Edward a third of France in full sovereignty in return for renouncing the French throne — a renunciation that, in the event, was never formally exchanged, allowing later kings to revive the claim.

The Order of the Garter, founded in 1348 in association with the rebuilt St George's Chapel at Windsor, was the institutional expression of the king's chivalric self-image — the most famous and most enduring of medieval lay orders, surviving with little alteration to the present day. The court he kept — mobile, expensive, attended by Geoffrey Chaucer and a generation of poets and chroniclers Jean Froissart among them — was the centre of the fashionable culture of Anglo-French chivalry, the wide European stylistic phenomenon to which his armies, his tournaments, and his diplomacy all belonged.

The second half of his reign was a slow declension. The plague of 1348 to 1349 carried off a third of his subjects; the Statute of Labourers attempted, with only partial success, to pin wages at pre-plague levels. The war with France resumed in 1369; the English position collapsed rapidly in the early 1370s as Charles V of France and Bertrand du Guesclin reconquered most of what had been ceded at Brétigny, while the English crown, exhausted of money, could no longer mount the great expeditions of the king's prime. The Black Prince, his eldest son and the chosen heir, came home from Aquitaine in 1371 broken by a wasting disease and died in 1376. The king himself, by then in his sixties, drifted into the influence of his mistress Alice Perrers and a clutch of court financiers whom the "Good Parliament" of the same year impeached.

He died at Sheen Palace on 21 June 1377, sixty-four years old, his queen Philippa of Hainault dead seven years before him, his eldest son dead twelve months before him, the dynastic future passing to his ten-year-old grandson Richard II, son of the Black Prince. Of his other sons — Lionel of Antwerp, John of Gaunt, Edmund of Langley, Thomas of Woodstock — the third would found the Lancastrian royal line through his son Henry IV, and the fourth, through his daughter Anne, the Yorkist line. The Wars of the Roses of the following century would be a quarrel among Edward III's grandchildren. He was buried beside Philippa in Westminster Abbey; the gilt effigy on his tomb, with its long beard, is one of the great surviving English royal effigies of the Middle Ages.
