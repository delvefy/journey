Henry VI of England

1421 – 1471, King of England and France, the holy and the helpless, the only English king crowned in both Paris and Westminster

Henry was born on 6 December 1421 at Windsor Castle, the only child of Henry V of England and Catherine of Valois, daughter of Charles VI of France. He was nine months old when his father died of dysentery at Vincennes on 31 August 1422, and a further fifty-one days old when his maternal grandfather Charles VI died at Paris on 21 October. By the terms of the Treaty of Troyes, which his father had imposed on the broken French government three years earlier, the infant succeeded to both crowns: England, by inheritance, from his cradle, and France, by the same treaty, in displacement of his uncle the dauphin Charles. He was the only English king ever crowned in both Paris and Westminster, and the only English king ever to lose both crowns to whose dynasty he had been born.

His minority government was conducted by his uncles. John, Duke of Bedford, the eldest of his father's surviving brothers, governed the Lancastrian half of France from Paris and Rouen, and was, by general consent, the ablest man of the dynasty. Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, the younger, governed England as Protector under the close supervision of a council in which Cardinal Henry Beaufort, the king's great-uncle and the wealthiest churchman in England, was the dominant figure. The boy was educated in piety, in Latin, in French, in some music; his tutor was the soldier-scholar Richard Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick. He was crowned at Westminster on 6 November 1429, eight years old; he was crowned again at Notre-Dame de Paris on 16 December 1431, in a ceremony designed to anchor the dual monarchy and to answer the rival coronation of Charles VII at Reims two years earlier under Joan of Arc.

The French half of his inheritance was lost during his lifetime, and largely against his ineffectual wishes. Bedford died at Rouen on 14 September 1435; within a week Burgundy, the English alliance on which the dual monarchy had been built, defected to Charles VII by the Treaty of Arras. Paris was lost in April 1436. The reconquest of Normandy by Charles VII's reorganised army took ten years, from the resumption of the war in 1449 to the loss of Cherbourg in August 1450 and the loss of Guyenne, the last continental possession, at Castillon in 1453. The king who watched these losses had reached his majority in 1437 and ruled, in principle, in his own right; in practice he was an unworldly, pious, indecisive young man without the temper of a fifteenth-century soldier-king.

His government from 1444 was dominated by William de la Pole, Duke of Suffolk, who negotiated the truce of Tours with France and the king's marriage to the fourteen-year-old Margaret of Anjou, niece of Charles VII, in 1445. The marriage brought no dowry; it ceded, in secret, the counties of Maine and Anjou to the French crown; when the cession became known it broke Suffolk's government. Suffolk was impeached in 1450 and murdered at sea on his way into exile; Jack Cade's rising the same summer signalled how far the country had drifted from confidence in the regime. The opposition coalesced around Richard, Duke of York, the king's nearest adult kinsman in the male line and a man passed over for the regency to which his descent entitled him.

On 30 March 1453 Margaret of Anjou bore a son, Edward of Lancaster, who was the king's heir. On 1 August at Clarendon, the king suffered a sudden and complete mental collapse — almost certainly a form of catatonia, possibly inherited from his maternal grandfather Charles VI — which lasted eighteen months. He could not recognise his wife or his newborn son; he could not speak; he could not feed himself. The kingdom was governed in his name by York as Protector. The king recovered partially at Christmas 1454, dismissed York, and reinstated the Beaufort party. Within six months, at the first battle of St Albans on 22 May 1455, Yorkist troops under Warwick the Kingmaker broke the king's army in the streets and killed Edmund Beaufort, Duke of Somerset. The king himself, wounded in the neck, was found sitting alone in a tanner's shop.

From that moment he was carried through the long crisis of the Wars of the Roses as the figurehead, sometimes of his own party under his wife's direction, sometimes of his enemies'. He was captured and recovered by either side at intervals. He was deposed in March 1461 by Edward IV after the Yorkist victory at Towton; he fled to Scotland and was hidden among loyal northerners for four years; he was captured in Lancashire in 1465 and lodged in the Tower of London. In October 1470, when Warwick the Kingmaker, having quarrelled with Edward IV, restored him to the throne in the brief reign called the readeption, he was brought out of the Tower, dressed in royal robes that no longer fitted him, and paraded through the city; he was, by then, by all accounts, a broken man.

Edward IV recovered the throne by force in the spring of 1471. Warwick was killed at Barnet on Easter morning, 14 April; Henry's seventeen-year-old son Edward of Lancaster was killed at Tewkesbury on 4 May; Margaret of Anjou was taken prisoner. On the night of 21 May 1471 Henry VI died in the Tower of London, almost certainly by murder. The Yorkist proclamation announced he had died "of pure displeasure and melancholy"; the body was exhibited at St Paul's, by tradition leaking fresh blood. He was buried at Chertsey, then moved by Richard III to St George's Chapel at Windsor, where the tomb survives.

He left no legitimate heir; the Lancastrian male line was extinct with him. The claim passed to the Beaufort descendants of John of Gaunt — in particular to Margaret Beaufort and her son Henry Tudor, the future Henry VII — from whose victory at Bosworth in 1485 the dynastic resolution would eventually come. Henry himself was, in the eyes of his English subjects, a saint, or near to one: pilgrims gathered at his tomb in the 1480s; Henry VII opened a formal canonisation suit at Rome; if not for the breach with Rome under Henry VIII he would in all probability have been canonised in the sixteenth century. He had founded Eton College in 1440 and King's College, Cambridge, in 1441, the two great educational foundations of the late medieval English crown, and these institutions, his only enduring works, still observe his anniversary. He was a gentle, scholarly, fatally indecisive man on whom a dynasty had been hung and broken.
