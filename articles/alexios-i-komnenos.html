Alexios I Komnenos

1057 – 1118, Byzantine emperor, founder of the Komnenian restoration

Alexios was born in 1057, the third surviving son of the kouropalates John Komnenos and of Anna Dalassene, in the year his uncle Isaac I Komnenos briefly held the throne (1057–1059). The Komnenoi were a great Anatolian military family of Paphlagonian origin, raised to imperial dignity by Isaac and pushed back into the second rank by the Doukai when Isaac abdicated in favour of his treasurer Constantine X Doukas. Alexios was therefore born into a clan with a recent grievance and an ambitious mother — Anna Dalassene, the implacable matriarch of the family, schooled her three sons in the politics of the capital and the army with a single object, the recovery of the throne. The empire into which Alexios entered service in his teens was disintegrating. The defeat at Manzikert in August 1071 had broken the field army and opened the Anatolian plateau to the Seljuks; the navy decayed; the central administration was strangled by the inflation of debased coinage; one usurpation followed another at Constantinople through the 1070s.

The young Alexios distinguished himself as a soldier in this chaos. Under Michael VII Doukas he ran down and captured the Norman mercenary Roussel de Bailleul, who had set up an independent lordship in the Armeniac theme, in 1075. Under Nikephoros III Botaneiates he was promoted Domestic of the Schools, the senior field command, and in 1078–1079 broke two Anatolian rebellions in succession, against Nikephoros Bryennios the Elder and Nikephoros Basilakes, by tactics that combined ambush, defection, and judicious bribery. By 1080 he was the most successful general of the empire and the most obvious threat to the throne. Botaneiates was old, childless, and irresolute. In February 1081, prompted by Anna Dalassene and the support of the Doukas faction whom he had courted by marrying Eirene Doukaina, granddaughter of the late emperor Constantine X, Alexios and his elder brother Isaac left the capital, raised the eastern themes, and on 1 April 1081 forced the gates of Constantinople by the bribery of a German mercenary captain. The city was sacked by the imperial troops who had nominally seized it for Alexios. He was crowned emperor in Hagia Sophia on 4 April 1081, twenty-four years old.

The reign that began in this confusion lasted thirty-seven years and re-established the empire on a new and durable footing. Three immediate threats — a Norman invasion in the west, a Pecheneg invasion in the north, a Seljuk advance in the east — were present at his accession. The Norman war was the most pressing. Robert Guiscard landed on Corfu in May 1081, besieged Dyrrhachium — the western terminus of the Via Egnatia — through the summer, and on 18 October broke the field army Alexios had brought up to relieve the city. Alexios was wounded and barely escaped. The city fell in February 1082, and Guiscard’s son Bohemond carried the war into Thessaly. Alexios learned in this campaign not to risk pitched battle against the Norman charge; he held the citadels, raised the levy of the Cumans against the Pechenegs in the north, financed Venetian intervention at sea by granting the republic a sweeping commercial chrysobull in 1082, and survived. Robert Guiscard’s death on Cephalonia in July 1085 ended the Norman threat for a generation. The Pecheneg invasion of 1086–1091 was even more dangerous — the steppe horsemen reached the suburbs of Constantinople in 1090 — but on 29 April 1091 at the Levounion in Thrace, with a Cuman army of forty thousand summoned to his aid, Alexios annihilated the Pecheneg horde so completely that it ceased to exist as a people; the chronicler John Skylitzes records that “an entire nation of myriads perished in a single day.”

The Anatolian recovery he could not manage alone. Through the late 1080s and early 1090s he negotiated with the Seljuk emirs, bought back individual cities, sent embassies to the Latin West asking for mercenary cavalry. The embassy to Pope Urban II at the council of Piacenza in March 1095 set in motion something unexpectedly larger: the council of Clermont in November of the same year called the First Crusade. From the summer of 1096 to the spring of 1097, four great undisciplined western armies passed through Byzantine territory on the way to Asia. Alexios met them all, fed them all, and at Constantinople in the spring of 1097 obtained from each of their leaders an oath of fealty: any city of the former Byzantine territory that the crusade recovered was to be returned to the empire. He ferried the armies across the Bosphorus, accompanied them to Nicaea, and on its surrender in June 1097 took possession of the city. The crusade marched east; through the second half of 1097 and 1098 Alexios’s armies recovered the western Anatolian coast, Smyrna, Ephesus, Sardis, Philadelphia, and pushed the Seljuks back to the central plateau. He did not march to relieve Antioch in the summer of 1098; the city fell to Bohemond, who refused to deliver it on the strength of that omission. Bohemond’s war on Constantinople in 1107–1108 ended at Dyrrhachium with the Treaty of Devol, by which the prince of Antioch became Alexios’s vassal — the most complete vindication of his Norman policy.

The institutional reforms that consolidated the recovery have given the period its name of the Komnenian restoration. He stabilised the coinage in 1092 with the introduction of the hyperpyron, a high-quality gold piece that became the standard of Mediterranean trade for two centuries. He reorganised the field army on the basis of pronoia grants — revenue assignments to military officers in return for service — in place of the broken theme system of the previous century. He reduced the senatorial aristocracy of the capital to a court order and concentrated political power on the extended Komnenian family, the brothers and brothers-in-law and nephews who held all the senior commands and governorships of the empire. He prosecuted the heretic Bogomils of Constantinople in a great public trial about 1110, burning their leader Basil in the Hippodrome. He founded the great almshouse of the Orphanotropheion at the head of the Akropolis, the largest charitable institution of medieval Christendom.

The court he left was the most literary the empire had seen since Photios. His wife Eirene Doukaina maintained a salon of philosophers and rhetoricians; his daughter Anna, born in 1083, was trained in Greek classical literature and would in her sixties write the Alexiad, the long Greek prose history of her father’s reign and one of the masterpieces of medieval Greek. His grandson Nikephoros Bryennios the Younger composed at his court a history of the same period that the Alexiad presupposes. The intellectual life of the capital, after a century in eclipse, was restored.

His succession was contested in the bedroom of the dying emperor. Anna and her mother Eirene preferred Anna’s husband Nikephoros Bryennios; Alexios himself, on the testimony of Anna’s own anguished narrative, refused to alter the order of inheritance and confirmed his son John as emperor at the very end. Alexios died at Constantinople on 15 August 1118. John II was acclaimed in the army camp outside the city the next morning. Anna’s subsequent attempt to displace him failed, and she retired to her mother’s monastery, where she wrote the book that has kept her father’s reign more fully present to later ages than that of any other Byzantine emperor.
