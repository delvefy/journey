Margaret Beaufort

1443 – 1509, Countess of Richmond and Derby, “My Lady the King's Mother,” founder of two Cambridge colleges

Margaret was born at Bletsoe Castle in Bedfordshire on 31 May 1443, the only child of John Beaufort, first Duke of Somerset, by his wife Margaret Beauchamp. Her father, a senior captain in Henry VI's French wars, returned in disgrace from an expensive and indecisive expedition to Maine in 1443 and died, possibly by his own hand, within a year of her birth. She inherited from him the estates of the Beaufort line and a thread of royal blood: her great-grandfather was John of Gaunt, fourth son of Edward III, who had legitimised by parliamentary statute in 1397 the four children of his long affair with Katherine Swynford, born before their eventual marriage. The legitimisation had been confirmed by Henry IV with the restriction excepta dignitate regali, "the royal dignity excepted." Whether that excluded her descendants from the throne was the central legal question of Henry VII's eventual title.

She was given as a child ward, in the politics of Henry VI's faction-ridden court, first to William de la Pole, Duke of Suffolk, who married her at six to his own son John in 1450. The marriage was dissolved on Suffolk's fall the same year. In 1453 her wardship was transferred by the king to his half-brothers Edmund and Jasper Tudor, the sons of Catherine of Valois and Owen Tudor; the king then arranged her marriage to Edmund. The wedding took place on 1 November 1455 when she was twelve; her husband was about twenty-five. By the following spring she was pregnant. In August 1456 Edmund was captured by Yorkist troops at Carmarthen, fell ill of plague in their custody, and died on 3 November. Margaret, thirteen years old, six months pregnant, and widowed, rode through hostile Wales to Pembroke Castle and the protection of her brother-in-law Jasper. There on 28 January 1457 she gave birth to her only child, Henry Tudor. The labour was long and dangerous; she never bore another child, and the surviving portraits of her later life are of a thin, narrow-shouldered, ascetic woman with an unusually small frame, possibly the result of injury at this birth.

Her widowhood was brief by necessity. In 1458 she married Henry Stafford, second son of the Duke of Buckingham, a man fourteen years older and politically modest, with whom she lived for thirteen quiet years until his death from wounds taken at the Yorkist victory of Barnet in 1471. By then her son was fourteen and in the wardship of the Yorkist Lord Herbert. The brief Lancastrian readeption of 1470–1471, ended at Tewkesbury, exterminated nearly every other Lancastrian claimant. Henry's place in the succession went from negligible to, after a sequence of deaths, marginal-but-real. Jasper Tudor smuggled him to Brittany in the late summer of 1471. Margaret remained in England.

In 1472 she made the fourth and most political of her marriages: to Thomas Stanley, the wealthiest landowner in north-west England, a magnate so skilled at being on the winning side of every Wars of the Roses confrontation that his loyalty would be the decisive uncertainty at Bosworth thirteen years later. Stanley brought her position at the Yorkist court of Edward IV; she became one of the ladies in waiting at Queen Elizabeth Woodville's churchings and attended at court as Countess of Richmond and Derby. She managed her own estates with care and accumulated capital; she corresponded with her son in Brittany through merchant intermediaries; she paid for the Welsh and Breton agents who kept his cause alive.

The Yorkist crisis of 1483 opened her opportunity. Edward IV died in April; his brother Richard III seized the throne and the persons of his nephews; the young Edward V and his brother Richard disappeared from the Tower of London by the autumn, presumed murdered. The Yorkist establishment was split. Margaret opened secret negotiations with the dowager Queen Elizabeth Woodville — who was in sanctuary at Westminster Abbey with her daughters — through the physician Lewis Caerleon, who attended both women. By Christmas 1483 they had agreed to a marriage between her son and Elizabeth's daughter Elizabeth of York, the principal Yorkist heir; Henry Tudor swore to it at Rennes Cathedral on Christmas Day. The Buckingham revolt of October 1483, which she had quietly supported, failed; her estates were forfeited to her husband Stanley to keep her safe and to keep him useful. She continued to plot.

In August 1485 her son landed at Milford Haven; on 22 August at Bosworth Field her husband's troops intervened at the critical moment to give Henry the battle. She had not, as far as is known, been at Bosworth; she met her son for the first time in fourteen years at his entry into London afterwards. From that month until her death twenty-four years later she occupied a position that had no precedent and no clear constitutional definition: the king's mother. She signed herself Margaret R, an ambiguity Henry VII tolerated; she walked in royal processions only a half-pace behind her daughter-in-law Queen Elizabeth of York; she had her own chambers at Westminster and Eltham; she was granted by parliament the legal status of femme sole, so that she could hold, alienate, and contract as if unmarried, despite her continuing fourth marriage. She separated herself from Thomas Stanley, by then Earl of Derby, from 1499 on a vow of chastity that he accepted.

The institutions she founded reshaped English higher education. From 1505 she refounded and endowed Christ's College, Cambridge, drafting its statutes herself in consultation with her confessor John Fisher, the future bishop of Rochester. She intended also to refound the failing hospital of St John the Evangelist in Cambridge; she did not live to complete the work, and her executors, led by Fisher, refounded it after her death as St John's College, the second of the two Cambridge colleges that owe their existence to her. She established at both Cambridge and Oxford the Lady Margaret professorships in divinity, the oldest endowed academic chairs in England. She patronised the printer William Caxton from her London household and his successor Wynkyn de Worde, encouraging the publication of devotional works for an audience of pious laymen and especially laywomen. Her own translation of the fourth book of Thomas à Kempis's Imitation of Christ, printed by Caxton in 1504, was the first of any female royal English writer to appear in print.

The personal life of her later years was austere. She rose early, heard several masses a day, wore a hair shirt under her gown, and was credited by her confessor with the regular practice of disciplines that her chamber women regarded with concern. She mourned in 1502 the death of her eldest grandson Prince Arthur, in 1503 the death of her daughter-in-law Elizabeth of York. Her son's last illness preoccupied her last spring. He died on 21 April 1509 at Richmond. She presided over the funeral and the proclamation of her grandson Henry VIII; she lived just long enough to see him crowned on 24 June and married to Catherine of Aragon. She died at Cheyneygates within the precincts of Westminster Abbey on 29 June 1509, in her sixty-seventh year, six days after the new king turned eighteen. She is buried in the south aisle of the Lady Chapel of Westminster Abbey, beneath a black marble tomb by Pietro Torrigiano, the same Florentine sculptor who made the tomb of Henry VII and Elizabeth of York.

Her influence on the dynasty was structural. She had preserved her son's cause through fourteen years of exile against the running grain of probability, married him to the Yorkist heir, transferred to him the institutions of a court when he had none, and lived to see his throne stable enough to pass without violence to his successor. She had, in the brutal phrase of the constitutional historian, founded a dynasty by managing the marriage of her son. She had also been a builder of colleges, a translator and patron of devotional literature, and the principal female intellectual figure of late fifteenth-century English public life. The thirteen-year-old widow at Pembroke had become the matriarch of the Tudor century.
