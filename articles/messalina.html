Messalina

c. AD 17 – AD 48 · third wife of the emperor Claudius

Valeria Messalina, born about AD 17 and dead in the Gardens of Lucullus at Rome on the eleventh of October AD 48, was the third wife of the emperor Claudius and the mother of his two acknowledged children, Octavia and Britannicus. She was nineteen or twenty when she married him, about thirty when she died; her empress-ship lasted seven and a half years and ended in a public scandal so spectacular that, even at a remove of nineteen centuries, the bare outline of the event still cannot be reduced to dependable history. The senatorial historians of the next generation made her the type of the sexually shameless empress; modern historical readings have variously argued that the story as transmitted is exaggerated, that the underlying conflict was a political fight for control of access to Claudius against the powerful imperial freedmen Narcissus and Pallas, or some combination of the two. The case, like that of Julia the Elder, cannot now be settled. What is undisputed is that she went through a public ceremony of marriage with a man other than her husband while her husband was emperor, that her husband's freedman Narcissus persuaded the emperor to act, and that she died by a tribune's sword in the late afternoon of the same day.

She was a great-granddaughter of Octavia the Younger, the sister of Augustus, by Octavia's second daughter Claudia Marcella the Younger and the consular Marcus Valerius Messalla Barbatus, and was thus a second cousin both to her own husband Claudius and to her own mother-in-law Antonia the Younger — the Julio-Claudian family at the third generation having become so intermarried that the precise relationships require a diagram. She was probably born about AD 17, although later dates have also been proposed; the exact date is not preserved. She was thus about fourteen when Sejanus fell, about twenty when Tiberius died, and about twenty when she was married to Claudius in AD 38 or 39, in Caligula's reign. Claudius was then about forty-nine, twice married and twice unhappy. The match was political — Messalina's family of Valerii Messallae stood high in the early Julio-Claudian period and her own grandmother had been one of the Marcellae — and the bride was nearly thirty years younger than the groom.

Two children of the marriage survived: a daughter, Octavia, born in AD 39 or 40, who would in time be married to her stepbrother Nero and put to death by him in AD 62; and a son, Tiberius Claudius Caesar Britannicus, born twenty-two days after his father's accession in February of AD 41 and given the cognomen Britannicus by his father's award two years later in honor of the British victory. The son was the natural heir of the new principate and the political center of Messalina's position in the imperial household.

Her seven years as empress are difficult to assess. The hostile senatorial tradition, transmitted at length by Tacitus and at greater length by the epitomators of Cassius Dio and by Suetonius, presents her as engaged in continuous adultery on a public and reckless scale and recounts a series of episodes — the alleged competition with the prostitute Scylla on who could service more men in a night, the alleged murders of senators who refused to sleep with her, the alleged sale of her favor for political access — that read as composed satire and are not independently corroborated. Modern readings have tended to treat these stories with the same skepticism that they apply to the parallel stories told of Julia the Elder and of Agrippina the Younger, and to look for the underlying political content. Two solid lines of analysis are available.

The first is that in the late 40s the senatorial class at Rome was fighting an increasingly losing battle for influence at the imperial court against the new and very powerful freedman secretariats — Narcissus's at the imperial correspondence, Pallas's at the imperial treasury, Callistus's at petitions — that Claudius had created. Messalina, by senatorial blood and senatorial connection, was the political ally of the senatorial faction against the freedmen; the destruction of senators who had crossed her in AD 47 and early AD 48 — most notably Decimus Valerius Asiaticus, executed on her information for treason, and other consulars of her father's generation — was as much a political purge as a personal vendetta, and the freedmen had their own scores to settle with her.

The second is that by the early summer of AD 48 Messalina, then about thirty and aware that the imperial freedmen and the alternative imperial woman Agrippina the Younger — Claudius's niece, two years older than Messalina, in waiting at Rome since her return from exile in AD 41 — were maneuvering against her, made the most desperate and most extraordinary political move of her short life. She compelled the consul-designate Gaius Silius — by Tacitus's report the most attractive young senator at Rome, whom she had been openly using for some time as a lover — to divorce his wife. In the late summer or early autumn of AD 48, while Claudius was at Ostia inspecting the new harbor works, Messalina and Silius went through a full Roman wedding ceremony at Rome, with witnesses, signed marriage contract, and a public ritual feast. The precise legal character of this ceremony — whether it was an actual bigamous marriage or a ritual play of some kind — has been the subject of much speculation; Tacitus himself, who tells the story, notes that he can hardly expect to be believed.

The most coherent reading of the move is that it was an attempt at a coup: Messalina, expecting that the freedmen would shortly be able to convince Claudius to repudiate her, was attempting to position Silius as an alternative consort and Britannicus as the heir of a new Silius-Messalina principate, with the political backing of the senatorial faction. Narcissus, on whose own continued position the survival of the existing arrangement depended, intervened.

He sent two of Claudius's mistresses to bring word to the emperor at Ostia. He arranged for two senators to accompany the emperor back to Rome and brief him on the situation in stages so that he could not lose his nerve. He took command of the praetorian guard for the day and dispatched a tribune with orders for the death of Silius and Messalina before Claudius could either reverse the decision or be reached by Messalina, who was attempting to send him a message through her children and her mother Domitia Lepida. The tribune found her in the Gardens of Lucullus, where she had taken refuge with her mother. Domitia Lepida is said to have urged her to do the deed herself with dignity; she could not bring herself to. The tribune did it for her. Silius was beheaded the same day. Claudius, on his return to the palace at evening, was at supper and asked, when news of his wife's death was brought, for more wine. He never afterwards referred to her by name in public. The Senate decreed a damnatio memoriae; her name and her image were chiselled off public monuments and her busts removed from the imperial palaces.

Her son Britannicus was seven at her death; her daughter Octavia eight or nine. Claudius the following year married his niece Agrippina the Younger; Agrippina engineered the displacement of Britannicus by her own son Nero; Britannicus was poisoned by Nero at a dinner table in AD 55, the year after Claudius's death. Octavia was married off to Nero and put to death by him eight years later. The line of Messalina, in short, did not survive her by very long. The political objective of Narcissus's intervention — to keep the principate inside the existing arrangement of Claudian freedmen and a Claudian heir — was undone within a year by the rise of Agrippina; and Narcissus himself, who had survived Messalina by saving the emperor and the prince, was forced into suicide on Agrippina's order shortly after Claudius's death.

What survives of Messalina in the public memory is the figure constructed for her by the senatorial historians: the empress who married another man while her husband ruled, who was killed in the gardens by a guardsman with her mother watching, who could not bring herself to do the deed herself. The political woman behind that figure, if she was there, is now beyond recovery.
