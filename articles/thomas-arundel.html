Thomas Arundel

1353 – 1414, Archbishop of Canterbury, Chancellor of England, the church's chief weapon against Lollardy

Thomas Arundel was born in 1353, the third son of Richard FitzAlan, third Earl of Arundel and ninth Earl of Surrey, and his second wife Eleanor of Lancaster. The FitzAlans of Arundel were among the wealthier of the older English earldoms, with great estates along the Welsh March and in Sussex; Thomas's elder brother Richard, fourth Earl of Arundel, would be one of the five Lords Appellant who overthrew Richard II's first favourites in 1387 to 1388. Like the third son of any great house in the fourteenth century Thomas was placed in the church. He matriculated at Oriel College, Oxford, in the early 1360s and was given a series of livings, accumulated by family pressure, before he had reached the age of fifteen. He was made Bishop of Ely — the wealthiest see in southern England after Winchester — in 1373 at the age of twenty.

The promotion of so young a man to so great a see was, even by the standards of the later fourteenth century, scandalous; it set the pattern for his career, which combined unusual ability with the unembarrassed deployment of the family interest. He held Ely until 1388, was translated to York in that year as one of the rewards of his brother's Appellant party, and was advanced to Canterbury in 1396. He was, for most of the period from 1388 to his death in 1414, the chief political churchman of England, allied at intervals with one faction or another but always defending the rights and the property of the church as he understood them.

His first political role of consequence was as Chancellor of England under Richard II from 1386 to 1389, the year of the Appellants. He was Chancellor again from 1391 to 1396. In 1397, when Richard II struck against the Appellants, Thomas's brother the fourth Earl of Arundel was beheaded on Tower Hill on 21 September; Thomas himself, then archbishop, was impeached and exiled the following month, his lands forfeited. He retreated to the papal court of Boniface IX at Rome, then to Florence; he was, in his absence from England, one of the principal figures in the small exiled opposition to Richard II that included his nephew the new Earl of Arundel and, from September 1398, Henry Bolingbroke.

He returned to England with Bolingbroke in July 1399 as Bolingbroke's chief ecclesiastical adviser. He sat with him on the parliamentary commission that drew up the articles of Richard II's deposition; he formally accepted Richard's abdication at the Tower on 29 September; he stood by Henry IV at his coronation at Westminster on 13 October. Restored to Canterbury, he resumed the chancellorship from 1407 to 1410 and again in 1412 to 1413. He was the architect of the church's policy through the first Lancastrian reign and, by general agreement, the most able administrator in the kingdom.

The dominant theme of his archiepiscopate was the campaign against Lollardy — the movement of unorthodox theology and biblical translation that descended from John Wycliffe. He convened the Council of Oxford in 1407 and, the following year, promulgated the Constitutions of Oxford, the most far-reaching set of anti-Lollard provisions ever enacted by the English church: they prohibited the translation of any part of Holy Scripture into English without diocesan licence, forbade unauthorised preaching, regulated theological teaching in the universities, and made the possession of unlicensed vernacular Bibles or theological tracts punishable by ecclesiastical court. The statute De heretico comburendo of 1401, the act that introduced burning at the stake into English law, had passed through parliament under his influence; he used it sparingly but with effect, presiding over the trial of William Sawtre, the first Englishman burnt for heresy in March 1401, and over the long examination of John Badby, burnt at Smithfield in 1410 in the presence of the future Henry V.

His relations with the future king were complicated. The prince of Wales had matured politically through the long Welsh war of 1403 to 1410 and had, by his early twenties, become an effective working head of council during his father's recurrent illnesses. Arundel resented being displaced by the prince and his Beaufort allies in the council of 1410 to 1411; the prince in turn distrusted the archbishop's appetite for clerical politics. The breach came in November 1411, when the king resumed personal control and dismissed the prince's council. Arundel was reinstated as Chancellor for the king's last fourteen months and held the office until the king's death in March 1413.

The new king Henry V removed him from the chancellorship at once but in other matters maintained him in honour. Arundel crowned the new king at Westminster on 9 April 1413, the second Lancastrian coronation he had performed. His last great public role was the examination of Sir John Oldcastle, Lord Cobham, the leading Lollard knight, in September 1413 — an interrogation that ended in Oldcastle's escape from the Tower in October and the abortive Lollard rising at St Giles's Fields in January 1414. The rising failed; the Lollard movement, deprived of its noble patrons, retreated underground for a century. Arundel had broken its political wing.

He died at the manor of Lambeth on 19 February 1414, sixty years old, having served two kings as Chancellor through five separate tenures, and three popes — Urban VI, Boniface IX, and the Roman line of the Schism — as the chief English ecclesiastic of the day. He was buried at Canterbury, where his tomb survives in the south aisle of the cathedral choir. The chronicler Thomas Walsingham, who was largely sympathetic to him, called him "the most distinguished ornament of the English church"; the Wycliffite memorialists, less sympathetic, recorded his death with a list of those he had persecuted.

His historical reputation has followed the long shifts in English judgement on the Lollards. The Reformation tradition, which canonised Wycliffe in retrospect as a proto-Protestant, treated Arundel as a heresy-hunter; the modern study of late medieval religion has been kinder, recovering him as an able and consistent defender of an established order under enormous political pressure. The Constitutions of Oxford remained on the English statute book until the Reformation, and their effect on English religious culture through the fifteenth century was unusually durable: vernacular biblical translation in England was driven, in part, into the underground in which Tyndale found it a century later.

Arundel's career sits at the political-ecclesiastical hinge of the late Plantagenet to Lancastrian transition. He had been a junior bishop under Edward III, a senior politician under Richard II, the kingmaker's bishop under Henry IV, and a continuing presence in the early reign of Henry V. The English church in those forty years was, by general agreement, more fully integrated with the political organisation of the kingdom than at any time before or after the period; Arundel was its working chief.
