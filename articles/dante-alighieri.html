Dante Alighieri

1265 – 1321, Florentine poet, exile, author of the Divine Comedy, founder of the Italian literary language

Dante was born at Florence in the second half of May 1265, almost certainly between 14 May and 13 June, under the sign of Gemini as he records in Paradiso XXII. His father Alighiero di Bellincione degli Alighieri was a small banker of modest social standing in the parish of San Martino al Vescovo; the family had been Guelph since the great factional break of the 1240s but were not among the leading houses of the city. His mother Bella died when Dante was perhaps seven; his father remarried and died about 1283 when Dante was eighteen. He was the eldest son.

His formal education is poorly documented but evident in his work: a grammar-school grounding in Latin, the trivium and quadrivium of the Florentine schools, the rhetoric of Brunetto Latini whose Tresor Dante would later read and to whose tutorship Dante credits himself in Inferno XV, and from his twenties the philosophical theology of the Dominican studium at Santa Maria Novella and the Franciscan studium at Santa Croce. He read Aristotle in the Latin translations of Albert the Great and his pupil Thomas Aquinas; he read Augustine and Boethius and Gregory the Great; he read what little Plato was available, mostly through the Timaeus in Calcidius's partial translation. The intellectual furniture of the Commedia is the Thomistic synthesis of Christian Aristotelianism, mediated through the schools of his city.

He had been betrothed by his father, in the conventional way, in 1277 to Gemma Donati of the great Donati family, and married her in the early 1280s. The marriage was the political and social arrangement of two Guelph families and produced three or four children. But the central erotic experience of his life, by his own account, was for another woman: Beatrice Portinari, the daughter of the merchant Folco Portinari, whom he had first seen at her father's house at a children's gathering in May 1274 when she was nearly nine and he was nine. She married Simone de' Bardi about 1287 and died young on 8 June 1290, twenty-four years old. The Vita Nuova, written between 1292 and 1294 and the work in which he first came to literary maturity, is an autobiographical prosimetrum — alternating prose narrative and lyric poems — that recounts his love for Beatrice and culminates in his promise, at the end of the book, to say of her things never before said of any woman.

He was, through his thirties, increasingly active in Florentine politics. He served on the Council of the Hundred in 1295, on several embassies through the late 1290s, and in June 1300 was one of the six elected priors of the city — the supreme executive office of the Florentine republic — for the brief two-month term of his priorate. His decision in that priorate to exile both the leaders of the Black Guelphs and the leaders of his own party the White Guelphs to quiet the city's factional violence was politically catastrophic. When in November 1301 Charles of Valois, brother of Philip IV of France, entered Florence at the invitation of the Black Guelphs and Pope Boniface VIII to "pacify" the city, the Black Guelphs returned, took power, and conducted a thorough purge of the Whites. Dante, who was on embassy at Rome at the time, was condemned in absentia on 27 January 1302 to two years' exile and a fine of five thousand florins; when he refused to return and pay the fine, the sentence was extended to death by burning if he ever set foot in Florence again. He never did.

His twenty years of exile took him through every major town of central and northern Italy: to Verona, where he stayed at the court of the della Scala lords, in particular Cangrande della Scala, the patron to whom Paradiso would be dedicated; to Padua and Treviso; to Lunigiana with the Malaspina; to Casentino with the Guidi; to Ravenna with Guido Novello da Polenta, where he settled in 1318 and lived out his last years. In the early years of exile he wrote political treatises — the Latin De Monarchia, his argument for a universal Roman emperor as the necessary temporal authority of Christendom; the unfinished Convivio, an Italian prose encyclopedia of Aristotelian philosophy aimed at the lay reader; the unfinished De Vulgari Eloquentia, the first systematic theoretical defence of vernacular Italian as a literary medium. Each was abandoned, in the form he had begun it, for the new work that was beginning to take shape.

That work was the Commedia, the long allegorical poem in 14,233 hendecasyllabic lines of terza rima, divided into a hundred cantos in three canticles — Inferno, Purgatorio, and Paradiso, of thirty-three cantos each plus an introductory canto to the whole. The action is the poet's vision-journey in Holy Week of the year 1300 through the funnel of Hell, up the mountain of Purgatory, and into the celestial spheres of Paradise. The guide through Hell and Purgatory is the shade of Virgil; the guide in Paradise is Beatrice, recovered from death and restored as the wisdom of revealed theology; the last vision is of the Trinity. The work draws into its scheme the entire intellectual, theological, political, and personal world of its author. It was begun about 1308; Inferno circulated by 1315; Purgatorio by 1317; the last canto of Paradiso was found by his sons after his death, in a recess behind a panel in his bedroom at Ravenna, and added to the manuscripts that already circulated incomplete.

The work was understood almost at once as a literary event of European magnitude. Boccaccio in the next generation copied multiple manuscripts, wrote the first commentary, and was the first lecturer on the poem in any university. The lyrical sweetness of the Vita Nuova, the great descriptive set-pieces of Inferno, the moral patience of Purgatorio, the philosophical and theological intensities of Paradiso, together created a literary work that has remained for seven hundred years one of the central poems of the European tradition. Its language — the Tuscan vernacular he refined and made into a literary medium — became, through the Commedia, the basis of the standard Italian language.

He died at Ravenna on the night of 13 to 14 September 1321, fifty-six years old, of malaria contracted while returning by the marshes from a diplomatic mission to Venice on Polenta's behalf. He was buried at the church of San Francesco in Ravenna, where his remains have stayed despite repeated Florentine attempts to recover them; the empty tomb prepared for him in Santa Croce in Florence stands beside Michelangelo's. Florence formally rescinded his sentence of exile in 2008, six hundred and eighty-seven years after his death.

His afterlife is the long story of the European reception of one poem. The Commedia was the first major vernacular poem of any country to take its place beside Latin classics in the lectured curriculum; it was printed within decades of the introduction of printing into Italy (first edition Foligno 1472); it carried Italian humanism into northern Europe through the imitations of Chaucer and the Spenserians; it was the model, in form and ambition, of every great long allegorical poem of the European Renaissance. T. S. Eliot in the twentieth century would write that "Dante and Shakespeare divide the modern world between them; there is no third"; the historical Dante was a Florentine exile of the early fourteenth century who had been condemned to death by his city and who, in the decades that followed, wrote in a foreign town the poem that gave that city its language.

The road continues to Pope Boniface VIII.
