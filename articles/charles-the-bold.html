Charles the Bold

1433 – 1477, Duke of Burgundy, last Valois lord of the Burgundian state, killed in the snow before Nancy

Charles was born at Dijon on 10 November 1433, the only surviving son of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, and Isabella of Portugal, herself a granddaughter of John of Gaunt of Lancaster. His baptism in the ducal church of Saint-Bénigne was attended by every great house in Christendom. The duchy he was born into was already an anomaly in Europe: not a kingdom but in wealth and military power the equivalent of one, a string of provinces straddling the language frontier of French and Dutch, held in vassalage partly from the King of France and partly from the Emperor, the financial and artistic capital of the north under his father, and held together by a court ceremonial more elaborate than any royal court of the age.

Charles grew up at Brussels and Hesdin, was raised by his mother in habits of unusual self-discipline and Latin learning, and held from infancy the title Count of Charolais. He married three times, all dynastically: at twelve, in 1440, the seven-year-old Catherine of France, daughter of Charles VII, who died in 1446; in 1454, Isabella of Bourbon, who bore him his only surviving child Mary in 1457 and died in 1465; and in 1468, with great ceremony at Bruges, the twenty-two-year-old Margaret of York, sister of Edward IV of England, a marriage that committed his foreign policy to the English alliance for the rest of his reign.

His relations with his father were strained. Philip the Good favoured the Croÿ family at court; Charles distrusted them. Their final breach in 1465 left Charles in effective control of the duchy, although Philip lived on until 1467. In that same year, 1465, with several great nobles of France around him, Charles led the so-called League of the Public Weal against the new French king Louis XI, fighting the inconclusive battle of Montlhéry — called la mêlée de Montlhéry, the brawl of Montlhéry — in which he was wounded and held the field, more or less, by nightfall. The peace that followed gave the league the Somme towns. From 1465 to his death, Charles's principal antagonist was Louis XI.

Their rivalry was the central political fact of the age in Western Europe. Louis was cautious, dissimulating, infinitely patient, a master of bribery and rumour; Charles was direct, soldierly, almost ostentatiously straightforward, willing to risk everything on the field. In 1468 Louis came to Péronne in person to negotiate with him; while they were talking, news arrived of a rising in Liège fomented by Louis's agents. Charles, in a fury, kept the king as a hostage for two days and forced him to ride with him to the sack of Liège, where the bishop's city was emptied and most of it burned. The Treaty of Péronne, signed under duress, was repudiated by Louis as soon as he reached Paris. The pattern set itself thereafter: Charles winning territories on the ground, Louis taking them back by diplomacy and gold.

The duke's ambition was a kingdom. The territories of the Burgundian house were a corridor with a gap in the middle — the duchy of Lorraine in the south, the duchy of Cleves and the imperial bishoprics in the north. Charles set out to close the gap and to translate his ducal status into a royal one. He occupied the duchy of Guelders in 1473 by purchase from its exhausted last duke. The same year he met the Emperor Frederick III at Trier with the intention of being crowned king of Burgundy or Frisia; the negotiations broke down. He pressed into Alsace by purchase of pawned lands from Sigismund of Tyrol, installed there a heavy-handed governor, Peter von Hagenbach, who within two years had so outraged the local towns that they rose, captured him, tried him, and beheaded him in 1474. The towns then turned to the Swiss for protection.

In 1474 he undertook the great siege of Neuss on the Rhine, intended to subdue the archbishop of Cologne; the siege ran for almost a year, drew an imperial relief army under Frederick III, and ended in May 1475 without a clear result. The expense ruined his treasury. The same year his English ally Edward IV crossed to Calais with an army, expecting Charles to support him in a joint invasion of France; Charles was tied down at Neuss, and Edward, finding no Burgundian army, accepted Louis XI's gold at Picquigny and went home. The League against Charles now included the Swiss, the duke of Lorraine, the Alsatian cities, and (covertly) the French crown.

The Swiss war was the end of him. In March 1476 he led a Burgundian army against the small Vaudois town of Grandson, took it, hanged its surrendering garrison, and was then surprised on 2 March by a relieving Swiss column. The Swiss pikemen rolled up his army with terrifying speed; he lost his baggage train, including his throne, his tapestries, his jewels, his great diamonds, and even his hat — treasures that ended up in the great churches and town halls of Bern, Zurich, Lucerne, and Solothurn, where some can still be seen. He rebuilt the army in three months and besieged Murten (Morat) on the Swiss frontier; the relieving Swiss force struck him on 22 June 1476, and a second, more comprehensive disaster followed. He retreated wounded into the duchy of Burgundy and brooded through the autumn at Nancy. Then he marched on Nancy itself, which the young René II of Lorraine had retaken from his garrison.

On 5 January 1477, in deep snow before the walls, a combined army of Lorrainers and Swiss attacked his besieging camp. His army, smaller than the relief force, was outflanked and surrounded. Charles, refusing to retreat, fought with the rear and was killed in the rout; his body, half-eaten by wolves and stripped by looters, was found three days later in a frozen ditch identified only by his missing teeth and a long scar. He was forty-three years old.

His state did not survive him by a year. Louis XI invaded at once, took Burgundy proper, Picardy, the Boulonnais, the Somme towns, and pressed for the marriage of his Dauphin to Mary. Mary married Maximilian of Habsburg instead, taking the surviving territories into the empire. The Burgundian dream of an autonomous kingdom between France and Germany was over; the lasting structures of the Burgundian state — the chancery, the order of the Golden Fleece, the court ceremonial, the patronage of Memling and Van der Goes — survived into Habsburg hands and through them into the Spanish empire and modern Belgium and the Netherlands.

The contemporary judgement, given by Philippe de Commynes who served first Charles and then Louis, was that he had been a great prince but had lacked the patience to outwait an enemy who was clever and the humility to listen to advice that contradicted him. The verdict has not greatly changed. He had pursued the maximum possible objective with the maximum directness against an opponent willing to do everything in the world to stop him, and on a winter morning he had paid for it.
