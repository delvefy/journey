Andrei Kurbsky

1528 – 1583, Russian prince, general, and the most famous defector of his age

Andrei Mikhailovich Kurbsky was born in 1528 into the upper rank of Muscovite princely society, descended through the Yaroslavl branch of the house of Rurik. He came of age in the years when Ivan IV was assembling around himself the council of reformers — Adashev, Sylvester, Macarius — who would later be remembered as the Chosen Council, and as a young man Kurbsky stood close enough to that group to share its outlook and to be marked by Ivan's later turn against it.

He proved a soldier of unusual capability early. In the great campaign against Kazan in 1552 he commanded one of the storming columns and was severely wounded in the climactic assault; the conquest of the Tatar capital was, in his own later telling, the founding event of his political life and his proof that an Orthodox prince in arms could end a heathen state. In the next decade he held senior commands on the southern and Livonian frontiers, was made a boyar, and was numbered among the closest advisers of the young tsar. The autobiography of his political position can be read in his later letters: he was a Rurikid magnate, conscious of his lineage as older than the Daniilovich line of Moscow, and convinced that the tsar ruled with and through the council of such men rather than alone.

In Ivan's reign that view became dangerous. The death of the tsaritsa Anastasia in 1560 broke the moderating influence on Ivan, and within a few years his old council had been dispersed: Adashev disgraced, Sylvester sent to a monastery, others executed. By 1563–1564 Kurbsky was in command at Dorpat (Yuriev) on the Livonian front, and his troops were defeated in January 1564 at the Battle of Ula by the Lithuanian hetman Mikołaj Radziwiłł the Red. He had been negotiating in secret with the Lithuanian court for some time; in late April 1564, expecting his recall and disgrace at any moment, he crossed the frontier with a small party of servants and presented himself to King Sigismund II Augustus. He left in Russia his wife, his young son, and his mother, all of whom suffered for his desertion at Ivan's hand.

In Lithuania he was made a magnate of the Crown. Sigismund granted him a starostwo at Kovel in Volhynia with estates sufficient to support a great house, and Kurbsky took service in arms against his own former command, leading Lithuanian cavalry in the campaigns of the late 1560s. From the moment he was settled at Kovel he began the work for which posterity remembers him most: the correspondence with the tsar he had left behind.

The first letter is dated 1564 and was carried across the frontier by his servant Vaska Shibanov, who was tortured to death on its delivery but, by the legend, refused to drop the letter. Ivan replied at length; Kurbsky replied to the reply; and so the exchange continued, with long intervals, into the late 1570s — five letters in all on each side, the most famous body of political correspondence in pre-modern Russian. The argument is about everything. Kurbsky accuses the tsar of murdering his own servants, of breaking the bond between sovereign and aristocracy, of betraying the Orthodox political ideal of a ruler advised by good counsellors and constrained by law. Ivan answers with a defence of unconstrained autocracy as a divinely instituted duty, derides Kurbsky's treason, mocks his learning, and threads through everything an unmistakable note of personal grievance against a man who had been one of his closest friends. The letters were unknown to Europe at large for centuries but became, in the nineteenth century, foundational texts of Russian political thought, the first explicit articulation in the language of the autocratic and the aristocratic visions that would contend through the next four hundred years.

The other half of Kurbsky's literary work was his History of the Grand Prince of Moscow, written in Lithuania in the 1570s, a treatment of Ivan's reign that explained the catastrophe of the oprichnina by reference to the corruption of evil counsellors, the abandonment of the Chosen Council, and the inherent dangers of unrestrained royal will. It is a polemic, but it is also the earliest substantial historical narrative of any Russian reign written by a contemporary insider; modern study of Ivan's politics begins with arguments about how far Kurbsky's account can be trusted.

In Kovel he also took up the cultural work of an exiled Orthodox prince. He gathered translators and copyists, sponsored Slavonic translations of the Greek Fathers, took an interest in the manuscript circle that traced back to Maxim the Greek, and wrote against the spreading Protestantism of Lithuania, which he regarded as a threat to Orthodox identity in the Commonwealth. His household in Volhynia became a small Orthodox literary centre at a moment when such centres mattered: the Union of Brest, by which much of the Ruthenian church would enter communion with Rome, was a generation away, and the cultural infrastructure of the Orthodox lands was being defined.

He remarried twice in Lithuania. His second wife, the wealthy widow Maria Yuryevna Holshanska, brought him further estates and a divorce-laden lawsuit that occupied much of his last decade; his third, Aleksandra Semashko, was a younger woman who bore him a son, Dmitri Kurbsky, who founded a Polish-Lithuanian noble line that lasted into the seventeenth century. He himself died in May 1583 at Kovel, in his fifty-fifth year, having survived by a few months the Truce of Yam-Zapolsky that ended the Livonian War on terms Ivan could never have accepted from any other hand than that of Stephen Báthory of Poland.

Posterity has been kinder to Kurbsky than to Ivan, though not unanimously. Karamzin, in his great history, drew on him heavily and accepted much of his portrait of the tsar. Soviet historians, conversely, often sided with Ivan against the “treason” of the boyar prince. The argument has never quite ended; Kurbsky's letters and Ivan's answers are still read in Russian schools, and the question of which man was right — the autocrat or the émigré — is still, in a sense, the question of Russian political philosophy.
