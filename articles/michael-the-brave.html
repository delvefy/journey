Michael the Brave

c. 1558 – 1601, Voivode of Wallachia and short-lived unifier of the three Romanian principalities

Michael was a striking figure to his contemporaries and a foundational one to posterity. Born around 1558 in the Wallachian gentry — possibly the illegitimate son of the voivode Pătrașcu the Good — he came of age in a Wallachia squeezed between Ottoman fiscal demands and the political ambitions of its own boyar class. His rise through the offices of ban of Mehedinți and then ban of Craiova was steady rather than meteoric, but it carried him close enough to the throne that, by 1593, after years of intrigue and large bribes paid at Istanbul, he was confirmed as Voivode of Wallachia.

He inherited a country crushed under the weight of indemnities, garrison costs, and the ever-mounting tribute owed to the Porte. Levantine moneylenders, advancing the boyars the sums needed to settle these obligations, had become a hated power in their own right. Within a year of taking the throne Michael moved against them. On 13 November 1594, in a coordinated action in Bucharest, his men set fire to a quarter where the creditors lodged and killed them and the Turkish garrison alike. This was not a riot but a declaration: Wallachia had joined the Holy League being assembled by Pope Clement VIII, the Habsburg emperor Rudolf II, and Sigismund Báthory of Transylvania, with the Moldavian voivode Aaron the Tyrant following his lead.

The Ottoman response came in the summer of 1595 in the form of Sinan Pasha, the elderly grand vizier, leading an army of around one hundred thousand south of the Danube. Michael, with a fraction of that number, chose his ground at the marshy crossing of the Neajlov at Călugăreni and on 13 August inflicted a sharp tactical defeat on the Ottoman vanguard before withdrawing into the Carpathian foothills. The retreat was prudent — he could not hold the field — but his reinforcement, when it came from Transylvania, allowed him to retake Bucharest and Târgoviște by autumn. Călugăreni would be remembered for centuries as the day a small Christian principality bloodied the Porte in open battle.

The cost of the Transylvanian alliance was the Treaty of Alba Iulia, by which Michael accepted vassalage to Sigismund Báthory. Through the late 1590s he chafed against this subordination while pressing the war: raids deep into Ottoman territory, the siege and burning of Nicopolis, a steady drumbeat of campaigning along the Danube. Sigismund's repeated abdications and re-accessions in Transylvania left the principality in chaos; when his cousin Cardinal Andrew Báthory took the throne in 1599 and prepared to make peace with Istanbul, Michael chose to act unilaterally. In October 1599, at Șelimbăr near Sibiu, he crushed Andrew's army; the Cardinal fled and was killed by Székely peasants in the mountains. Michael entered Alba Iulia and was acclaimed prince of Transylvania, ostensibly in Rudolf II's name but in practice as his own master.

A year later he carried his arms north. Ieremia Movilă, the Polish-backed voivode of Moldavia, was driven out in May 1600, and for a few months in the spring and summer of that year Michael held all three Romanian-speaking principalities — Wallachia, Transylvania, and Moldavia — under a single ruler. It was an unprecedented arrangement, never formalised as a single state and held together only by his presence and his army, but it would become one of the foundational images of later Romanian national thought.

He could not keep it. The Hungarian nobility of Transylvania had no wish to be governed by a Wallachian voivode and revolted under the Habsburg general Giorgio Basta; at Mirăslău in September 1600 they routed Michael's forces. Almost simultaneously the Polish hetman Jan Zamoyski crossed into Moldavia, restored Movilă, and pressed on into Wallachia, where his troops defeated Michael at Bucov and installed a puppet voivode. Stripped of all three thrones within a few months, Michael made his way to Prague to throw himself on the mercy of Rudolf II.

The emperor, calculating that he was still useful, received him, paid him, and dispatched him back to the Carpathians in 1601 as co-commander with Basta. The two retook Transylvania from the rebel nobles at the Battle of Guruslău on 3 August. Six days later, on 9 August 1601, on the plain of Câmpia Turzii, a detachment of Walloon mercenaries acting on Basta's orders surrounded Michael's tent and killed him; his head was struck off and reportedly buried at the monastery of Dealu beside the body of his supposed father.

His reign had lasted scarcely eight years. He left no consolidated state, no dynasty of any duration, and a country exhausted by war. Yet the brief moment of 1600 — three principalities under one prince — became, two centuries later, the central proof-text of nineteenth-century Romanian nationalism, which read it back into history as the prefiguration of the modern state. Mihai Viteazul, in that retrospect, ceased to be a Balkan warlord of the Long Turkish War and became the first Romanian, the founder of an idea his contemporaries would not have recognised.
