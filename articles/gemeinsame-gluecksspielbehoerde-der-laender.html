The Gemeinsame Glücksspielbehörde der Länder (GGL; “Joint Gambling Authority of the Länder”) is the common regulator that Germany’s sixteen federal states maintain for gambling offered across state lines, above all on the internet. Created on 1 July 2021 by the Interstate Treaty on Gambling 2021 (Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021) and seated in Halle (Saale) in Saxony‑Anhalt, it examines and licenses cross‑state gambling offerings, supervises the conduct of its licensees, and pursues unlicensed operators and their advertising. It assumed its duties in stages and has exercised the full catalogue of them since 1 January 2023.

Origins

Gambling regulation in Germany is, by constitutional habit, the business of the individual Länder — an arrangement that served tolerably until the internet declined to respect state borders. Successive interstate treaties attempted to coordinate sixteen separate supervisory regimes with mixed success; operators faced a patchwork, and enforcement against offshore websites fell to whichever state authority felt most energetic. A licensing round for sports betting opened under the treaty of 2012 collapsed in the courts without producing a single licence, leaving the online market in a long juridical twilight.

The Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021, which entered into force on 1 July 2021, reset the arrangement. It legalised virtual slot machines and online poker nationwide for the first time, subjected them to strict player‑protection rules, and provided for a single joint authority to administer the new market. The GGL was established the same day as a public‑law institution carried jointly by all sixteen states. Its powers arrived in instalments: from July 2022 it took up the fight against illegal gambling, and on 1 January 2023 it inherited the remaining licensing and supervisory duties that interim state authorities had been performing.

Organisation

The authority is governed by two organs. An administrative board (Verwaltungsrat), on which every state is represented and whose chairmanship rotates among them, settles matters of principle; the day‑to‑day house is run by a two‑member executive board (Vorstand), held since its first appointment in 2021 by Ronald Benter and Benjamin Schwanke. The GGL is a young institution by regulatory standards — industry observers long described it as still in a developmental state — and it has built up its staff, premises and procedures in Halle essentially from nothing since 2021, beginning full operations at the start of 2023 with some seventy‑five employees. It is financed by the Länder jointly and, increasingly, by fees charged to the operators it supervises.

Remit

The GGL holds exclusive competence for gambling offered across state borders, chiefly online: virtual slot machines and online poker, sports betting and horse betting on the internet, nationwide social lotteries, class lotteries, and the commercial brokerage of lottery products in more than one state. Conspicuously outside its remit remain the classical preserves of the individual Länder — casinos (Spielbanken), gaming arcades (Spielhallen), the large state lotteries, and online casino table games, which each state may licence on its own account or not at all.

Alongside licensing, the authority polices the treaty’s player‑protection apparatus — among other things a cross‑operator monthly deposit limit of €1,000, enforced through the central monitoring system LUGAS, and the nationwide exclusion register OASIS — supervises gambling advertising, observes the market, and promotes research into gambling and its harms.

Enforcement

The GGL’s most consulted publication is its whitelist: the official register of every operator permitted to offer gambling in Germany, by which a website’s legality may be checked at a glance. Against those not on it, the authority proceeds with prohibition orders, fines, and payment blocking directed at the financial intermediaries that move the money. Its early attempts to compel internet access providers to block unlicensed websites fared poorly: German administrative courts held in 2023 that the treaty gave it no such power over mere conduits, blunting one of its sharper instruments and leaving payment blocking and advertising enforcement to carry the load.

In practice the authority’s decisions trace the contested edges of the German market — approving and disapproving payment methods, revoking sports betting licences where operators fall short, and classifying borderline products such as daily fantasy sports, which it has treated as unauthorised gambling. It also maintains a whistle‑blower system through which violations may be reported.

A deeper delve

The constitutional setting

To understand why Germany needed a “joint authority of the Länder” at all, one must begin with the Basic Law. The Grundgesetz assigns legislative competences to the federation only where it says so; what it does not mention falls to the states. Gambling, treated as a matter of public order and safety, is for the most part one of those unmentioned things. The federation holds a few old footholds — the criminal prohibition of unauthorised public gambling in § 284 of the Criminal Code, the trade‑law regime governing amusement machines with prizes, and the Rennwett‑ und Lotteriegesetz of 1922, a venerable statute on horse‑race betting that doubles as the country’s gambling‑tax law — but the substance of who may offer what game to whom, and under which conditions, is state law, sixteen times over.

The Länder coordinate such shared business through interstate treaties (Staatsverträge), instruments with a distinctive fragility: every one of the sixteen state parliaments must ratify, and any one of them can sink the whole edifice or, scarcely better, stay aboard while quietly rowing in another direction. The history of German gambling regulation since the turn of the millennium is largely the history of such treaties being negotiated, ratified, litigated, and renegotiated.

Two judicial interventions set the terms of that history. In March 2006 the Federal Constitutional Court held that the state monopoly on sports betting, as then practised, violated the constitutional freedom of occupation: a state that reserves betting to itself while marketing its own product with commercial gusto cannot claim to be fighting addiction. The monopoly could stand only if consistently and credibly oriented towards the prevention of gambling harm, and the Länder were given until the end of 2007 to choose between genuine consistency and genuine liberalisation. The second intervention came from Luxembourg: the Court of Justice of the European Union, in a line of cases culminating in the judgments of September 2010, demanded “coherence” — a member state restricting the free movement of gambling services must pursue its declared public‑interest aims systematically, not protect a lottery monopoly with one hand while tolerating an expansive commercial slot‑machine trade with the other. Between the Karlsruhe hammer and the Luxembourg anvil, the Länder spent fifteen years forging one treaty after another, and the GGL is the eventual product of that smithy.

The treaty of 2008 and the treaty of 2012

The first answer to the Constitutional Court was the Glücksspielstaatsvertrag of 2008. It chose monopoly over liberalisation: lotteries and sports betting remained reserved to the states, advertising was curtailed, and — most consequentially — the treaty banned the organisation and brokering of public gambling on the internet outright. The ban was defensible in the abstract; the Court of Justice accepted in 2010 that a member state may judge the internet a peculiarly dangerous channel. But the same 2010 judgments found the German monopoly as a whole incoherent, given the commercial machine sector and the states’ own promotional habits, and the regime’s authority never recovered. Offshore operators, typically licensed in Malta or Gibraltar, served German customers more or less openly, arguing that a monopoly contrary to EU law could not be enforced against them.

The First Amendment Treaty (Erster Glücksspieländerungsstaatsvertrag), in force from 1 July 2012, attempted a controlled experiment: sports betting would be opened to private operators under an “experimental clause”, but to no more than twenty licensees, selected in a single nationwide procedure conducted by the state of Hesse on behalf of all. Online casino games and poker remained forbidden. The treaty also created the Glücksspielkollegium, a sixteen‑member coordination committee whose binding majority decisions over individual state authorities drew persistent constitutional doubt — an institutional headache the GGL was later designed to cure.

One state declined to play. Schleswig‑Holstein, under a liberalising government, enacted its own Gambling Act in 2011 and in 2012 issued several dozen licences for online sports betting and even online casino games — the first of their kind in Germany. A new government repealed the act in 2013 and rejoined the treaty, but the licences already granted ran out their six‑year terms, leaving a curious northern enclave of legality. The Court of Justice, asked whether this divergence doomed the treaty under EU law, answered in 2014 that a federal state’s temporary internal untidiness did not by itself render the common regime disproportionate.

The twenty‑licence procedure itself ended in fiasco. Dozens of operators applied; Hesse announced its chosen twenty in 2014; disappointed applicants promptly obtained injunctions; and in 2015 the Hessian administrative courts found the selection opaque and procedurally untenable, with pointed criticism of the Glücksspielkollegium’s role. Not one licence was ever issued. The coup de grâce came from Luxembourg in February 2016, in the Ince judgment: where a licensing regime exists on paper but has demonstrably failed in practice, a member state may not punish an EU operator for lacking the licence it could never actually obtain. Germany’s sports‑betting prohibition had become unenforceable against the very market it was meant to govern — a grey market, tolerated by necessity, that by the late 2010s was worth billions of euros in stakes annually.

The long road to 2021

Repair came slowly. A Second Amendment Treaty, which would have lifted the twenty‑licence cap, was signed in 2017 but died in ratification when Schleswig‑Holstein’s incoming coalition — once again the awkward partner — declined to carry it through. A Third Amendment Treaty succeeded where the second had failed: in force from 1 January 2020, it abolished the numerical cap and converted sports‑betting licensing into a purely qualitative examination, administered by the Regierungspräsidium Darmstadt in Hesse. Even this modest procedure stumbled at the threshold — in the spring of 2020 an Austrian bookmaker persuaded the Administrative Court of Darmstadt to halt the award on procedural grounds — but the obstacle was cleared within months, and in October 2020 Germany issued its first nationwide sports‑betting licences, roughly eight years after first promising them.

By then the Länder had already agreed on something far larger. Negotiations through 2019 divided the states into roughly two camps: those, led by the more restrictive governments, who saw online casino gaming as an evil to be banned harder, and those who argued that fifteen years of prohibition had produced not abstinence but a flourishing black market, and that only a legal offer of tolerable attractiveness could channel players towards protected ground. The channelisation school prevailed. In early 2020 the minister‑presidents settled the text of a new principal treaty, the Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021, and all sixteen state parliaments ratified it in time for entry into force on 1 July 2021.

A peculiar interregnum bridged the gap. In September 2020 the heads of the state chancelleries resolved, by circular decision, that operators of virtual slot machines and online poker who brought their offerings into line with the future treaty’s rules in advance — imposing the deposit limit, the one‑euro stake, the removal of casino table games from their German sites — would not be pursued in the meantime. This “toleration regime”, effective from mid‑October 2020, was derided by addiction researchers as legalisation by administrative fiat and welcomed by the industry as the first honest acknowledgement of reality; either way it gave the market a year’s rehearsal of the coming rulebook.

What the treaty of 2021 permits, and on what terms

The Glücksspielstaatsvertrag 2021 declares its purposes in its first section: to prevent gambling addiction, to channel the natural gambling instinct of the population into orderly and supervised paths, to protect minors and players, and to ward off the criminality that attends unregulated play. Its method is a controlled opening. Three online verticals became licensable nationwide: sports betting (continuing the 2020 regime), virtual slot machines, and online poker — the latter two legal across Germany for the first time in the internet era. Horse‑race betting retained its historic regime, and lotteries remained where they had always been, in the hands of the state monopolists. Online casino table games — roulette, blackjack and their kin — were deliberately left out of the common framework: each Land may offer them itself, license a limited number of private operators (broadly tied to its count of land‑based casino concessions), or decline altogether.

The conditions attached to the new permissions are among the strictest in Europe. Every player must hold a verified account, identified against official data, before a cent is staked. Deposits are capped at €1,000 per month — not per operator but across all operators combined, an aggregate enforced centrally; under the accompanying rules a player who demonstrates the financial means may be allowed a higher individual ceiling, in narrow cases up to several times the default, but the principle of the cross‑operator cap is untouchable. Virtual slot machines may take no more than €1 per spin, each spin must last at least five seconds, autoplay functions are forbidden, and jackpots are banned. A player may be active with only one operator at a time: the ban on parallel play is policed centrally, and switching operators entails a short compulsory pause. A “panic button” on every site triggers an immediate twenty‑four‑hour self‑exclusion.

Advertising, the old battlefield of the monopoly era, is rationed rather than banned. Broadcast and internet advertising for virtual slots and online poker is prohibited between six in the morning and nine in the evening; advertising may not target minors or vulnerable players; and sports‑betting promotion with active athletes and club officials is forbidden. Operators must maintain a social concept (Sozialkonzept) for the early recognition of problem play, train their staff accordingly, and record their game data on a so‑called safe server to which the supervisory authority has access.

Even the vocabulary is regulated. The legalised slot product goes by the treaty’s own coinage, virtuelles Automatenspiel — the virtual machine game — and its operators may not so much as call themselves casinos, that word being reserved to the genuine article. Critics of the deposit cap note that it limits deposits rather than losses, so that a fortunate or patient player may churn considerably more than €1,000 through the system in a month; critics from the other direction note that the cap is real, central and cross‑operator, which no comparable European market then enforced. The whole apparatus rests on a wager of its own: that a legal offer so thoroughly girdled would still be attractive enough to draw players away from the unregulated alternative. Whether that wager is being won remains the central dispute of German gambling policy, and is treated below.

The central nervous system: LUGAS and OASIS

Rules that bind all operators at once require machinery that sees all operators at once, and the treaty therefore built two central databases into the heart of the regime. The first is LUGAS, the Länderübergreifendes Glücksspielaufsichtssystem (“cross‑state gambling supervision system”), to which every licensed operator of virtual slots, poker and sports betting must be connected. LUGAS keeps two files. The limit file (Limitdatei) holds each player’s monthly deposit ceiling and the running total of deposits across all operators: before accepting money, an operator must query the file, and a deposit that would breach the aggregate limit is refused regardless of where the player’s previous deposits were made. The activity file (Aktivitätsdatei) records where a player is currently active and so enforces the ban on parallel play; a player who logs out of one operator to try another waits out a brief cooling interval before the second will seat him. Developed under Hessian stewardship during the transitional years, the system has been in the GGL’s hands since the beginning of 2023.

The second database is older and broader. OASIS — the acronym renders roughly as “online enquiry, player status” (Onlineabfrage Spielerstatus) — is the nationwide exclusion register, grown out of a Hessian system and still administered by the Regierungspräsidium Darmstadt rather than by the GGL. It spans the whole gambling landscape, online and terrestrial alike: casinos, gaming arcades, betting shops and licensed websites must all query it before admitting a customer, and an excluded player is excluded everywhere. Players may bar themselves (with a minimum term of three months, lifted afterwards only on application), and third parties — family members, or the operators themselves on evidence of endangerment — may initiate an exclusion. The register has swollen steadily since the 2021 treaty made its use universal: by 2023 it held roughly two hundred thousand active exclusions, and the count has continued to climb well beyond that since, with operators querying it hundreds of millions of times a month. Privacy scholars have grumbled at the breadth of these files — a complete central ledger of who gambles where, and how much they deposit, is no small thing in a country with Germany’s data‑protection sensibilities — but the courts have so far left the architecture standing.

Anatomy of the authority

The GGL itself is constituted in the treaty’s own text as an Anstalt des öffentlichen Rechts — an institution of public law — carried jointly by all sixteen Länder, with legal capacity of its own and its seat fixed at Halle (Saale). The choice of Halle, a mid‑sized university city in Saxony‑Anhalt, owed something to the standing policy of placing new federal‑level institutions in the eastern states and something to energetic lobbying by the host Land, which also exercises legal supervision over the authority. The GGL is, notably, not a federal agency: the federation appears nowhere in its constitution. It is the sixteen states acting as one — the first time German gambling supervision has had a single address.

Two organs govern it. The administrative board (Verwaltungsrat) seats one representative of each Land, typically a senior ministry official; its chairmanship rotates among the states, and its decisions are taken, as a rule, by a two‑thirds majority — a deliberate echo of, and improvement upon, the old Glücksspielkollegium, whose binding majority votes had been attacked as constitutionally rootless. The board settles questions of principle, adopts the budget, and appoints the executive. Day‑to‑day direction belongs to a two‑member executive board (Vorstand), filled on 30 September 2021 with a pairing that neatly mirrors the authority’s dual nature: Ronald Benter, an economist who had headed the gambling unit of Schleswig‑Holstein’s interior ministry and brought that state’s singular experience of actually licensing online gambling, and Benjamin Schwanke, a lawyer who had led gambling supervision in Hamburg. The administrative board that appointed them sat, fittingly, in Halle, under the chairmanship of the host state’s representative; Saxony‑Anhalt thus presided over the authority’s infancy before the gavel began its rotation through the other fifteen capitals.

Beyond licensing and policing, the treaty loads the authority with quieter duties: it observes and measures the market, publishing annual activity reports and the yearly market report that the state supervisors had previously issued jointly; it is charged with promoting research into gambling and its harms, and with developing common standards — among them the systems by which operators are to recognise problem play early. In these capacities the GGL functions less as a policeman than as the standing secretariat of German gambling knowledge, a role no single Land authority could have filled.

The build‑up was a feat of administrative bootstrapping. Established on paper on 1 July 2021, the authority spent eighteen months acquiring premises, hiring staff and writing procedures while interim arrangements carried the load: the Land administrative office of Saxony‑Anhalt processed the new licences for virtual slots and online poker (the first of which issued only in 2022), the Regierungspräsidium Darmstadt continued to handle sports betting, and Hesse ran the central systems. The GGL’s own powers arrived on a statutory timetable: from 1 July 2022 it took over the combat against illegal gambling and unlawful advertising throughout Germany, and on 1 January 2023 the remaining licensing and supervisory competences passed to Halle, where the authority began full operations with some seventy‑five employees — a complement that has since grown to over a hundred. Its finances follow the federal habit: the Länder contribute according to the Königsteiner Schlüssel, the standard population‑and‑revenue formula for shared burdens, while fees levied on applicants and licensees, scaled to the size of their business, carry an increasing share of the cost.

Licensing and the whitelist

Obtaining a German licence is an exercise in documentary endurance. An applicant must demonstrate reliability, expertise and financial capacity; disclose its ownership to the last beneficial holder; lodge a security deposit, set by the treaty at no less than five million euros and adjustable upward towards the operator’s expected monthly turnover; present a social concept and a payment‑processing arrangement free of forbidden channels; and connect its technical systems to LUGAS, to OASIS and to the safe server before going live. Permits are granted for a limited term, never in perpetuity, and arrive festooned with ancillary provisions (Nebenbestimmungen) — the fine print by which the GGL fixes, for instance, the permissible mechanics of poker tables or the required conduct of identity checks. Breach of the provisions is ground for revocation, a sanction the authority has used against sports‑betting licensees that fell short of their obligations.

The public face of all this examination is the whitelist (Whitelist erlaubter Glücksspielanbieter), the official and continuously updated register of every operator, website and lottery broker permitted to offer gambling in Germany. Its logic inverts the older world: a German player need not parse Maltese licence numbers or terms‑of‑service legalese, but simply checks whether the site appears on the list — everything absent from it is, for German purposes, illegal. The list runs to several hundred entries across the verticals, and the authority has supplemented it with a seal that licensed operators may display. Banks, payment providers, advertising platforms and app stores consult the same register, which quietly turns a consumer‑information tool into an enforcement lever: a name struck from the whitelist finds doors closing in several markets at once.

The instruments of enforcement

Against the operators who never sought a licence — the websites run from Malta, Gibraltar or Curaçao that continue to court German custom — the GGL wields a graduated arsenal. It opens with prohibition orders backed by coercive penalty payments, addressed to the operator and, where the operator shrugs, to those who assist it. The scale of the task is considerable: in a single recent year the authority examined some 1,700 gambling portals and judged roughly half of them unlawful, tracing them back to a couple of hundred distinct operating groups.

The financial flank has proved the more defensible. The treaty empowers the authority to forbid banks and payment service providers from processing transactions connected with unauthorised gambling, and this payment blocking — choking the money rather than the website — survived judicial scrutiny: in October 2023 the Higher Administrative Court of Saxony‑Anhalt upheld the GGL’s practice, coercive penalties included. The technical flank fared worse, and its story is worth telling in full. The GGL’s chosen first target was the secondary‑lottery trade — websites that take bets on the outcomes of official lottery draws without ever buying a ticket, a product German law has never licensed and the state lottery companies particularly resent. Beginning in July 2022, within days of acquiring its enforcement powers, the authority opened proceedings to compel internet access providers to block such sites, and by the autumn had addressed orders to the five largest German providers. One provider resisted in court, and in early 2023 the Higher Administrative Court of Rhineland‑Palatinate in Koblenz held that the treaty offered no legal basis for blocking orders against mere access providers, who under German telemedia law are not responsible for the third‑party content they merely transmit. The Federal Administrative Court confirmed that reading in April 2024, closing the door for good. The authority has since redirected its blocking requests at host providers — who do bear responsibility for what they host — and reports that several hundred domains have been taken down or rendered inaccessible to German users by that route, while publicly wishing the treaty’s drafters would hand it an explicit network‑blocking power.

Advertising enforcement occupies a growing share of the docket: proceedings against affiliate sites that steer players to unlicensed casinos, against influencers streaming play on forbidden platforms, and against foreign streaming services carrying gambling promotion into Germany — in one such case the Higher Administrative Court of Saxony‑Anhalt sustained the GGL’s order against advertising delivered from abroad. The authority has also drawn the map’s contested borders, classifying daily fantasy sports as unauthorised gambling and ruling on the admissibility of payment methods. Offshore operators respond with geo‑blocking arguments — insisting that they do not target Germany, or that a German IP address proves little — and with the protection of their home jurisdictions: Malta’s Bill No. 55 of 2023 shields its licensees from the enforcement of foreign judgments that contradict Maltese gambling policy, a statute German courts and the European Commission have eyed with conspicuous coolness. Meanwhile a parallel front has opened in the civil courts, where German players have sued unlicensed operators for the return of their losses, frequently with success; the Federal Court of Justice signalled in 2024 that contracts with unlicensed operators are void, while referring aspects of the question to the Court of Justice of the European Union. The GGL itself rounds out its arsenal with a whistle‑blower system through which violations may be reported anonymously.

The taxman’s share

Alongside the treaty, and often confused with it, stands the fiscal regime. In 2021 the federation amended the old Rennwett‑ und Lotteriegesetz to tax the newly legalised games: virtual slot machines and online poker pay 5.3 per cent — not of the operator’s revenue, but of every stake wagered. The figure was no accident: it extended to the new games the turnover tax that sports betting had carried since 2012, when the federation first taxed the tolerated bookmakers at five per cent of stakes, a rate recalibrated to 5.3 in the 2021 reform so that all three online verticals now stand on the same fiscal footing. Taxation, it should be noted, is the one corner of this field the federation does control, gambling levies being federal law even where gambling itself is not — so the tax was debated in Berlin while the treaty was ratified in the sixteen state capitals, two legislative trains running on adjacent tracks. The distinction matters enormously in games of thin margins: a slot machine that returns 96 cents of every euro to players keeps four cents of margin, from which a 5.3‑cent turnover tax cannot be paid; German‑licensed operators have therefore lowered their payout ratios — commonly into the region of ninety‑four per cent or below — to fit the tax inside the game.

The industry’s objection writes itself: the unlicensed competitor pays no German tax, returns 96 to 98 cents on the euro, observes no €1 stake limit, and is one search away. Economists commissioned by the trade associations — most prominently in studies associated with Justus Haucap — predicted before the law passed that the turnover tax would hold channelisation in slots and poker below half, and the trade bodies have campaigned against the levy ever since, including by way of complaints to the European Commission. The treasuries reply that the tax yields several hundred million euros a year, that a stiff levy on a hazardous pastime is sound Pigouvian manners, and that a deliberately unattractive legal product is partly the point. The dispute is, at bottom, the channelisation argument in fiscal dress, and it remains unresolved.

Channelisation and the black market

The treaty’s own success criterion is channelisation: the share of German gambling that flows through licensed channels. On its measurement there is something close to open war. The GGL’s assessments are sanguine. Its market reports have put the unlicensed online market at a low single‑digit share of the total German gambling market — on the order of two to four per cent of turnover, or some €400–600 million in gross gaming revenue against a legal market of roughly thirteen to fourteen billion — while conceding that within the contested segment of online slots and poker the black market takes a far larger bite, on the order of a quarter. A survey‑based study the authority commissioned reached a compatible figure, finding about 77 per cent of online gambling volume on regulated sites.

The industry’s arithmetic is darker. A study from the University of Leipzig commissioned by the sports‑betting and online‑casino associations found, for early 2023, nearly half of interested players active on unlicensed portals and attributed the large majority of online gross revenue to the black market; the sports‑betting association estimates the illegal share of its vertical at one half; one international data house has put German online channelisation as low as roughly a third; and a comparative index assembled by Haucap and colleagues ranked Germany last among six European markets, far behind Denmark and Britain with their channelisation rates near or above ninety per cent. The gulf between the camps is partly honest method — whether one counts players, stakes or revenue; whether one trusts surveys, web traffic or payment data — and partly the familiar physics of motivated reasoning, since the industry’s figures argue for loosening the rules and the regulator’s for keeping them. The GGL, for its part, has promised to repeat its surveys at intervals, so that the quarrel may at least be conducted over a time series.

What the GGL does not regulate

For all its centrality, the GGL governs only a slice of German gambling, and not the largest. The terrestrial casinos (Spielbanken) remain creatures of the individual Länder, run by state companies or by concessionaires under state law, a few dozen houses in spa towns and city centres whose regulation has never left home. The commercial arcades (Spielhallen) and the gambling machines in pubs — by some measures the largest single segment of the German market — answer to federal trade law for the machines themselves and to increasingly austere Land statutes for the premises, with minimum distances between arcades and from schools that have thinned their numbers considerably. The sixteen state lottery companies, joined in the Deutscher Lotto‑ und Totoblock, keep their monopoly on the classic draw games, as do the joint class lotteries; the GGL touches lotteries only at the cross‑state margins, in the brokerage and the nationwide charitable lotteries. Horse‑race betting lives on under its 1922 statute.

The oddest gap is the one the treaty drew deliberately: online casino table games. A Land may grant licences for online roulette or blackjack, broadly in proportion to its land‑based casino concessions, or operate them itself, or do nothing — and most have so far done little, with only a handful of states having brought such offerings to market. The result is that a German player may legally spin a virtual slot anywhere in the federation but may join a legal online blackjack table only by grace of his particular Land — a residue of federalism that critics call the regime’s most obvious channelisation leak, since the table‑game customer who finds no legal offer knows exactly where to find an illegal one. The GGL acts in all these neighbouring domains only as a hub and clearing house; the Land authorities remain sovereign in them.

The European dimension

German gambling law has been argued in Luxembourg for as long as it has been argued in Karlsruhe. Gambling is a service within the meaning of the EU treaties, and restrictions on providing it across borders must be justified by overriding public interests and pursued coherently — the doctrine built across Schindler, Gambelli, Placanica, the German judgments of 2010, Digibet and Ince. For two decades offshore operators wielded that case law as both shield and sword, and with considerable effect, since the old monopoly genuinely was incoherent and the old licensing genuinely was illusory. The 2021 settlement was designed to end that game: the draft treaty was duly notified to the European Commission under the information‑procedure directive and emerged without a blocking opinion, and an operator who today declines to seek a German licence can no longer plead that none was obtainable. German courts have accordingly grown brisk with free‑movement defences, and operators based outside the Union altogether — Curaçao’s licensees above all — never had them to begin with.

The live European questions now run the other way: whether Malta’s statutory shield for its licensees against foreign judgments is compatible with the mutual recognition that EU civil justice presumes, a matter pressed upon the Commission from Germany and Austria; and how far the player‑ reimbursement jurisprudence of the German civil courts comports with Union law, on which questions have been carried to the Court of Justice. The GGL, for its part, has worked to knit itself into the fabric of European regulators’ cooperation, exchanging information with its counterparts — an unglamorous necessity, since its quarry is by construction extraterritorial.

Evaluation, criticism and reform

The treaty that created the GGL carries its own expiry mechanism: the Länder are obliged to evaluate its working, an exercise falling due by the end of 2026, and the treaty itself lapses at the close of 2028 unless prolonged — so that German gambling regulation, true to form, is already negotiating its next iteration while the present one is still bedding in. The evaluation is no formality: studies have been commissioned, the authority’s leadership has taken public positions at the academic symposia where such matters are aired, and researchers on both sides have warned, in their opposite ways, against letting the exercise be decided by whoever measures loudest. It arrives amid criticism from both flanks. Addiction researchers and public‑health voices argue that the opening of 2021 went too far: that advertising, though rationed, remains ubiquitous around televised sport; that the deposit limit is porous in practice; and that the toleration regime of 2020 set a precedent of policy by fait accompli. The industry argues the opposite: that the stake limit, the deposit cap, the five‑second spin, the advertising windows and above all the turnover tax make the legal product uncompetitive, and that every regulatory ounce added to the licensed offer is a gram of revenue gifted to Curaçao.

The GGL’s own reform appetite is more particular. It has asked, in effect, for sharper teeth: an explicit statutory basis for network blocking to repair what the courts struck down, smoother instruments against advertising platforms and payment intermediaries, and better cross‑border cooperation. Observers add structural questions to the docket — whether the online table‑game carve‑out should be folded into the common regime, whether the tax base should shift from stakes to revenue, and whether a young authority assembled at speed in Halle has yet the staffing to police a market this fluid. What is no longer seriously proposed, anywhere on the spectrum, is a return to the world before 2021: the patchwork of sixteen uncoordinated supervisors, the unenforceable prohibitions and the licensing rounds that licensed no one have few mourners. The GGL may be argued over; its existence, five years in, is the one settled point in an otherwise unsettled field.
