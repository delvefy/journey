Saladin

Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub, 1137 or 1138 – 4 March 1193, sultan of Egypt and Syria, founder of the Ayyubid dynasty

Yusuf, the son of Ayyub, was born in 1137 or 1138 at Tikrit on the upper Tigris, where his father Najm ad-Din Ayyub, a Kurdish soldier from the Hadhabani tribe of the mountains north of Mosul, held the citadel for the Seljuk governor. The family was Sunni and Shafi’i, ambitious without being grand, and Kurdish in a Turkic and Arab military world. Within a year of the future sultan’s birth the family fell out with the governor of Tikrit and migrated south to Mosul under the protection of Imad ad-Din Zengi, the great atabeg whose campaigns were unifying upper Mesopotamia and northern Syria. Yusuf grew up at Damascus from about 1146, when his father transferred to the service of Zengi’s son Nur ad-Din; he was educated as a young gentleman in the Arabic and Persian classics, in horsemanship, in the Qur’an and the law, and entered the household troops of the prince at about the age of fifteen. The early sources, including the friendly biography by his secretary Baha ad-Din ibn Shaddad, agree that he was a modest, devout, slightly bookish boy, more interested in religious learning and polo than in war, and that the family destined him for an administrator’s career rather than a soldier’s.

The Egyptian expeditions of his uncle Shirkuh changed his life. From 1163 the Fatimid caliphate in Cairo, riven between its viziers, became the object of competing interventions by Nur ad-Din from Syria and by King Amalric of Jerusalem from the south. Shirkuh, Nur ad-Din’s ablest general, was sent three times into Egypt between 1163 and 1169; on the third expedition the young Yusuf went with him as second in command, evidently reluctantly. The Frankish army was driven out; Shirkuh entered Cairo in January 1169 and was named vizier of the caliph al-Adid. He died of overeating two months later, on 22 March 1169. The Fatimid court, assuming that the young Kurdish nephew would be more pliant than any of the senior amirs, appointed Yusuf vizier in his place. He was thirty-one and almost unknown.

He took the office and never let go of it. Over the next two years he replaced the Fatimid palace troops with his own Syrian and Kurdish soldiers, suppressed a Sudanese mutiny in the streets of Cairo, distributed the great Egyptian fiefs to his brothers and to the lieutenants he could most rely on, and on 10 September 1171, in the dying month of the caliph al-Adid, ordered the Friday sermon at the mosque of Amr to be read in the name of the Abbasid caliph of Baghdad. The Fatimid caliphate, two centuries old, ended without resistance. Yusuf, now openly the master of Egypt under the Sunni allegiance, became from this moment the sultan Salah ad-Din, the Westerner’s Saladin. Nur ad-Din, in Damascus, watched his lieutenant’s rise with mounting suspicion; the two men were on the point of war when Nur ad-Din died of angina on 15 May 1174. The death cleared the field. Saladin marched on Damascus that autumn, was admitted by the population without a fight, and by 1183, after a decade of campaigns against the Zengid heirs and the various Muslim emirates of the Jazira, controlled Egypt, southern Syria, Aleppo, and the upper Tigris — the whole encirclement of the Latin states of the Syrian coast.

The frontier with the kingdom of Jerusalem was a chronic, low-intensity war through these same years. The leper king Baldwin IV, a courageous adolescent dying slowly of his disease, defeated Saladin at Mont Gisard in November 1177 in the most humiliating reverse of the sultan’s career; Saladin escaped to Egypt with a few horsemen. The lesson was that the Frankish field army, once committed, could not be destroyed piecemeal. He prepared instead for a decisive battle. The provocation he wanted was given by Reynald of Châtillon, the Latin lord of Kerak, who in the winter of 1186–1187 attacked a Muslim caravan in defiance of the existing truce; the king who succeeded Baldwin, Guy of Lusignan, married to Sibylla, the dead king’s sister, refused to compensate. In the summer of 1187 Saladin invaded with the full army of Egypt and Syria, drew the kingdom’s field force into the dry hills of Galilee, and on 4 July 1187, at the Horns of Hattin above the Sea of Galilee, surrounded and destroyed it. The True Cross was taken; Guy was captured; Reynald, presented at Saladin’s tent that evening, was beheaded by the sultan personally for his violation of the truce.

The kingdom collapsed almost overnight. Acre, Sidon, Beirut, Caesarea, Jaffa, and Ascalon surrendered through July and August; on 2 October 1187 Jerusalem itself, defended by Balian of Ibelin with a handful of knights and the city’s civilian inhabitants, capitulated on terms that allowed most of the Christian population to leave. Saladin entered the city without sack or massacre, in conscious contrast to the conduct of the First Crusade in 1099. Tyre alone of the great coastal cities held out, defended by Conrad of Montferrat, and would become the bridgehead of the recovery.

The news of the fall of Jerusalem reached Europe within weeks. The Third Crusade gathered the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa, King Philip II of France, and King Richard I of England; Frederick drowned in a Cilician river in 1190; Philip went home after the siege of Acre in 1191; Richard alone of the great princes prosecuted the war into 1191 and 1192. The two-year duel between Richard and Saladin became the defining narrative of the late twelfth century in both Christian and Muslim memory. Richard won the open-field engagements — Arsuf in September 1191, Jaffa in August 1192 — but could not press his line of communications inland to Jerusalem; Saladin, although unable to defeat him in battle, kept the army of the kingdom intact and held the hill country. The Treaty of Jaffa in September 1192 left Saladin in possession of the interior and the holy city, the Latins in possession of the coast from Tyre to Jaffa, and pilgrim access to Jerusalem open.

Saladin returned to Damascus in November 1192. He died there on 4 March 1193, at the age of about fifty-five, of a fever that overtook him within a fortnight of his return. He left, in the words of his secretary, one dinar and forty-seven dirhams in coin and a great many books; the funeral arrangements were paid for by the courtesy of his brother al-Adil, who succeeded him in Egypt and brought together, in the next decade, the divided inheritance the sultan had left to his quarrelling sons. Saladin’s tomb stands today in a small mausoleum beside the Umayyad mosque in Damascus.
