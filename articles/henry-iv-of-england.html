Henry IV of England

1367 – 1413, Henry Bolingbroke, the first Lancastrian king, usurper of Richard II

Henry of Bolingbroke was born at Bolingbroke Castle in Lincolnshire in April 1367, the eldest surviving son of John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, by his first wife Blanche of Lancaster. He was three months younger than his cousin Richard, son of the Black Prince, born at Bordeaux the same winter; the two boys were brought up together at the English court of their grandfather Edward III. When in 1377 Edward III died, the ten-year-old Richard was crowned king and the ten-year-old Bolingbroke was created Earl of Derby, an event that for the rest of their lives held them in a particular pattern: cousins of identical age, the king and the senior royal duke's heir, joined in childhood and, in the end, in the destruction of the senior line of the dynasty.

His mother Blanche died of the plague when he was a year old. His father's second marriage to Constance of Castile and the long affair with Katherine Swynford which became his third marriage gave Henry several half-siblings, including the Beaufort line: John, the first Earl of Somerset, Henry, the future Cardinal Beaufort, Thomas, and Joan, who together with their descendants would supply the Tudor royal house. Henry himself married Mary de Bohun, coheiress of the great Hereford and Northampton estates of the de Bohun family, in 1380 or 1381, when he was thirteen. The marriage produced six children, of whom the first, born at Monmouth in 1386, was the future Henry V.

His first political role, at twenty-one, was as one of the five Lords Appellant who in 1387 to 1388 overthrew Richard II's favourites and reduced the king to a formal regency. The Appellants — Thomas of Woodstock, Duke of Gloucester, the king's youngest uncle; Bolingbroke and his close ally Thomas Mowbray; the earls of Arundel and Warwick — defeated the king's army under Robert de Vere at Radcot Bridge in December 1387, and in the "Merciless Parliament" of February 1388 condemned to death or exile most of the king's friends. Richard never forgot the episode and never forgave the participants; the reckoning, when it came in the summer of 1397, fell upon all five, but Bolingbroke and Mowbray were drawn aside into a private quarrel.

Mowbray, by Henry's later sworn testimony, accused Richard at a meeting between them on the road of plotting to destroy them as he had destroyed Gloucester, Arundel, and Warwick the same summer. Henry reported the conversation to the king. Mowbray denied it. The matter was referred to a trial by combat at Coventry on 16 September 1398; the king, with the lists already set up and the champions in the saddle, intervened at the last moment and ordered both into exile, Mowbray for life and Bolingbroke for ten years. Henry travelled to the French court at Paris, where he was received with honour.

The fatal turn came four months later. On 3 February 1399 John of Gaunt died at Leicester. The enormous Lancastrian inheritance — the largest single estate in England outside the royal demesne — should have descended to the exiled Henry. Richard, in a decision that broke the hereditary principle on which his magnates' world rested, declared the exile permanent and confiscated the lands to the crown. Within five months, while Richard was campaigning in Ireland, Henry landed at Ravenspur in Yorkshire in July 1399 with a small force, declaring he had come only to claim his inheritance, and was joined by the great northern lords. By the time Richard returned, his support had melted away. He surrendered to Henry at Flint in August. On 29 September 1399, in proceedings whose constitutional form was deliberately ambiguous, Richard signed a deed of abdication; on 30 September parliament confirmed the deposition; Henry was crowned at Westminster on 13 October 1399 as Henry IV. Richard was sent north to Pontefract Castle and died there in February 1400, almost certainly starved on Henry's order.

His reign was unstable from its first months. In January 1400 the so-called Epiphany Rising, a conspiracy of Ricardian earls to assassinate the new king at Windsor, was betrayed and crushed; the participants — Salisbury, Huntingdon, Kent, Despenser — were lynched by mobs at Cirencester, Pleshey, and Bristol. In Wales Owain Glyndŵr proclaimed himself prince in September 1400 and led a national revolt that ran for fifteen years, allied at intervals with the great northern family of the Percys. The Percys, who had brought Henry to power in 1399, broke with him over the unpaid costs of Scottish border service and over the king's refusal to ransom Mortimer prisoners; Sir Henry Percy, called Hotspur, rebelled in 1403 and was killed at Shrewsbury on 21 July of that year, where the king's heir, the sixteen-year-old future Henry V, took the famous arrow in the face. The Percy patriarch, the Earl of Northumberland, rebelled again in 1405 with Archbishop Scrope of York; the rebellion was broken at Shipton Moor and the archbishop executed in his vestments at the king's order — an unprecedented act that contemporaries took for the cause of the leprous skin disease that began to disfigure the king from that summer onward.

The latter half of his reign was governed in worsening health. From 1406 he was subject to fits of paralysis or seizure, his face disfigured, repeatedly bedridden, sometimes thought dying. His son Prince Henry, in close alliance with his half-brother the future Cardinal Beaufort, increasingly took over the council; quarrelled with his father in 1411 to 1412; was readmitted to favour only in the last weeks. The Welsh revolt, slowly squeezed by the prince's campaigns out of Anglesey and Brecon, was effectively ended by 1409. The treasury was empty. Henry IV had governed England, against repeated revolt, for thirteen and a half years, and had handed the dynasty intact to his son.

He died in the Jerusalem Chamber at Westminster Abbey on 20 March 1413, aged forty-five. The episode is recorded by the chronicler Adam of Usk — whether truly or as fitting moral — with a long-standing prophecy that he would die in Jerusalem; in extremity he had asked the name of the chamber and, on being told, gave thanks that the prophecy was fulfilled and that "in this chamber shall I die." He was buried at Canterbury, the only English king of the late Middle Ages not buried at Westminster, by his own choice as a gesture toward the saint who had been his patron in life. His son Henry V succeeded him without contest.

Henry IV had, as a politician, no programme beyond the maintenance of his line; in everything but the maintenance of his line, by all accounts, he succeeded only at great cost. The hereditary illegitimacy of the Lancastrian title — admitted in private by his own son, and underwritten only by the parliamentary act of 1399 — would be revived sixty years later by the Yorkist claim, when his grandson Henry VI lost the throne. The Lancastrian dynasty lasted three reigns; the Wars of the Roses came directly from the constitutional question Henry IV had opened.
