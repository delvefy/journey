Joan of Arc

c. 1412 – 1431, peasant girl of Lorraine, liberator of Orléans, who put Charles VII on the throne and was burned at Rouen at nineteen

Joan, called in her lifetime Jeanne la Pucelle or simply La Pucelle, the Maid, was born about 1412 at Domrémy on the river Meuse, a village on the frontier between the duchy of Bar and the kingdom of France, in country loyal to the Armagnac party of the disinherited Dauphin. Her father Jacques d'Arc was a substantial peasant farmer, a parish official with some thirty hectares of land; her mother Isabelle Romée was, according to her own later testimony, an unusually pious countrywoman whose surname suggests a pilgrimage to Rome. Joan was the youngest but one of five children. She was never sent to school, never learned to read or write more than her own name, and worked in the household and the fields. The village had been raided more than once by Burgundian or English-leaning soldiers, and she remembered the church bells of her childhood as a warning system.

From the age of about thirteen, she said, she began to hear voices, accompanied at first by a bright light: the voices, she identified after a time, were of the Archangel Michael, of St Catherine of Alexandria, and of St Margaret of Antioch. The voices told her, with increasing insistence over three years, that she was to ride to the Dauphin Charles, raise the siege of Orléans, and lead him to be crowned at Reims. In May 1428 she went to the small town of Vaucouleurs to ask its captain, Robert de Baudricourt, for an escort to the king at Chinon. He laughed at her. She returned in January 1429 and pressed him again. This time, with the situation of the kingdom desperate — Orléans had been under English siege since October — he gave way, organised an escort of six men, and sent her dressed in male clothes for safety on a five-hundred-kilometre ride through hostile Burgundian country. She arrived at Chinon on 23 February 1429.

Charles VII tested her by hiding among his courtiers; she walked through the assembled crowd and identified him. He gave her a private audience that lasted, she said later, until she had told him "his secret," a phrase that has been argued over by every biographer for six centuries. He sent her to Poitiers, where for three weeks she was examined by an ecclesiastical commission for orthodoxy and signs of demonic origin; the commission found nothing against her and recommended that the king accept her offer. He gave her a household, a standard painted with the names of Jesus and Mary, a suit of white armour, and the relief expedition for Orléans.

The relief of Orléans in late April and early May 1429 was decisive. The town had been under English-Burgundian siege for over six months and was on the point of negotiating surrender. Joan crossed the Loire with supplies and reinforcements, joined the city's garrison under Jean Dunois, called the Bastard of Orléans, and over the next nine days drove the English from the bastilles around the city in three set assaults; she was wounded above the breast by a crossbow bolt at the bastille of Les Augustins on 7 May and returned to the field the same evening. The English raised the siege on 8 May. The Loire campaign that followed cleared Meung, Jargeau, and Beaugency, and on 18 June at Patay a French force led nominally by the Duke of Alençon and effectively by Joan and La Hire destroyed an English column under Talbot in open field. The English commander Sir John Fastolf escaped; the situation of three centuries was reversed in a fortnight.

Joan led the king's army across hostile territory to Reims, taking the towns of the Champagne by their fear rather than by siege, and on 17 July 1429 she stood beside the altar of Reims cathedral, holding her white standard, while Charles VII was anointed and crowned king of France. The propaganda effect was vast. The Burgundian alliance with England, already strained, began to be questioned in Flanders and Brabant. Castile, Scotland, the cities of the Empire, the Italian states began to receive the news of the Maid with bewilderment and hope.

Her fortunes turned with the failure to take Paris in September 1429. She was wounded in the thigh by a crossbow bolt outside the Porte Saint-Honoré; the king, listening to his Burgundian peace negotiators, ordered the withdrawal. The next six months were spent in small operations on the northern frontier. On 23 May 1430, while leading a sortie from the besieged town of Compiègne, she was unhorsed by Burgundian troops of the Count of Luxembourg and taken prisoner. The king made no recorded effort to ransom her. After six months of captivity she was sold to the English for ten thousand livres tournois in November and taken to the English headquarters at Rouen.

Her trial began on 9 January 1431 in the chapel of the archbishop's palace at Rouen under the Bishop of Beauvais, Pierre Cauchon, who had been driven from his see by her victories and who acted in this trial as the English client he was. The charges were of heresy, witchcraft, and idolatry, focusing on her claim of direct revelation, on the male clothing she had assumed first for travel and continued to wear in prison for protection, and on her refusal to submit her revelations to the judgement of the church on earth. She was held in a secular military prison, not the ecclesiastical one to which she was entitled, and was watched by English guards day and night. The interrogations are preserved in their Latin record and in a French minute; her answers, against an examining theological commission of more than seventy clerics, were extraordinarily clear and quick. Asked whether she was in a state of grace — a theological trap — she answered, "If I am not, may God put me there; if I am, may God keep me there." The court found her guilty on 24 May; she signed an abjuration, was returned to prison, resumed male dress (because, she said, the soldiers had stripped away her women's clothes), was declared a relapsed heretic, and was burned in the marketplace of the Vieux-Marché at Rouen on 30 May 1431. Her ashes were scattered in the Seine.

Her death did not stop her campaign. Bedford lost the alliance with Burgundy at the Treaty of Arras four years later; Paris fell to the French in 1436; the Hundred Years War was over by 1453. In 1455 the new pope, Calixtus III, at the request of Joan's mother Isabelle Romée and of Charles VII, opened a nullification trial. The hearings ran for a year through Paris, Rouen, Orléans, and Domrémy; over a hundred witnesses, including her childhood companions, the soldiers who had ridden with her, and the surviving members of the Poitiers commission, were heard. On 7 July 1456 the original sentence was declared null and Joan's name was formally cleared.

She passed out of immediate memory and back into it. In the eighteenth century Voltaire mocked her in La Pucelle; Schiller and Mark Twain rehabilitated her in literature; the French Third Republic took her up as a national symbol; the Catholic Church beatified her in 1909 and canonised her in 1920. She remained one of the most fully documented persons of the fifteenth century, her trial and rehabilitation transcripts preserving the speech of an illiterate teenage girl against the most learned doctors of theology of her age. She had ridden into the history of the kingdom for two years and turned a lost war. The court that condemned her had been the agent of the side that lost; the verdict of the war was hers.
