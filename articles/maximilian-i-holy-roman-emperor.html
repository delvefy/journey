Maximilian I, Holy Roman Emperor

1459 – 1519, the “last knight,” husband of Mary of Burgundy, founder of Habsburg world power by marriage and inheritance

Maximilian was born at Wiener Neustadt on 22 March 1459, the eldest surviving son of Emperor Frederick III and Eleanor of Portugal. His father, the slowest and most patient of the Habsburgs, was nominally emperor for more than fifty years over a Reich that scarcely obeyed him, and during Maximilian's boyhood the dynasty's prospects looked thin. The decisive event came when he was eighteen. In January 1477 Charles the Bold of Burgundy, the great rival of Louis XI of France, was killed in the snow outside Nancy, leaving his only child Mary as heiress of a sprawling complex of duchies, counties, and lordships that ran from the Somme to the Zuiderzee and included most of what is now Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, and Franche-Comté. Louis XI invaded at once; the Burgundian estates, looking for a protector, accepted the long-standing engagement of Mary to Frederick III's son. Maximilian rode north and married her at Ghent on 19 August 1477.

The Burgundian marriage was the founding act of Habsburg power. Mary brought him the wealthiest and most urbanised territory in northern Europe; he brought her an army that, at Guinegate in August 1479, beat off the French and saved most of the inheritance, with the loss of the duchy of Burgundy itself. The marriage was, by all surviving accounts, also a love-match. Mary bore him a son Philip in 1478 and a daughter Margaret in 1480, then died at twenty-five in March 1482 from injuries after a fall from her horse hunting near Bruges. Maximilian, then twenty-three, was made guardian of his son and never remarried for love. He would in old age dictate to his secretaries the long autobiographical fictions Theuerdank and Weisskunig in which Mary's death is the central wound.

The Burgundian estates resisted his regency. The towns of Flanders held him prisoner briefly at Bruges in 1488; only the slow imperial relief army secured his release. The Netherlands would not be wholly pacified for a generation. Meanwhile his father pushed him forward in imperial politics. He was crowned King of the Romans at Aachen in 1486, the imperial heir designate; on Frederick's death in 1493 he became sole emperor in name. He never received the papal coronation, the long Italian wars making the journey to Rome impossible; in 1508 at Trent, with papal acquiescence, he assumed the new title of Erwählter Römischer Kaiser, Elected Roman Emperor, which all his successors used until the office ended in 1806.

His domestic project was the reform of the empire. The Diet of Worms in 1495, called by him at the urging of the elector of Mainz Berthold von Henneberg, attempted to convert the loose federation of imperial princes and free cities into a working state. It legislated a Common Penny tax, a Public Peace prohibiting feud, an Imperial Chamber Court (the Reichskammergericht) for adjudicating princely disputes, and an Imperial Governing Council. Maximilian got most of the framework and almost none of the money. The Common Penny was rejected by the estates; the Chamber Court was set up but lay outside the emperor's direct authority. For the rest of his reign he had to finance his wars by his own hereditary revenues, by loans from the Fugger of Augsburg, and by the silver of Tyrol. He developed in compensation an extraordinary administration in his Austrian and Tyrolean lands and at his court at Innsbruck, with a chancellery, an artillery park, a postal service operated by the Tassis family that survived for centuries, and a registered fiscal system that was the most modern in Germany.

In Italy he was almost always at war and almost always defeated. He invaded Lombardy in 1496 to expel the French and failed; he tried to make himself master of Venice in the League of Cambrai of 1508 and failed; he made and unmade alliances with Charles VIII, Louis XII, and Francis I, with Ludovico Sforza (his nephew-in-law after his second marriage in 1494 to Bianca Maria Sforza), with the Swiss, the pope, and the king of Naples. His Italian policy cost him enormously in money and prestige; what it produced was the imperial investiture of Ludovico Sforza in 1495 and a number of dynastic links that bore fruit later.

His statecraft was, in the end, conducted not on the battlefield but in the marriage contracts. He betrothed his son Philip the Handsome to Joanna of Castile, daughter of Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, in 1496; from that marriage descended the Spanish Habsburgs, Charles V, Philip II, and the rest. He betrothed his daughter Margaret first to the Dauphin of France and then to the heir of Castile (her two husbands dying young), and used her in his old age as governor of the Netherlands. By the Treaty of Vienna in 1515 he arranged the double marriage of his grandchildren Ferdinand and Mary with the heirs of Hungary and Bohemia; in 1526, after his death, the last Jagiellon king of Hungary fell at Mohács and his grandson Ferdinand inherited the Hungarian and Bohemian crowns. The Latin tag attributed to him, Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube — let others wage war, you, happy Austria, marry — captures the strategy without quite doing it justice; he had waged plenty of wars too, and lost most of them.

He was an extraordinary patron of the arts, principally of the German Renaissance painters and printmakers who were his contemporaries. He commissioned from Albrecht Dürer the three woodcut series — the Triumphal Arch, the Triumphal Procession, and the Great Triumphal Chariot — that survive as the most ambitious imperial propaganda of the age. He gathered an enormous collection of armour, books, and tapestries, and ordered for his projected tomb in Innsbruck twenty-eight oversized bronze ancestors and ancestral models, of which Dürer designed several and Peter Vischer cast some of the masterpieces — the so-called Schwarze Mander, "Black Men," that still stand in the Hofkirche around an empty cenotaph. He was buried, in the end, at Wiener Neustadt where he had been born, beneath the altar of his castle chapel.

He died at Wels in Upper Austria on 12 January 1519. The imperial election that followed brought his grandson Charles V to the throne and inaugurated the first universal Habsburg monarchy. The empire Maximilian had failed to reform survived, the Burgundian inheritance survived, the Spanish marriage paid off; the dynastic structure on which the Habsburgs would rest for the next four hundred years was wholly his work. The men of his own time called him the last knight, partly in irony, partly in respect. Posterity has been kinder, having seen what came after.
