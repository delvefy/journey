Zengi

Imad ad-Din Zengi ibn Aq Sunqur, c. 1085 – 14 September 1146, atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo, conqueror of Edessa

Zengi was born about 1085, the son of the Turkic emir Aq Sunqur al-Hajib, governor of Aleppo for the Great Seljuk sultan Malik Shah. His father, an able and severe administrator of the Iraqi-Turkic school that had been bequeathed by Malik Shah’s minister Nizam al-Mulk, was put to death in 1094 by Malik Shah’s brother Tutush for refusing to break with the senior Seljuk house in the war of succession. Zengi was perhaps nine years old; he was taken in by another of his father’s associates, Kerbogha, the future commander of Mosul, and brought up in the great Turkic military households of the Jazira and Iraq. He grew up among soldiers, learned the language of the Persian administration and the protocols of the Abbasid court, and by his late twenties was one of the most useful field officers of the second-generation Seljuk armies. He served at the long siege of Tikrit in 1113, in the campaigns of the sultan Muhammad against the Crusader principalities, and in the wars between the rival Seljuk sultans of Iraq and Persia. In 1122 he was appointed governor of Wasit and Basra; in 1126 he won the favour of the boy sultan Mahmud II of the Iraqi Seljuks by leading his army to victory at the Battle of al-Saba against the caliph al-Mustarshid; in September 1127, in reward, he received the appointment of atabeg of Mosul, the principal Seljuk command in the upper Jazira and the springboard from which his career took its real shape.

He was about forty-two and arriving, as he meant to, on a frontier that was politically open. The Crusader states had been entrenched on the Syrian coast for thirty years; the petty Muslim principalities of the interior, Aleppo, Damascus, Homs, Hama, Shaizar, Mardin, Hisn Kayfa, were divided among Turkic dynasties of the second rank, none capable of resistance to a Frankish field army on its own. Zengi’s programme, repeated in formal proclamations of his own and elaborated by the chroniclers after his death, was the simple one of bringing all of Muslim Syria and the Jazira under his hand as the precondition of any war against the Latins. He executed it with patience over twenty years. In June 1128 he occupied Aleppo by the connivance of a faction within its citadel. Hama fell to him in 1129, Homs in 1138, the small lordships of the Jazira one after another through the 1130s. He failed twice to take Damascus, in 1135 and again in 1138; he failed before the great Crusader castle of Montferrand in 1137, although on that occasion he took the Latin king Fulk of Jerusalem inside the fortress and extracted from him a humiliating treaty before letting the king depart. He played the Seljuk sultans of Baghdad and Persia and the Caliph at Baghdad against each other with such success that no Muslim sovereign was ever again strong enough to recall him. By 1140 he was the dominant power between the Tigris and the coast.

The campaign against Edessa was the consequence of a quarrel he had picked, with characteristic foresight, with the Artuqid lord of Mardin. The Latin count of Edessa, Joscelin II, had marched out of his city in October 1144 to support the Artuqid — an obscure ally, but the only one he had — against Zengi; he left the capital almost ungarrisoned, since the Latin army of Edessa was small even when concentrated. Zengi turned his column off the Artuqid line of march, swung north-east, and on 28 November 1144 appeared with his full army before the walls of the city. The Christian population of the city, made up of Armenians and Syriacs as well as a small Latin garrison, attempted a defence of about a month; on the night of 23–24 December 1144, while the Latin clergy of the city were attending a Christmas Eve service in the cathedral, Zengi’s sappers breached the wall by the gate of the Hours and the army stormed in. Joscelin II, riding hard from Tell Bashir, arrived too late. The Latin inhabitants were massacred or sold into slavery; the Christian Armenians and Syriacs, less politically dangerous in Zengi’s view, were spared and confirmed in their property. He had taken the first of the four Crusader states. It was the first major reversal of the conquests of 1098 and the event that, in the following spring, prompted Pope Eugenius III to call and Bernard of Clairvaux to preach the Second Crusade.

The Second Crusade itself was still two years in the future when Zengi was murdered. In the late summer of 1146, having marched up the Euphrates to subdue the small rebellious garrison of Qalat Jabar — a tower on a high spur over the river held by an Arab amir who had refused his summons — the atabeg laid siege to the fortress with the bulk of his army. On the night of 14 September 1146, after a long evening of drinking, he was murdered in his own tent by one of the eunuch slaves of his household, a Frank by birth named in some sources as Yarankash, whom the atabeg had threatened the previous day with execution for having sipped from his cup. The killer escaped to Qalat Jabar; the army dispersed in shock; the various heirs took the parts of the empire they could hold. The eastern half, Mosul, passed to his eldest son Saif ad-Din Ghazi; the western half, Aleppo and Syria, to his second son Nur ad-Din Mahmud. The two halves would never quite be reunited under a single Zengid hand, but the political project Zengi had bequeathed — the unification of Muslim Syria as the precondition of any general war against the Latin states — was carried through by Nur ad-Din in his own generation and by Saladin in the next.

His reputation, in the Arabic tradition, was always more that of a fierce and disciplined commander than of a saint or a statesman. Ibn al-Athir, whose family served the Zengids and whose loyalty was personal, wrote that he was harsh with his troops, just to the poor, and pitiless to anyone who broke the order he was determined to impose. The chroniclers’ commonplace was that under his command no merchant or peasant from Mosul to Aleppo dared to be robbed: a slightly stylised compliment, but one with the kernel of an accurate political memory. He was the first of the great twelfth-century Sunni commanders to set the project of the recovery of Jerusalem in motion.
