Richard II of England

1367 – 1400, last Plantagenet of the senior line, deposed by his cousin Henry Bolingbroke

Richard was born at the Abbey of St André at Bordeaux on 6 January 1367, the second son of Edward the Black Prince and Joan of Kent. The first son, also Edward, died at six in 1371; from that year the boy Richard was the heir presumptive to the Plantagenet crown. His grandfather Edward III was already in his ageing decade; his father, broken by dysentery contracted in Spain in 1367, died in June 1376. The ten-year-old prince was created Prince of Wales at his father's death and a year later, on 22 June 1377, succeeded his grandfather as King of England. He was crowned at Westminster on 16 July, a small fair-haired boy at the centre of the most elaborate coronation ritual the abbey had yet seen.

His minority was governed by a series of councils dominated by his uncles, principally John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, and to a lesser extent by Edmund of Langley, Duke of York, and Thomas of Woodstock, the youngest. The first political event of his own life was the Peasants' Revolt of June 1381, when an army of forty thousand men from Kent and Essex marched on London demanding the abolition of serfdom and the heads of the king's councillors. The fourteen-year-old king rode out to meet them at Smithfield on 15 June after the rebels' leader Wat Tyler had been struck down by the mayor; he is recorded by every contemporary chronicler as having faced the army alone, declared "I will be your captain", and led them out of London. The rising collapsed; the promises of amnesty were revoked in the following months; the king's bearing on the day passed into the heroic legend of his reign that, in retrospect, his enemies preferred to forget.

He had married in January 1382 the fifteen-year-old Anne of Bohemia, daughter of the Emperor Charles IV. The marriage was childless and, by the testimony of those near them, unusually affectionate; her death from plague at Sheen in June 1394 broke him, and the manor of Sheen at his order was razed to the ground. His political style in the 1380s was already drawing him into conflict with the older nobility. He surrounded himself with a small circle of personal favourites — Robert de Vere, Earl of Oxford, and Michael de la Pole, Earl of Suffolk, the most prominent — promoted them above their rank, conducted business through them rather than through the council, and quarrelled with his uncle Gloucester and with his cousin Henry Bolingbroke, son of Gaunt.

The first crisis came in 1386 to 1388. The "Wonderful Parliament" of October 1386 impeached Suffolk and forced the king to accept a commission of government for one year. The king declared the commission illegal; in December 1387 the so-called Lords Appellant — Gloucester, Bolingbroke, Thomas of Mowbray, the Earl of Warwick, and the Earl of Arundel — defeated the king's army under De Vere at Radcot Bridge; the "Merciless Parliament" of February 1388 condemned to death or exile most of the king's friends. The king himself, twenty-one years old, survived politically; his uncle Gaunt returned from Spain in 1389 and brokered an uneasy reconciliation. The king worked outwardly within the constitutional framework through the early 1390s and made peace with France by a twenty-eight-year truce in 1396, sealed by his second marriage to the seven-year-old Isabella of Valois, daughter of Charles VI.

In the summer of 1397 he struck back. Gloucester was arrested and taken to Calais, where he died, almost certainly murdered, in mysterious circumstances in September; Arundel was beheaded; Warwick was exiled to the Isle of Man. The other two Appellants, Bolingbroke and Mowbray, were drawn into a private quarrel that the king referred to trial by combat at Coventry in September 1398, and at the last moment ordered both into exile — Mowbray for life, Bolingbroke for ten years. The years 1397 to 1399 are what historians call Richard's "tyranny": rule by the king's personal will, with parliament packed and his enemies stripped, with circuits of his own retainers raised on a private livery as a household army.

The fatal misjudgement came in February 1399, when his uncle Gaunt died at Leicester. The Lancastrian inheritance, with which Gaunt had been the wealthiest subject in England, should have descended to the exiled Bolingbroke. Richard declared the exile permanent and confiscated the lands to the crown. The decision broke the principle of hereditary inheritance on which the magnates' world rested. Within five months Bolingbroke had landed at Ravenspur in Yorkshire with a small force, declaring he had come only to claim his inheritance, and was joined by every Lancastrian retainer in the north. Richard, who had been campaigning in Ireland, returned too late, was abandoned by his army on the march down from Conwy, and surrendered to Bolingbroke at Flint Castle in August.

He was taken to London, lodged in the Tower, and on 29 September 1399, in proceedings whose constitutional form contemporaries argued over and historians still argue over, signed a deed of abdication. The next day parliament confirmed the deposition, on a list of thirty-three charges, and accepted Bolingbroke as King Henry IV. The deposed king was moved north to Pontefract Castle in Yorkshire. He died there about 14 February 1400, almost certainly starved to death on Henry's order. He was thirty-three. His body was exhibited at St Paul's so that the country could be certain he was dead, then buried at the Dominican priory of King's Langley; Henry V, his cousin once removed, in an act of conscience reburied him beside Anne of Bohemia in Westminster Abbey in 1413.

He left no legitimate issue. The senior Plantagenet line in the male line was extinct with him. The Lancastrian house that supplanted him was descended from Edward III's third son; the Yorkist house that supplanted the Lancastrians was descended through the female line from the second; the dynastic argument was already there in his deposition, and its working-out provided the cause and the candidates of the Wars of the Roses.

Richard's reign was, in cultural terms, a major one: Geoffrey Chaucer, John Gower, the Pearl-Poet, the painter who made the Wilton Diptych, the rebuilding of Westminster Hall with its great oak hammerbeam roof, the cultivation of the white hart as the king's badge, all belong to it. As an artistic patron he stands at the head of late medieval English court culture. As a political king he was thin-skinned, autocratic, capable, and finally undone by a misjudgement made against the strongest of his cousins. Shakespeare's Richard II, written two centuries later under Elizabeth I, set the deposition scene at the centre of the early modern English imagination of monarchy and its limits.
