John the Fearless

1371 – 1419, Duke of Burgundy, assassin of Louis of Orléans, killed himself on the bridge of Montereau

John was born at Dijon on 28 May 1371, the eldest son of Philip the Bold of Burgundy, youngest son of John II of France, and of Margaret III of Flanders, the rich heiress through whom the Valois Burgundians acquired the county of Flanders and the great Flemish towns. His youth, as Count of Nevers, was passed in the court of his uncle Charles V and his cousin Charles VI at Paris, where political power was contested between his father, the king's brother Louis, Duke of Anjou, and a shifting cast of royal uncles and cousins. The mental collapse of Charles VI in 1392, which would recur intermittently until his death in 1422, opened the long power vacuum that would govern John's career.

His first claim to fame was the disastrous crusade of Nicopolis in 1396. King Sigismund of Hungary had appealed to the West for help against the Ottoman advance into the Balkans, and Philip the Bold dispatched his twenty-four-year-old son with an expensive Burgundian-French contingent. On 25 September 1396 the crusader army met Sultan Bayezid I before the Bulgarian town of Nicopolis. The Burgundian and French knights charged without waiting for the Hungarian centre, broke the Ottoman front line, and rode straight onto the prepared positions of the janissaries; they were destroyed. John was captured, held with several other lords for almost a year, and ransomed for an enormous sum negotiated by his father. The Burgundian propagandists made of his conduct on the field a story of personal courage and gave him, on his return, the surname Sans Peur, "Without Fear," that he retained.

His father died in April 1404, leaving him the duchies of Burgundy, Brabant, and Limburg, the counties of Flanders, Artois, Nevers, and Charolais, and Franche-Comté. He inherited also a political feud at the Paris court. Louis, Duke of Orléans, the king's brother, controlled the royal treasury and the queen Isabeau of Bavaria; rumour, sometimes loud, sometimes whispered, made him also the queen's lover. Louis taxed the royal demesne, paid himself enormous pensions, and used the royal council to advance his own clients. John, returning to Paris with a Burgundian retinue, took the opposite line: he denounced the taxes, courted the Paris guilds, and made himself the spokesman of the city against the wasteful court. The split between Burgundians and Orléanists was open by 1405; in November Louis and John formally reconciled at Chartres and rode together into Paris.

The reconciliation lasted two years. On the night of 23 November 1407, as Louis was returning from a visit to the queen at the Hôtel Barbette, he was set upon in the rue Vieille-du-Temple in the Marais by some fifteen masked men with axes; he was struck from his horse, his left hand severed when he raised it in defence, and his head split open in the gutter. The next day Paris was in shock; the king's council demanded that the murderers be found. On 25 November John appeared before the council and admitted to having ordered the killing, declaring that the duke of Orléans had been a tyrant whose removal was lawful. He fled the city, then returned in March 1408 with an army; a Burgundian theologian, Jean Petit, delivered before the king and court at the Hôtel de Saint-Pol a long Latin justification of the murder, the Justification du duc de Bourgogne, citing twenty-four propositions from Scripture and Aristotle in defence of tyrannicide. John was formally pardoned.

The murder was the founding act of the French civil war that would run until 1435. Louis's young son Charles of Orléans married Bonne of Armagnac, daughter of the Count of Armagnac, who became the new leader of the Orleanist faction, by then called Armagnacs. The Armagnacs took Paris in 1413; the Burgundians took it back in 1418, with the help of the Paris butchers (the so-called Cabochien rising had already shaken the city in 1413). The civil war was unusually savage: the streets of Paris in 1418 saw a Burgundian massacre of Armagnac prisoners, including a constable and an archbishop, dragged from their cells and slaughtered in the gutters.

The English invasion under Henry V cut across the civil war. At Agincourt on 25 October 1415 Henry destroyed a largely Armagnac French army; John, who had been ambiguous through the campaign, lost two younger brothers, Anthony of Brabant and Philip of Nevers, both of whom had ridden with the French. He did not, however, mobilise his own Burgundian troops, and the suspicion that he had wanted the French defeat clung to him. Henry V occupied Normandy through 1417 and 1418; John, controlling Paris from June 1418, governed the kingdom in the name of the increasingly enfeebled Charles VI while the Dauphin Charles, expelled from Paris, ruled south of the Loire.

By the summer of 1419 the situation required a settlement between Burgundian and Armagnac if the English were to be resisted. John and the Dauphin met first at Pouilly-le-Fort in July, then on 10 September on the bridge at Montereau-Fault-Yonne where the Yonne meets the Seine. The bridge had been provided with a barrier in the middle so that each side could meet with reduced escort. Accounts vary, eyewitnesses contradicted one another for years, and the trial pieces collected later have never settled the question of premeditation; what is certain is that during the conversation between the Dauphin and the duke, members of the Dauphin's escort drew their weapons and killed John with an axe-blow to the face. The body was hastily buried at Montereau. The next year his son Philip the Good had it exhumed and transferred to the Charterhouse of Champmol; the skull, with its great cleft above the left eye, was preserved as a Burgundian relic.

The political consequence was the Treaty of Troyes of May 1420, by which Philip the Good and the queen Isabeau of Bavaria gave the crown of France to Henry V and his heirs and disinherited the Dauphin Charles. The English-Burgundian alliance, made over the duke's body, would rule most of France for the next fifteen years; Joan of Arc's coronation of Charles VII at Reims in 1429 reversed its premise, the Treaty of Arras of 1435 dissolved it formally. The civil war whose central act had been the murder of Louis of Orléans in 1407 was finally laid down two decades after the death of the man who had ordered it.

John was a hard, intelligent, suspicious, capable man, of medium height with a face famously ugly even by his own court's standards. The historian Bertrand Schnerb, his modern biographer, calls him the first French politician of the modern type. He had inherited the second power in the kingdom and made it the first; he had murdered the second man of the realm and lived to be pardoned; he had been killed in turn by the heir of the man he killed, in a duplication that contemporaries did not fail to notice. The Burgundian state he passed to his son was the strongest it had ever been; the price his son would spend forty years paying.
