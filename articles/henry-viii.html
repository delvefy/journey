Henry VIII

1491 – 1547, King of England, second monarch of the House of Tudor, severer of the English Church from Rome

Henry Tudor was born at Greenwich Palace on 28 June 1491, the second surviving son of Henry VII and Elizabeth of York. His elder brother Arthur, Prince of Wales, was the heir; Henry was prepared for a career in the church or as a great noble. He was given the best humanist education available in England, with John Skelton as one of his tutors, and grew up tall, athletic, vain of his looks and his Latin, and devoted to the conventional ideals of late chivalric kingship. Arthur's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, daughter of Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, took place in November 1501; in April 1502 Arthur died at Ludlow, possibly of the sweating sickness, and Henry became heir to the throne at the age of ten. To keep Catherine's dowry and the Spanish alliance, Henry VII betrothed his second son to his elder son's widow, after a papal dispensation explained away the impediment of affinity. The boy spent the rest of his father's reign at court, largely shielded from public business.

He came to the throne on 22 April 1509, two months short of his eighteenth birthday. Within seven weeks he had married Catherine of Aragon at Greenwich, and on 24 June the two were crowned together in Westminster Abbey. The early years of the reign were dominated by the cardinal-archbishop Thomas Wolsey, the butcher's son from Ipswich who rose by sheer administrative capacity to be Lord Chancellor and effective ruler of England. Henry preferred to hunt, joust, hawk, dance, and compose music; he wrote a Latin defence of the seven sacraments against Luther in 1521, the Assertio Septem Sacramentorum, partly his own work and partly Thomas More's, for which Pope Leo X granted him the title Fidei Defensor, Defender of the Faith, which the British monarchy retains. He twice invaded France in alliance with the Emperor Charles V — Catherine's nephew — and won the small but flashy victory of the Spurs in 1513; in his absence, Catherine and the Earl of Surrey destroyed a Scottish army at Flodden on 9 September 1513, killing King James IV. The Field of the Cloth of Gold in 1520, the great display near Calais where Henry met Francis I of France, was the high theatre of his early kingship.

Catherine bore him a daughter, Mary, in 1516, and several other children who did not live. By the mid-1520s it was clear there would be no male heir. Henry came to believe, with the literal-minded biblicism that was always one side of him, that the marriage was cursed because of the verse in Leviticus forbidding a man to marry his brother's widow. By 1527 he was in love with Anne Boleyn, the daughter of one of his courtiers, and had decided that the marriage to Catherine must be declared null. Wolsey was sent to obtain the annulment from Pope Clement VII. The pope, a prisoner of Charles V after the sack of Rome that May, could not deliver. Wolsey failed and in 1529 was stripped of office; he died in 1530 on his way south to face trial.

What followed is sometimes called the King's Great Matter and was in fact the English Reformation. Thomas Cromwell, formerly Wolsey's servant, rose to be the king's chief minister and engineered, in a sequence of statutes between 1532 and 1536, the legal separation of England from the Roman obedience: the Submission of the Clergy in 1532, the Act in Restraint of Appeals in 1533, the Act of Supremacy in 1534 declaring the king the only Supreme Head on earth of the Church of England, the Treasons Act later that year. Thomas Cranmer, raised to the see of Canterbury for the purpose, pronounced Henry's marriage to Catherine void in May 1533; Anne, by then secretly married and pregnant, was crowned in June. The princess Elizabeth was born in September. Those who refused the new order were destroyed: John Fisher of Rochester and Thomas More were beheaded in 1535, several Carthusian priors and monks were hanged, drawn, and quartered. The Dissolution of the Monasteries followed between 1536 and 1540, transferring to the crown about a fifth of the landed wealth of England, much of it then sold to the gentry whose loyalty the dynasty needed.

Anne Boleyn did not give him a son either. In May 1536 she was tried on charges of adultery, incest, and treason that almost no modern historian believes; she was beheaded at the Tower on 19 May, and Henry married Jane Seymour eleven days later. Jane bore him Edward in October 1537 and died of childbed fever within a fortnight. The fourth marriage, to Anne of Cleves in January 1540, was a diplomatic match arranged by Cromwell; Henry found her unattractive and the marriage was annulled in six months, taking Cromwell with it — he was beheaded on 28 July 1540. The same day Henry married Catherine Howard, a teenage cousin of Anne Boleyn; she was beheaded in February 1542 for adultery, which in her case was real. The sixth wife, Catherine Parr, married him in 1543 and survived him.

The last decade of the reign was harsher and heavier. Henry put down the great northern rising of 1536, the Pilgrimage of Grace, with execution of its leaders. He invaded France again in 1544 and took Boulogne at huge expense. He warred with Scotland in the "Rough Wooing", trying to force the marriage of his son to the infant Mary Queen of Scots. He grew enormous — his armour from the end of the reign measures fifty-four inches around the waist — suffered from an ulcerated leg, and became increasingly suspicious and cruel. He died at Whitehall on 28 January 1547 in his fifty-sixth year, attended at the end by Cranmer, to whom he gave a last pressure of the hand instead of a confession.

He was succeeded by his nine-year-old son Edward VI, then by his daughters Mary and Elizabeth in turn, the dynasty dying out with the last of them in 1603. The settlement he had imposed on the English church survived him by a wider margin than the Tudors themselves: a national church, episcopal, royal in its supreme governance, doctrinally unstable but politically immovable. The cost to those who would not bend — More, Fisher, the monks, the rebels of the North — and to those who served him too well, including most of his ministers and two of his wives, was the price of an institution that survived in the form he gave it for centuries.

The road continues to Catherine of Aragon.
