Nikephoros Bryennios the Younger

c. 1062 – 1137, Byzantine general, statesman, and historian, husband of Anna Komnene

Nikephoros was born about 1062 at Orestias, the Macedonian fortress city better known by its later name of Adrianople, the chief place of one of the great military families of the European themes. His grandfather and namesake, Nikephoros Bryennios the Elder, was duke of Dyrrhachium and one of the leading commanders of the eleventh-century empire; in 1077, in the year of Nikephoros’s adolescence, the elder Bryennios had been proclaimed emperor by the troops of Macedonia and Thrace against the rule of Michael VII Doukas, had been defeated and captured by the young general Alexios Komnenos at the battle of Kalavrye in 1078, and had been blinded under the orders of the same Doukas court that had a year earlier offered to negotiate. The younger Bryennios grew up therefore in the awkward inheritance of a great house whose leading member had been broken by the very emperor who would later seize the throne and adopt the younger man into his family.

The match was Alexios’s deliberate work. In about 1097, when Bryennios was thirty-five and his eldest daughter Anna fourteen, Alexios married them and conferred on Bryennios the dignity of panhypersebastos and, in due course, that of kaisar — a court rank that placed him among the four or five most senior figures of the empire. The marriage was at once political and personal. Politically it bound the Bryennioi to the Komnenian regime so completely that the old family grievance disappeared: the historian who emerged in the next decades would write of the rebellion that had cost his grandfather his eyes in a tone of cool and even respectful dispassion. Personally, by every contemporary report, Anna and Bryennios were devoted; the funeral oration of Tornikes for Anna a half-century later recalled that “they lived in continual conversation about books, about her father’s reign, about the past.” They had four children — Alexios, John, Eirene, Maria — and Bryennios spent the next twenty years dividing his life between the imperial council, the campaigns of his father-in-law, and the writing room.

As a soldier he served Alexios in most of the major campaigns of the reign. He fought against the Pechenegs in Thrace in the early 1090s, on the Adriatic frontier when Bohemond launched his second invasion in 1107–1108, and in the Cilician campaign of 1112 against the Latin principality of Antioch. His own writing is reticent about his role in these years; but the Greek and Latin sources for the Norman war of 1107–1108 attribute to him a leading part in the negotiations that produced the Treaty of Devol, by which Bohemond accepted vassalage for Antioch. Alexios used him also for diplomacy at home: the imperial council relied on his judgment, and in the constitutional crisis at Alexios’s deathbed in August 1118 he was at the centre of the question of the succession.

That crisis is the only event of his life on which his contemporaries and posterity have judged him severely. Anna and her mother Eirene Doukaina, in the days of the dying emperor, urged Bryennios to seize the throne — Eirene as her preferred candidate for the imperial purple over her son John II, Anna as a partisan of her husband and of her own claim as the eldest porphyrogenita. Bryennios refused. Whether out of principle, out of unwillingness, or out of an old soldier’s political realism, he would not stand against John, who in any case had the support of the army and of his own younger brother Isaac. When in 1119 Anna and her mother joined a fresh plot to overthrow John on the Bithynian frontier, Bryennios again would not commit himself; on Anna’s testimony the plot collapsed because he “made of the male organs no use,” and was forgiven by John precisely because he had refused to use them. The episode dissolved Anna’s political life and confirmed Bryennios’s. He was kept on the imperial council; his property was untouched; he accompanied John on the Anatolian and Cilician campaigns of the 1130s, in particular on the great expedition of 1137 against Lesser Armenia and the Latin principalities of Antioch and Edessa, from which he returned to Constantinople sick and from which he died, probably in the autumn of that year, at his Constantinople house. He was about seventy-five.

His literary work was the Hyle Historias — the “Material for a History” — an unfinished prose history of the empire from the accession of Constantine IX Monomachos in 1042 to the year 1079, undertaken at Anna’s urging and at the request of his mother-in-law the empress Eirene, and broken off by his death before he could carry it beyond the campaigns of the elder Bryennios and of Alexios’s early generalship. The four books that survive are the principal Greek source for the disasters of the third quarter of the eleventh century — the loss of Apulia to the Normans, the loss of central Anatolia to the Seljuks, the defeat at Manzikert in 1071, the civil wars of the 1070s — and the principal Greek source for the early career of Alexios Komnenos before his usurpation in 1081. Bryennios wrote in the high classicising prose of the Komnenian renaissance, with conscious echoes of Xenophon and Polybius; his judgment is even-handed where his grandfather’s rebellion is the subject, his account of his father-in-law’s ascent is plain narrative, his treatment of the Doukai is even-handed in a way that the courtiers of the Komnenian regime would not have managed. The book breaks off mid-sentence in book four; whether Bryennios meant to take it down to 1118 or to his own time we cannot know. Anna in the Alexiad remarks that she had to take up the project from his death because the book he had begun could not be finished by “the hand of any other man,” and her own work is in essence the continuation of his.

Bryennios’s standing in the next two centuries was that of a respected but secondary writer. The Alexiad overshadowed his book by its scale, its ambition, and the literary force of its author; the Hyle circulated in a handful of manuscripts; the editor of the Bonn corpus in the nineteenth century treated it as the lesser book of a great pair. More recent scholarship has read the two works as a single project, written over forty years by a husband and a wife who shared archive, sources, opinions, and disappointments. The historian behind the Alexiad is in many places the elder of the two; her husband, who was older, had served the emperor a generation longer, knew the soldiers and the diplomats whose memoirs Anna would borrow, and had been at the centre of decisions that Anna, by sex and by the conventions of the court, could only watch. After his death, with Bryennios gone, Anna entered the longest and most productive phase of her own writing in the rooms of her mother’s monastery.

The road continues to John II Komnenos.
