Jesus Christ

c. 4 BC – c. AD 30 · rabbi of Nazareth, founder of Christianity

Jesus of Nazareth, the central figure of Christianity, was born near the close of the reign of Herod the Great, around 4 BC, in the small town of Bethlehem in Roman Judea. The gospels of Matthew and Luke recount his birth to Mary, a young Jewish woman from the village of Nazareth in Galilee, and to her husband Joseph, a carpenter said to be of the line of David. The infancy narratives place him in modest surroundings — a manger, shepherds, eastern visitors bearing gifts — and report a flight into Egypt to escape Herod's massacre of male infants. Of his childhood almost nothing is preserved, save a single episode in which the twelve-year-old, separated from his parents during a Passover pilgrimage, is found in the Jerusalem Temple sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions.

He emerged from obscurity around the age of thirty. At the river Jordan he was baptized by his kinsman John the Baptist, a fiery ascetic preaching repentance in view of an imminent kingdom of God. The gospels describe the descent of the Spirit and a voice from heaven confirming Jesus as God's son. Immediately afterward he withdrew into the Judean wilderness for forty days of fasting and temptation, and then, when John was arrested by Herod Antipas, began a public ministry of his own — first in Galilee, around the lake at Capernaum and the surrounding villages.

His message centered on the imminent arrival of what he called the kingdom of God or kingdom of heaven, a reign of justice and mercy in which the poor would be lifted up, the meek would inherit the earth, and the conventional hierarchies of wealth, ritual purity, and power would be inverted. He taught largely in parables — the Sower, the Mustard Seed, the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Pearl of Great Price — using everyday images of farming, fishing, and family life to disclose what he claimed were the inner workings of that kingdom. The Sermon on the Mount, preserved in Matthew's gospel, gathers many of his most distinctive teachings: the Beatitudes, the injunctions to love one's enemies and turn the other cheek, the Lord's Prayer, the warning against storing up treasures on earth.

He gathered around him a circle of twelve close disciples — fishermen like Peter, Andrew, James, and John; a tax collector, Matthew; a zealot, Simon; and others including the one who would betray him, Judas Iscariot. A wider group of followers traveled with him, among them women such as Mary of Magdala, Joanna, and Susanna, who supported the movement from their own resources. Crowds gathered wherever he went, drawn as much by reports of healings as by the teaching itself. The gospels record him restoring sight to the blind, cleansing lepers, casting out unclean spirits, calming a storm on the Sea of Galilee, feeding five thousand from a few loaves and fish, and raising the dead, most dramatically his friend Lazarus of Bethany.

These deeds put him on a collision course with the religious establishment. He associated openly with people considered ritually or morally unclean — tax collectors, prostitutes, Samaritans, Gentiles — and accepted invitations to their tables. He healed on the Sabbath and defended his disciples for plucking grain on it. He overturned the money-changers' tables in the Temple court and prophesied the Temple's destruction. To the Pharisees he charged hypocrisy; to the Sadducees, ignorance of scripture and the power of God. His followers began to call him Messiah and Son of David, titles weighted with political danger under Roman occupation.

The final week of his life is recounted in unusual detail. He entered Jerusalem on a donkey to the acclaim of pilgrim crowds waving palm branches, an act loaded with prophetic resonance. He shared a last Passover meal with the Twelve in an upper room, broke bread and shared wine as his body and blood, and washed their feet. That same night, in the garden of Gethsemane on the slope of the Mount of Olives, he prayed in anguish and was arrested by a Temple detachment guided by Judas. Tried first by the high priest Caiaphas and the Sanhedrin on a charge of blasphemy, he was handed over to the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate, who, after questioning him and finding no clear political crime, nevertheless yielded to the crowd's demand and sentenced him to death by crucifixion.

He was scourged, mocked, crowned with thorns, made to carry the cross-beam through the streets of Jerusalem, and nailed up between two criminals at a place called Golgotha. He died after a few hours, with the words "It is finished" — or, in Mark's starker version, a cry quoting the opening of Psalm 22. The Roman centurion at the foot of the cross is said to have remarked, "Truly this man was the Son of God." Because the Sabbath was approaching, the body was claimed by a wealthy and secret disciple, a member of the very council that had condemned him, who provided a new rock-cut tomb in his own garden and laid the body in it under the eyes of the women who had followed from Galilee.

On the third day, according to the unanimous testimony of all four gospels and the earliest letters of the apostle Paul, that tomb was found empty. The risen Jesus appeared to Mary Magdalene first, then to Peter, to the Twelve gathered behind locked doors, to two disciples on the road to Emmaus, to a crowd of more than five hundred at once, and finally to a skeptic named Thomas. He commissioned his followers to take his message to all nations and, after forty days, withdrew from sight in what the tradition calls the Ascension.

From this small, beaten community in occupied Judea grew, within three centuries, the official religion of the Roman Empire, and within two thousand years the largest religious tradition on earth. The shape of the Western calendar, the moral vocabulary of much of the world, the cathedrals and parish churches of Europe, the missions of every continent, and the inner life of perhaps a third of humanity all trace their origin to the rabbi from Nazareth who, in the brief span of a three-year ministry, redrew the map of what human beings believe about God and one another.
