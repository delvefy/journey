Herod Agrippa I

10 BC – AD 44 · king of Judea, last ruler of Herodian Israel

Herod Agrippa, the king known to history as Herod Agrippa I to distinguish him from his son of the same name, ruled the kingdom of his grandfather Herod the Great briefly and brilliantly from AD 41 until his sudden death three years later. He was born about 10 BC, raised at the imperial court in Rome alongside the future emperors Claudius and Caligula, ran through his money and his luck in a series of escapades that twice landed him in prison, and emerged, on the death of his patron Tiberius, with the boyhood friendship of the new emperor Caligula and a kingdom restored to his family. The book of Acts knows him as the Herod who put the apostle James to the sword and imprisoned Peter; Josephus, who knew his court from oral tradition, gives the longest and most vivid portrait of any Herod in the historical record.

He was the grandson of Herod the Great by Aristobulus, one of Herod's two sons by his Hasmonean wife Mariamne. Aristobulus and his brother had been executed by their father in Herod's last suspicious years; the orphaned Agrippa was sent in his early childhood to Rome by his mother Berenice, who had attached herself to the household of Antonia the Younger, the widow of Drusus the Elder and one of the most influential women of Augustus's later court. He grew up with Antonia's sons — including the lame and stammering Claudius, with whom he formed a lifelong friendship — and her grandson Drusus the Younger and great-nephews, including a boy a few years younger than himself named Gaius, remembered to history as Caligula.

His Roman youth was a long display of fashionable debt. By his thirties he had spent his inheritance, fled creditors to Idumea, attempted suicide, been rescued by his sister Herodias and her husband Herod Antipas at Tiberias, and then quarreled with Antipas and gone back to Rome. He was caught making indiscreet remarks about the aged Tiberius wishing for the succession of Caligula and was thrown into prison, where he was kept in chains for six months until Tiberius died. Caligula's first acts on becoming emperor were to release him, give him a gold chain of the same weight as the iron one he had worn, and grant him the tetrarchies that had been his uncle Philip's, with the title of king. Within three years a denunciation of Antipas brought him the rest of Galilee and Perea as well.

When Caligula was assassinated in January of AD 41, Agrippa was in Rome and played a decisive role behind the scenes. According to Josephus, he negotiated between the Praetorian Guard, who had hidden Claudius behind a curtain and proclaimed him emperor, and a Senate that wished to restore the Republic. He persuaded the Senate to accept Claudius and Claudius to spare the Senate, and emerged from the day with the rest of his grandfather Herod the Great's kingdom — Judea, Samaria, and southern Syria — added to his own. From AD 41 to 44 he ruled, for the first and last time, a Jewish kingdom of essentially the same extent as the one his grandfather had held.

His three-year reign in Jerusalem appears to have been remembered as the most popular of any Herodian's. He observed the law with public ostentation, wept openly at the Temple at the reading of Deuteronomy's prohibition on a non-Israelite king, and was assured by the priests that he was their brother. He cancelled an unpopular tax. He attempted to fortify the northern wall of Jerusalem and was checked by Claudius. He also did something his grandfather had not had to do: he laid hands on the leadership of the new Christian movement in his capital. He executed James the brother of John with the sword and arrested Peter, intending to bring him out for public execution after Passover. Peter's escape from the prison, narrated in Acts 12, ends with the apostle leaving the city; Agrippa, finding the guards baffled, executed them and went down to Caesarea.

At Caesarea on the coast he died in circumstances reported with rare agreement by Acts and Josephus. He sat in the theater of the city, in a robe woven of silver thread that caught the morning sun and dazzled the crowd, and presided at games dedicated, in Josephus's account, to the safety of the emperor. The crowd hailed him as a god. He failed to disclaim the salutation. He was struck within moments by a violent pain in the stomach, was carried back to his palace, and died five days later in great agony at the age of fifty-four. The narrators differ only in detail: Luke ascribes the death to an angel of the Lord because he had not given God the glory; Josephus, more clinically, names a great bowel pain and a five-day decline.

He left a seventeen-year-old son, the future Herod Agrippa II, judged too young by Claudius to take the kingdom, and three daughters — Berenice, Mariamne, and Drusilla — all of whom would marry into provincial royalty and reappear in the later chapters of the New Testament. Berenice would be at her brother's side, decades later, when the apostle Paul defended himself at Caesarea before the procurator Festus; Drusilla, by then the wife of Felix, was the prefect before whom Paul had earlier been brought. Judea was reabsorbed into the Roman province, never again to be ruled by a Jewish king. The dynasty of Herod the Great, which had reigned in some part of the country for a hundred and ten years since the senatorial decree of 40 BC, was effectively at an end.

He was a man of formidable charm, generosity, and Roman political instinct, the only Herodian who appears in his own person in three independent ancient sources — Acts, Josephus, and the rabbinic memory of the Mishnah, which records him as a king who tearfully accepted his place under the Torah — and the last to sit on his grandfather's throne in Jerusalem. The kingdom he ruled for three years was the kingdom his grandfather had built, but it would not survive his death.
