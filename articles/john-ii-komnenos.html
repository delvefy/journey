John II Komnenos

1087 – 1143, Byzantine emperor, “Kaloiöannes”

John was born at Constantinople on 13 September 1087, the second child and elder son of the emperor Alexios I Komnenos and Eirene Doukaina; the dignity of porphyrogenitos, which his elder sister Anna possessed and to which she would attach so much, came to him by the same right. At five he was crowned co-emperor on the prompting of his father, who saw clearly that Anna’s betrothal to the young Constantine Doukas would not survive a male heir of his own blood, and who substituted his son for his prospective son-in-law on the imperial coinage and in the imperial titulature. The court of his boyhood was governed by an extraordinary trio: his father, busy with the Pechenegs and the Norman war; his grandmother Anna Dalassene, until her retirement to a monastery about 1095, the working head of the civil administration; and his mother Eirene, who never reconciled herself to her son’s eventual succession and through the next twenty-five years preferred her daughter Anna and her son-in-law Nikephoros Bryennios. The crisis of his accession, in August 1118, was therefore a family quarrel as much as a constitutional one: at the bedside of the dying emperor John stripped the imperial signet from his father’s hand — some sources say Alexios pressed it on him deliberately, others that he took it without permission — slipped out of the palace, secured the acclamation of the army camped at the Philopation, and entered Constantinople through the gates the next morning with the patriarch riding behind him. His mother and sister, having failed to anticipate the move, retired to the Kecharitomene monastery; their attempted counter-plot of 1119 was put down without bloodshed, as John’s temperament required.

The byname Kaloiöannes — “John the Good” — was given him by the citizens of the capital and was used freely by historians who had every reason to dislike the Komnenian regime. The chronicler Niketas Choniates, looking back at the imperial reigns of the next century from the wreckage of 1204, called John “the best of the emperors of the Komnenian line, and indeed the most virtuous prince that ever sat on the Roman throne since Marcus Aurelius.” Choniates was a moralist for whom virtue and policy were sometimes the same and sometimes not, but the unanimity of the contemporary judgment is striking. John was personally pious, austere in dress and table, monogamously devoted to his Hungarian wife, refused to put men to death even for conspiracy, never sacked a captured city, never confiscated the property of his enemies beyond a temporary restraint. In a court that was learning under his successor to value cleverness and ostentation, he stood out for an old-fashioned simplicity that the rhetors of his own court had difficulty praising in the high mode and the rhetors of the next generation invoked as a lost model.

His wife was Piroska of Hungary, daughter of King Ladislaus I, betrothed to him in 1104 in his father’s reign as part of the diplomatic encirclement of the Norman threat in the Balkans. Renamed Eirene at her Byzantine baptism, she bore him eight children of whom four sons and four daughters survived infancy; she died in 1134 and was commemorated by John’s foundation, the monastery of the Pantokrator on the Fourth Hill of Constantinople — a complex of three churches, an attached hospital of fifty beds, an old-age home for retired courtiers, and the imperial mausoleum where Eirene, John himself, and most of their dynasty would be buried. The Pantokrator was the largest and most elaborate Byzantine foundation since the Macedonian dynasty’s and the most influential model for monastic charity in the later empire.

The reign was nearly twenty-five years of unbroken war, conducted in person and almost everywhere successfully. The Pecheneg remnant whom his father had broken in 1091 reorganised and re-entered the Balkans in 1122; John destroyed them at the battle of Beroia in Thrace on the feast of his foundation of that name and incorporated the survivors into his armies as a mercenary corps. The Serbs of the Drina valley, who had been raiding into Byzantine Bosnia, were defeated at the battle of the Drim in 1129 and their grand župan rendered tributary. The Hungarians, with whom he was related by marriage, were checked in two summer campaigns of 1127 and 1128 and confined to the line of the Danube. The Cilician Armenian principality of Leo I, who had seized the imperial cities of Tarsus, Adana, and Mamistra in the 1120s, was brought to heel in the campaign of 1137: the Roupenid was captured, sent to Constantinople in chains, the cities recovered. The principality of Antioch under Raymond of Poitiers, the husband of Bohemond II’s daughter Constance, was forced in the same campaign to acknowledge Byzantine overlordship; John entered the city as overlord in 1137. The next year his army campaigned in Syria proper, taking the small town of Buza’a near Aleppo, failing to take Shaizar in a difficult month-long siege at which his Crusader allies were openly hostile, and retiring to Cilicia.

His Anatolian recovery, conducted in annual campaigns through the 1130s, was on the same model: methodical, careful of his men, never decisive in a single battle but cumulative. By 1142 the empire’s frontier in Asia was further east than at any time since Manzikert seven decades before. In the spring of 1143 John assembled at the camp of Tarsus a great army with which he meant to march into Edessa, perhaps to take the city from the count Joscelin II and re-found a Byzantine governorship there. The plans collapsed in a hunting accident in the Cilician mountains in April: John scratched himself on a poisoned arrow drawing blood for a wild boar, the wound mortified, and the emperor died on the camp bed eight days later, on 8 April 1143, in his fifty-sixth year. The army was gathered around the tent. His three elder sons — Alexios, the co-emperor; Andronikos, the second; both died in the previous year, the elder of fever at Cilicia, the second of an unspecified illness in the same campaign — were already gone. His third son Isaac, his obvious heir by primogeniture, was at Constantinople. His fourth son Manuel, the youngest, twenty-four, was at the camp at Tarsus. To the surprise of the council John named Manuel his successor in a brief speech recorded by Choniates that ascribes to him a clear preference for the boy on grounds of military capacity. The army accepted; Manuel sent the loyal commander John Axouch ahead to secure Constantinople before Isaac could organise resistance; the empire passed without fighting to the youngest of the brothers and to a reign that, in glamour and ambition, would be larger than John’s own.

The reputation of John has remained higher in the historical literature than in the popular tradition, where Alexios is the founder and Manuel is the brilliant successor of the Komnenian century. But John was the holder — the prince who in a quarter-century of unspectacular wars converted his father’s recovery into a stable empire, who left the army intact, the treasury full, the frontiers further advanced than he had found them, and the capital adorned with the great Pantokrator that his successors continued to inhabit and to be buried in. Choniates, summing up the century, said that John’s reign was the only one in which the empire’s actual power and its claims to power exactly coincided. Manuel inherited the resources John had assembled; he would spend them differently.
