Joseph of Arimathea

1st century AD · councillor of Jerusalem who buried Jesus

Joseph of Arimathea, a wealthy member of the Jewish council known as the Sanhedrin, is named in all four canonical gospels as the man who claimed the body of Jesus after the crucifixion and gave him burial in a tomb cut from rock. Beyond this single intervention almost nothing of his life can be securely recovered, and yet the act for which he is remembered — a hurried, costly, and politically risky burial on the eve of the Sabbath — has fastened his name to the central event of the Christian story and made him, in turn, the subject of an extraordinary later legend.

He is described as coming from Arimathea, a town the gospels place in Judea but which has never been firmly identified, most often associated with the Hebrew Ramathaim, the home of the prophet Samuel in the hill country north of Jerusalem. He was, according to Mark, a prominent member of the council who was himself waiting for the kingdom of God; Matthew adds that he was a rich man and a disciple of Jesus; Luke calls him a good and righteous man who had not consented to the council's decision and deed; John alone tells us that he was a disciple, but secretly, for fear of the authorities. The four sketches converge on a single picture: a man of standing and means, an insider of the religious establishment that had just condemned Jesus, and a half-hidden sympathizer who broke his silence only when the rabbi he admired was dead.

The hour at which he chose to act is part of what makes him remembered. By Jewish law a body left exposed defiled the land, and the approach of the Sabbath — and in that week, of Passover — required burial before sundown. The Roman practice was to leave the crucified hanging as a warning, sometimes for days. To intervene meant going personally to Pilate, a Roman prefect known for short patience with Jewish petitioners, and asking, as a member of the body that had just demanded the crucifixion, for the corpse of a man executed for sedition. The gospels say Joseph took courage and went.

Pilate, on Mark's account, was surprised that Jesus was already dead and questioned the centurion before granting the body. Joseph then bought a length of fine linen, took the body down from the cross, wrapped it, and laid it in a new tomb cut in the rock — his own, Matthew specifies — in a garden near the place of execution. The gospel of John adds that Nicodemus, another Pharisee and council member who had once come to Jesus by night, brought a great quantity of myrrh and aloes, perhaps a hundred Roman pounds, for the burial. The two men together, the gospels imply, gave Jesus the burial of a rich man in the brief hours between his death and the start of the Sabbath. The women from Galilee, who had followed the cortege, marked the spot so they could return after the festival with spices of their own. They returned to find the tomb empty.

Joseph plays no role in the resurrection narratives beyond owning the tomb in which the resurrection is announced. The book of Acts does not mention him. The earliest letters of the apostle Paul, which preserve a creed older than the gospels stating that Jesus was buried and rose on the third day, do not name the man who buried him. His historical footprint, then, is the single scene of the entombment, attested by four independent gospel traditions that agree on his name, his role, and his unease.

That single scene was enough to draw to itself, in later centuries, a luxuriant growth of legend that has no claim on history. From the third century onward an apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus elaborated his trial by the Sanhedrin and his miraculous release after the resurrection. Medieval romances, beginning in twelfth-century France with Robert de Boron, identified Joseph as the man who caught the blood of the crucified Jesus in the cup of the Last Supper, made him the first guardian of what came to be called the Holy Grail, and sent him at the head of a band of converts to evangelize Britain, where, according to a Glastonbury tradition that crystallized in the same period, he planted his staff in the soil of Wearyall Hill and saw it bloom each Christmas as the Holy Thorn. None of these stories appears in any source before the high Middle Ages, and modern scholarship treats them as edifying invention rather than memory.

What does belong to the man is the contour of his choice. He was a member of a privileged class whose council had just secured the death of a popular Galilean teacher on a charge of blasphemy and handed him to the Romans. Whether out of conviction, conscience, or simple revulsion at the spectacle of an unburied body, Joseph used his standing — his access to Pilate, his wealth, his willingness to be seen leaving the prefect's residence carrying a corpse — to spare the dead man the indignity of a common pit and to provide him a tomb fit for a notable. In doing so he gave the resurrection narrative the location it needed: a known, sealed, guarded grave belonging to a verifiable witness from the very council that had condemned the executed man. Without that tomb, the empty-tomb tradition that anchors every gospel account would have had nowhere to be empty.

In Christian art and devotion he is fixed forever in the moment of the Deposition: an older, bearded man in fine robes, helping to lower the body from the cross alongside the younger Nicodemus, the two of them keeping promise to a teacher whose followers had fled. The reach of that quiet act, surfacing in scripture only briefly and only on its single occasion, has carried him from the margins of a gospel scene into two thousand years of Christian memory.
