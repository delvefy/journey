Geoffrey Chaucer

c. 1342 – 1400, English poet, civil servant, the father of English literature, author of the Canterbury Tales

Geoffrey Chaucer was born in London about 1342 to John Chaucer, a prosperous vintner and deputy butler to the king, and his wife Agnes. The family lived in Thames Street in the Vintry ward; John Chaucer's business supplied wines from Gascony and Bordeaux to the royal household, and the boy grew up in a mercantile household with constant connections to the king's officers. He was placed about 1357, fifteen years old, in the household of Elizabeth de Burgh, Countess of Ulster, the wife of Lionel of Antwerp, Edward III's second surviving son. The court placement was the start of his long life in royal service.

He served as a page or junior squire in Lionel's household; he sailed to France with the English army in the campaign of 1359 to 1360 and was taken prisoner in the unsuccessful siege of Rheims. His ransom of sixteen pounds was paid by Edward III himself — a fact recorded in the royal accounts — and he was repatriated in March 1360. The royal contribution to his ransom is the earliest evidence of the king's recognition of him as a man worth keeping. He was made an esquire of the king's household in 1367.

About 1366 he married Philippa de Roet, a maid of honour to Queen Philippa of Hainault. The marriage was professionally consequential: Philippa Chaucer's sister Katherine Swynford was successively governess in the household of Blanche of Lancaster, mistress, and eventually wife of John of Gaunt, with whom Chaucer was thus connected by marriage. Gaunt remained Chaucer's patron for the rest of the duke's life. The marriage produced perhaps four children, of whom Thomas Chaucer would be a substantial Oxfordshire landowner and chief butler of England, and Elizabeth Chaucer became a nun at Barking.

Through the 1360s and 1370s he travelled abroad repeatedly on royal business. He was in Spain on a diplomatic mission in 1366; he was in Italy in 1372 to 1373 negotiating a commercial treaty at Genoa, with a side journey to Florence on which he is conventionally supposed to have encountered the works of Dante, Petrarch, and Boccaccio in the languages of their composition; he was in France in 1377 negotiating Richard II's marriage to a French princess and in Lombardy in 1378 negotiating with Bernabò Visconti and Sir John Hawkwood. The Italian travels in particular changed his literary practice: the immense corpus of Italian narrative poetry of the previous generation, almost unknown in England in 1370, became available to him in a form he could read.

His public office shifted gradually from soldier to civil servant. In June 1374 he was appointed Comptroller of the Customs and Subsidies on Wool, Skins, and Hides at the Port of London, a major fiscal post with a substantial salary, and from the same year was granted a daily pitcher of wine for life. He held the customs office for twelve years, with a brief intermission, during which time he lived above the city gate at Aldgate. In 1385 he was appointed a Justice of the Peace for Kent and the following year was elected one of the Knights of the Shire for Kent in the parliament of October 1386. In 1389 he was appointed Clerk of the King's Works, in charge of repairs to the Tower, Westminster Palace, the chapel at Windsor, the Lower Mews, and to a string of royal lodges. The duties — supervising masons and carpenters, accounting for nails and lead and oak — were heavy and he was robbed twice in the course of them.

His writing ran in parallel with this career through forty years. The early Book of the Duchess, written about 1369, was an elegy for Blanche of Lancaster, John of Gaunt's first wife who had died of plague the year before; the poem is one of his earliest extant works and the first major English love-vision after the French model. The 1370s brought the House of Fame, the Parliament of Fowls, and the translation of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy. The first great work of his maturity was Troilus and Criseyde, completed in the mid-1380s in some eight thousand lines of rhyme royal stanzas: it adapted Boccaccio's Il Filostrato into the most ambitious English narrative poem yet written, and was widely read across the next century. The Legend of Good Women, with its long prologue addressed to Queen Anne of Bohemia, followed in the late 1380s; it is the work in which the legendary procession of nine classical heroines moves before the poet at the queen's command, and contains some of his most graceful pastoral passages.

The Canterbury Tales, on which he worked from about 1387 to his death, was conceived on a scale Chaucer himself never completed. The General Prologue describes a company of thirty pilgrims gathering at the Tabard inn at Southwark for the ride to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury, each of whom is to tell two tales going and two coming; only twenty-four tales were finished, and the return journey was never written. The pilgrims — the Knight, the Squire, the Miller, the Wife of Bath, the Pardoner, the Prioress, the Franklin, the Clerk of Oxford, the Parson, and the rest — constitute the most detailed and various social portrait of late medieval England in any literary work of the period. The tales themselves run the gamut from courtly romance (the Knight's Tale) to fabliau (the Miller's, the Reeve's, the Shipman's) to saint's life (the Second Nun's) to allegory (the Tale of Melibee) to mock-heroic beast-fable (the Nun's Priest's). The work was left, on his death, with substantial gaps and an unsettled order; the manuscript tradition transmits it in different sequences, of which the Ellesmere manuscript order has been the most followed.

Politically he weathered the storms of the 1380s and 1390s with the canny survival instinct of the lifelong civil servant. The Appellant ascendancy of 1387 to 1388 cost him the Westminster appointments held under Richard II's friends but he was reinstated under the king's restoration in 1389. The Lancastrian revolution of 1399, which deposed Richard II in favour of Chaucer's old patron Gaunt's son, Henry IV, brought him a new and increased royal pension — almost his last office in life was the granting of the Henry IV's protection in October 1399.

He died on 25 October 1400, fifty-eight years old, in a house leased from the abbot of Westminster on the south side of Westminster Abbey. He was buried in the abbey, the first lay person buried there for any reason other than his official connection — the position is the corner of the south transept that became, in the seventeenth century, Poets' Corner. His tomb was a simple stone for the first century and a half; in 1556 Nicholas Brigham raised over it the present canopied tomb-chest, the first memorial in what would become the gathering-place of English literary commemoration.

Chaucer's afterlife in English letters has been steady and well marked. He was, with William Langland and the Pearl-Poet, the third major figure of the burst of vernacular literary activity in the second half of the fourteenth century; he became, in the centuries that followed, by far the most read and most influential. The Spenserians, the Augustans, the Romantics — all returned to him as the source of the English literary tongue. Dryden, in the preface to his 1700 Fables Ancient and Modern, called him "the Father of English Poetry"; the title has remained largely unchallenged for three hundred years.
