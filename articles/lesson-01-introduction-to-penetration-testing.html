Welcome to the start of a long, rewarding road. Penetration testing is the practice of attacking a system — legally, deliberately, and with permission — in order to find the weaknesses a real attacker would, and to report them before anyone with bad intentions gets there first. You spent years in QA making software prove it does what it should. Pentesting is the mirror image: you make software confess what it shouldn’t do. The instincts you already have transfer better than you think. This first lesson lays the foundations — what the job actually is, the rules that keep it lawful, the method that gives it shape — and ends with you running your first scan from the Mac in front of you.

What you’ll learn

  • What penetration testing is, and how it differs from vulnerability scanning, red teaming and bug bounties.
  • Why your QA background is a head start, not a handicap.
  • The five-to-seven phase methodology that structures every engagement.
  • The non-negotiable law and ethics: authorisation, scope, and rules of engagement.
  • Where you are allowed to practise — and where you are not.
  • How to install your first tool on macOS and run a legal scan.

What penetration testing actually is

A penetration test (“pentest”) is an authorised, simulated attack against a computer system, network, or application, carried out to evaluate its security. You take on the role of an attacker, but you operate under a contract: a defined target, a defined window of time, and a written permission slip. The deliverable is not chaos — it is a report that tells the owner exactly what is broken, how badly, how you proved it, and how to fix it. The point of breaking in is to make the system harder to break into.

It helps to define the thing being protected. Security people talk about the CIA triad: Confidentiality (only the right people can read the data), Integrity (the data can’t be tampered with undetected), and Availability (the system is up when it’s needed). Almost every vulnerability you will ever find is, at root, a way to violate one of those three. A SQL injection that dumps a customer table breaks confidentiality; ransomware breaks availability; a tampered firmware update breaks integrity. Keep the triad in the back of your mind — when you find something, ask which leg of it you just kicked out.

It is not the same as its neighbours

Beginners blur these together; professionals don’t. Knowing the difference is the first sign you take the field seriously.

  • Vulnerability scanning / assessment — an automated tool sweeps for known weaknesses and produces a list. It is broad, shallow, and does not exploit anything. It answers “what might be wrong?”. A pentest answers “what can an attacker actually do with it?” by proving the impact.
  • Penetration testing — a human exploits real weaknesses to demonstrate concrete impact, within an agreed scope. Breadth meets depth.
  • Red teaming — a goal-oriented, stealthy, often long-running adversary emulation (“get to the crown-jewel database without the blue team noticing”). It tests detection and response, not just whether a hole exists.
  • Bug bounty — crowdsourced, pay-per-valid-finding testing of a public scope. Same skills, different commercial model.

Your QA background is a head start

You are not starting from zero. You are switching from one kind of testing to another, and most of the discipline carries over. Make this mapping explicit in your head — it will demystify a lot of what looks like new jargon:

  • A finding is a bug report with an exploit attached and an impact statement. You already know how to write a tight, reproducible report — that is half of what makes a good pentester.
  • Reconnaissance is exploratory testing: poking at the edges of a system to understand it before you have a plan.
  • Steps to reproduce are steps to reproduce. A finding a developer can’t reproduce is as useless as a flaky bug.
  • Severity / priority becomes CVSS (the Common Vulnerability Scoring System) — a structured way to rate how bad a finding is. Same instinct, formal scale.
  • Regression testing becomes the retest: after the client fixes a finding, you verify the fix actually holds.
  • Edge cases and “what if I do the unexpected thing?” is the entire job. The difference is one of intent: QA asks “does it work?”; security asks “can I make it misbehave on purpose?”.

The genuinely new muscles are the adversarial mindset (assume the system is lying to you and look for the gap between what it promises and what it enforces) and a body of technical knowledge — networking, the web stack, operating-system internals, and a toolkit. We build that knowledge a lesson at a time.

The shapes an engagement takes

How much you’re told up front defines the “box” you work in:

  • Black box — you get a name or an IP range and nothing else, like an outside attacker. Realistic, but slow.
  • White box (or crystal box) — you get full knowledge: source code, credentials, architecture diagrams. Fast and thorough; best for finding the most bugs per hour.
  • Grey box — partial knowledge, e.g. a low-privilege login. The common middle ground that simulates a malicious insider or a compromised user.

Engagements also have a domain: network/infrastructure, web application, mobile, wireless, cloud, social engineering, or physical. This course leans toward network and web first — the bread and butter — then branches out.

The methodology: the spine of every test

Professionals don’t poke at random; they follow a repeatable process. Several formal frameworks codify it — the PTES (Penetration Testing Execution Standard), the OWASP testing guide for web, the Cyber Kill Chain, and MITRE ATT&CK for cataloguing real-world attacker techniques. Strip them down and they describe the same arc. Internalise these phases now; every later lesson lives inside one of them.

  1. Pre-engagement — the paperwork that makes it legal: scope, timing, contacts, and written authorisation. Skipped by amateurs, never by professionals.
  2. Reconnaissance — gathering information about the target. Passive (public records, DNS, search engines — without touching the target) then active (interacting with it directly).
  3. Scanning & enumeration — mapping live hosts, open ports, running services and their versions. This is where Nmap, which you’ll meet today, earns its keep.
  4. Vulnerability analysis — turning that map into a list of likely weaknesses and matching them to known exploits.
  5. Exploitation — the dramatic part: actually using a weakness to gain access. Often a small fraction of the total effort.
  6. Post-exploitation — now that you’re in, what does it mean? Escalating privileges, moving deeper into the network (“pivoting”), and demonstrating real business impact.
  7. Reporting — writing it all up so the client can fix it. This is the product you are actually paid for, and the phase where your QA report-writing makes you immediately better than most beginners.

Two truths beginners find surprising: most of the time goes into recon and enumeration, not the “hacking” itself; and the report — not the shell — is the deliverable. A brilliant compromise that nobody can understand or fix is a failed engagement.

The law and ethics — read this twice

Everything in this course is legal in one context and a crime in another, and the only thing separating them is permission. There is exactly one rule, and it is absolute:

Never test a system you do not own or do not have explicit, written authorisation to test.

Unauthorised access, and even unauthorised scanning, is a criminal offence almost everywhere: the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act in the United States, the Computer Misuse Act in the UK, and equivalents across the EU and beyond. “I was only curious” and “I didn’t change anything” are not defences. A professional engagement is wrapped in documents precisely to make the authorisation unambiguous:

  • Scope — the exact systems, IPs, domains and times you are allowed to touch. Anything not in scope is off-limits, full stop.
  • Rules of Engagement (ROE) — what techniques are permitted (is social engineering allowed? denial-of-service? testing in production?), and who to call if something breaks.
  • Authorisation letter — the signed “get-out-of-jail” document from someone empowered to grant it, proving you are acting with consent.

Beyond the law there is professional ethics: stay in scope, protect any data you encounter, do no avoidable harm, and report honestly. The aim of the whole discipline is a better defender. Skills pointed the other way make you a criminal, not a hacker.

⚠ Rules of engagement for this course

From this lesson on, you will only ever practise against targets that are yours or that explicitly invite testing. Permitted: your own machines and VMs; intentionally vulnerable apps you run yourself (OWASP Juice Shop, DVWA, Metasploitable); sanctioned learning platforms (PortSwigger Web Security Academy, TryHackMe, Hack The Box, OverTheWire); and a small number of hosts whose owners publicly permit light scanning — including scanme.nmap.org, which we use today. Pointing these tools at any other system “just to see” is illegal. When in doubt, don’t.

Your Mac as a hacking workstation

Most of the trade’s tooling grew up on Linux, and the famous distribution — Kali Linux — ships hundreds of tools preinstalled. You do not need it today, and you don’t have to abandon macOS to learn. You have three good paths on a Mac, and you’ll end up using all three:

  • Native tools via Homebrew — a huge number of security tools (Nmap, and many more) install straight onto macOS with one command. Perfect for getting started, which is what we do now.
  • Docker — to run vulnerable practice targets in seconds without polluting your system.
  • A Kali VM under UTM (free, Apple-Silicon-friendly) or VMware Fusion — a full Linux attack box for when a tool is Linux-only. We build this in Lesson 2.

Homebrew is the macOS package manager — think of it as apt for the Mac. We’ll lean on it constantly, so installing it is step one of the whole course.

❯ Hands-on lab — your first scan

Open the Terminal app (press Cmd+Space, type Terminal, hit Return). We’ll install Homebrew and Nmap, then run a single, fully sanctioned scan.

1. Install Homebrew (skip if brew --version already works):

$ /bin/bash -c "$(curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Homebrew/install/HEAD/install.sh)"
# Follow the prompts. On Apple Silicon, run the two "Next steps" lines
# it prints so the `brew` command is on your PATH, then reopen Terminal.

2. Install Nmap, the network mapper — the single most useful tool in scanning & enumeration:

$ brew install nmap
$ nmap --version
Nmap version 7.9x ( https://nmap.org )

3. Run your first scan against scanme.nmap.org — a host the Nmap project explicitly provides for exactly this purpose:

$ nmap -sV scanme.nmap.org
Starting Nmap 7.9x ( https://nmap.org )
Nmap scan report for scanme.nmap.org (45.33.32.156)
Host is up (0.17s latency).
Not shown: 996 closed tcp ports (conn-refused)
PORT      STATE SERVICE VERSION
22/tcp    open  ssh     OpenSSH 6.6.1p1 Ubuntu ...
80/tcp    open  http    Apache httpd 2.4.7
...
Service detection performed. ...

What just happened. The -sV flag asked Nmap not only which ports are open but what software is listening on them (“service/version detection”). You now know the host is alive, that it speaks SSH on port 22 and HTTP on port 80, and roughly which versions are running. That last detail is gold: in a real engagement, a version number is the thread you pull to find a known vulnerability. You have just completed, in miniature, the scanning & enumeration phase. Your exact output will differ — the box changes over time — and that’s fine.

If it didn’t work: a corporate VPN or firewall can block outbound scans — try from a home network. If brew isn’t found after install, you missed the “Next steps” PATH lines; reopen Terminal and re-run them.

Key takeaways

  • A pentest is an authorised, methodical, simulated attack whose real product is a clear report that makes the target more secure.
  • It is distinct from scanning (no exploitation), red teaming (stealthy, goal-driven) and bug bounty (crowdsourced) — depth plus breadth, with permission.
  • Your QA discipline — reproducible reports, severity, retesting, exploratory poking — transfers directly; the new muscle is the adversarial mindset.
  • Every engagement follows the same spine: recon → scan/enumerate → analyse → exploit → post-exploit → report — and most time lives in recon, not exploitation.
  • One absolute rule: only test what you own or are authorised, in writing, to test.
  • Your Mac is a fine starting workstation — Homebrew for native tools now, Docker and a Kali VM soon.

Check your understanding

  1. In one sentence each, distinguish a vulnerability assessment, a penetration test, and a red-team engagement.
  2. Which leg of the CIA triad does each of these break: a leaked password database; a defaced homepage; a site knocked offline by traffic?
  3. Name the seven methodology phases in order. Which one is the actual deliverable, and which one usually consumes the most time?
  4. You find a public-facing server belonging to a company you don’t work for and you’re curious whether it’s vulnerable. What do you do, and why?
  5. Re-run today’s scan without -sV (just nmap scanme.nmap.org). What information did you lose, and why does the version detail matter to an attacker?
