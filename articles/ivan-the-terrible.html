Ivan the Terrible

1530 – 1584, Grand Prince of Moscow and first Tsar of all Russia

Ivan IV Vasilyevich, called the Terrible — the Russian Groznyi carries something more like awe or dread than mere cruelty — was born on 25 August 1530 at the village of Kolomenskoye outside Moscow, the long-awaited son of Grand Prince Vasili III and his second wife, Elena Glinskaya. His birth was attended by the rumour of comets and his death by the rumour of poison, and his life between the two was the most consequential reign in early modern Russian history.

He was three years old when his father died and eight when his mother, who had ruled as regent, died in turn — almost certainly poisoned by the boyar factions that resented her authority. The years of Ivan's minority were a war of palace clans. The Shuiskys and the Belskys seized and lost power by turns, executed each other in the antechambers of the Kremlin, and treated the boy ruler with a contempt he remembered. By his own later account he was sometimes neglected to the point of hunger, sometimes ignored, sometimes forced to watch the killing of his servants. The psychological mark was permanent; whether it explains the violence of his later reign is a question on which Russian historiography has never agreed.

In January 1547, at sixteen, he was crowned in the Dormition Cathedral with a new title: tsar of all Russia, the first Russian ruler so styled. A few weeks later he married Anastasia Romanovna Zakharyina, of a relatively modest boyar family, and the marriage proved to be the emotional centre of his life. The same year he gathered around him a council of capable men — Adashev, Sylvester, Macarius, Kurbsky — and embarked on a programme of reform. The Stoglav Synod of 1551 regulated church affairs; the new Sudebnik of 1550 codified law; the streltsy were created as Russia's first permanent firearm-equipped infantry; provincial administration was rationalised. In 1552 his army took Kazan after a long siege, breaking the most powerful of the Tatar khanates; in 1556 Astrakhan followed, and the entire course of the Volga lay under Moscow's hand. Russia now reached the Caspian.

The Livonian War, begun in 1558, was the hinge of the reign. Ivan's intent was to open Russia to the Baltic, breaking the commercial monopoly of the Livonian Order and the Hansa. The first campaigns were successful, but as the Order disintegrated its lands were claimed in pieces by Sweden, Denmark, and Lithuania, and what had begun as a regional war became a quarter-century of attrition against half of northern Europe. In the same period Anastasia died in August 1560, in circumstances Ivan was sure were poisoning; whether or not he was right, his collapse afterward was complete. He turned on his own councillors, drove Sylvester into a monastery, executed Adashev's family, and broke decisively with the great prince Andrei Kurbsky, who fled to Lithuania in 1564 and from exile began a famous correspondence with his old sovereign.

In late 1564 Ivan staged a more elaborate flight of his own. He left Moscow for Aleksandrovskaya Sloboda, announced his abdication, and waited until the Muscovites came to beg him back. He returned on terms. The country was to be split in two: a vast personal domain called the oprichnina, ruled by a black-clad and oath-bound corps of oprichniki answerable only to him, and the rest, the zemshchina, left to the old administrative order. For seven years the oprichniki acted as Ivan's instrument of terror. Boyar estates were confiscated, dynasties extinguished, monasteries plundered. In the winter of 1569–1570 the corps descended upon Novgorod on a rumour of treason and killed several thousand of its inhabitants in five weeks of methodical slaughter. The exact toll is disputed; the brutality is not. In 1571 the Crimean khan Devlet Giray, taking advantage of the disorder, burned Moscow itself, but in 1572 the boyar prince Mikhail Vorotynsky destroyed his army at Molodi, and Ivan, perhaps chastened, abolished the oprichnina the same year.

The Livonian War, meanwhile, ground on. The decisive break came with the election of Stephen Báthory to the Polish throne in 1576. Báthory's three campaigns of 1579 to 1581 took Polotsk and Velikiye Luki and brought a Polish-Lithuanian army to the walls of Pskov. The Truce of Yam-Zapolsky in 1582 returned all of Livonia. Russia would not reach the Baltic for another century and a quarter.

Two events of Ivan's late reign carried him beyond his own catastrophes. The first was his quarrel with his eldest son and heir, the tsarevich Ivan Ivanovich, in November 1581; in a sudden fury, by every credible account, Ivan struck him with an iron-shod staff, and the young man died of his wounds a few days later. The succession now passed to the second son Feodor, devout and feeble-minded, and behind him the infant Dmitry born of Ivan's last marriage. The second was the entry of the Cossack ataman Yermak Timofeyevich into the Khanate of Sibir in 1581–1582, which began the Russian conquest of Siberia under the patronage of the Stroganov merchant family. Ivan would not live to see most of it.

He died on 28 March 1584, reportedly while preparing to play chess. A modern exhumation found unusually high levels of mercury in his bones; whether this was a slow poisoning, a course of medical mercury for syphilis, or simply environmental, is unsettled. The throne passed to Feodor and, through him and his minister Boris Godunov, into the long crisis that became the Time of Troubles. Ivan left a Russia much larger than the one he inherited — the Volga, Siberia, the conquest of the Tatar khanates — and a Russia much more wounded: the administrative class decimated, the central provinces emptied, the legitimacy of the dynasty bound up in a single ageing tsar whose own line he had cut down with his own hand.
