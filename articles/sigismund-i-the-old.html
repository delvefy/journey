Sigismund I the Old

1467 – 1548, King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania

Sigismund I, called the Old to distinguish him from his son of the same name, was born on 1 January 1467 at Kozienice in Mazovia, the fifth son of King Casimir IV Jagiellon and his Habsburg queen Elizabeth. The Jagiellonian dynasty was at the height of its continental reach when he was born — his eldest brother Vladislaus would soon wear the crowns of Bohemia and of Hungary, his second brother John Albert and his third brother Alexander would in turn wear the Polish, his fourth brother Casimir would die young and be canonised as patron of Lithuania, and a fifth brother Frederick would become primate of Poland and a cardinal — and Sigismund, far down the line of succession, was raised as a younger son with no obvious throne ahead of him.

He spent the formative years of his maturity at the Hungarian court of his brother Vladislaus II in Buda, in the polyglot Renaissance environment that Matthias Corvinus had built and his successor had inherited. There he absorbed Italian humanist culture more thoroughly than any Polish prince before him, formed the connections that would later allow him to import Italian artists wholesale into Kraków, and acquired the duchies of Głogów and Opava in Silesia as appanages from his brother. He returned to the family seat only as the line of succession thinned: John Albert died in 1501, Alexander in 1506, and at the age of thirty-nine the youngest of the Jagiellonian brothers found himself elected king of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania.

His reign of forty-two years was, by the standards of east-central Europe in his time, peaceful, prosperous, and intensely creative. The most consequential single event was the Prussian Homage of 10 April 1525 in the market square of Kraków, where Sigismund's nephew Albert of Brandenburg-Ansbach, last Grand Master of the Teutonic Knights, knelt and swore feudal allegiance to him in return for being recognised as hereditary duke of a newly secularised, Lutheran Prussia. The arrangement ended two and a half centuries of intermittent war between the Polish crown and the Order on terms that, in the short run, gave Sigismund a tractable vassal in his nephew, and that, in the long run, planted the seed of the Hohenzollern state that would one day partition his kingdom — though no one in the Wawel Square that morning was thinking of that.

The wars of his early reign were against Muscovy, where Vasili III had taken up the unfinished business of Ivan III. In 1514 Sigismund lost the great fortress of Smolensk, which would not return to Polish hands until the next century, but a few weeks later his hetman Konstanty Ostrogski destroyed a far larger Muscovite army at the Battle of Orsha on the Dnieper. The double event — strategic loss, tactical triumph — set the pattern for sixteenth-century relations between the two states. Other campaigns followed against Moldavia, against the Crimean Tatars whose raids reached deep into the Ruthenian provinces almost every summer, and against the rebellious Teutonic Order before its dissolution. None broke his reign's general stability.

His personal life was structured around two marriages. The first, in 1512, was to the young Hungarian noblewoman Barbara Zápolya, sister of John Zápolya the future king of Hungary; she died in 1515 after bearing him two daughters. The second, in 1518, was to Bona Sforza of Milan and Bari, the Italian princess who would become a power in the kingdom in her own right and the principal political partner — and sometimes the principal political problem — of his reign. She bore him a son, the future Sigismund II Augustus, in 1520, and she was the force behind the boy's coronation as junior king in 1530, an arrangement his nobility tolerated as a one-time exception. She was also the conduit through which the high Italian Renaissance entered Kraków: the rebuilding of the Wawel Castle under the master Bartolomeo Berrecci, the construction of the Sigismund Chapel — the finest single Renaissance monument north of the Alps — Italian musicians, Italian sculptors, Italian gardeners.

He gave the early Reformation a cool, pragmatic response. The Lutheran ideas spread through Royal Prussia and through Lithuania in the 1520s and 1530s; his nephew in ducal Prussia became a Lutheran prince in 1525; his own Crown remained Catholic, but Sigismund refrained from systematic persecution. In matters of faith he preferred edicts and warnings to executions, and the great Polish tradition of religious latitude — for the nobility, at least — that would be enshrined under his son was already implicit in his style of rule. Nicolaus Copernicus, a canon of the cathedral chapter of Warmia in Royal Prussia, prepared his De Revolutionibus and his treatise on currency reform under Sigismund's reign, sending the latter to the Sejm as a memorandum that influenced royal monetary policy in the late 1520s.

The Sejm itself, the rising estate of the lesser nobility (szlachta), became an increasing political problem. In 1537 the levée en masse called at Lwów for a Moldavian campaign turned into a vast political demonstration of the szlachta against royal absolutism, royal taxation, and the influence of the foreign queen. It was nicknamed the “war of the chickens” (wojna kokosza), since the assembled nobles consumed the local poultry while petitioning rather than fighting, and Sigismund had to concede a long list of constitutional limitations on the crown. The shape of the elective Commonwealth that would emerge a generation later — strong nobility, weak king — can be seen forming in his reign even as he resisted it.

He lived to eighty-one, an age then almost unheard of for a reigning monarch. His son had been crowned twice — junior king in 1530, married to Elizabeth of Habsburg in 1543 — and the succession was secure. Sigismund the Old died at the Wawel on 1 April 1548 after a brief illness and was buried in the Sigismund Chapel he had built. The Polish-Lithuanian state he left to his son was at its political and cultural peak; the structural difficulties he had not solved — the elective monarchy in waiting, the unfinished war for the Baltic, the dynastic infertility of the next generation — would shape the next reign and the next century. His reputation in Polish memory has remained, with few interruptions, that of the wisest of the Jagiellonian kings.
