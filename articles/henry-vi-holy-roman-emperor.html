Henry VI, Holy Roman Emperor

1165 – 1197, son of Barbarossa, conqueror of Sicily, father of Frederick II, the brief master of the central Mediterranean

Henry was born in November 1165 at Nijmegen in the lower Rhineland, the second surviving son of Frederick Barbarossa and his Burgundian empress Beatrice; the death of his elder brother Frederick of Rötteln in 1170 made him heir to the German throne. He was educated under Conrad of Querfurt, an able cleric of the imperial chapel, and grew up at his father's court at the level of attention that the heir to the empire could expect: he wrote competent Latin and good Middle High German, was a respectable Minnesänger whose verses, with his name as Heinrich VI, survive in the Manesse codex, and was known to be cold, intelligent, and disinclined to small talk. He was elected king of the Romans at the Diet of Bamberg in August 1169 when he was three years old — his father wanted the succession unambiguously settled in his own lifetime — and was crowned at Aachen in the same month. At the great Court of Mainz in May 1184 he was knighted with his elder brother at the Whitsun festival that, by general consent of contemporaries, marked the height of his father's reign.

The Sicilian alliance that determined the political shape of his life was negotiated by his father in 1184. The Norman kingdom of Sicily, founded by Roger II in 1130 and ruled at the moment by Roger's grandson William II, had no legitimate male heir; the closest in blood was William's posthumous aunt Constance, then a woman of thirty in the convent of Palermo, who had been kept there in part because of a prophecy that she would bring ruin to the kingdom if married. Barbarossa and William, brought together by their respective fears of the Lombard cities and the Greek empire, agreed that Constance would marry Henry, with the Sicilian succession reserved to her if William died without children. The wedding was celebrated with extraordinary splendour at the cathedral of Milan on 27 January 1186; the bridegroom was twenty, the bride thirty-one. They were crowned king and queen of Italy at the same ceremony by the patriarch of Aquileia, the seat of Milan being vacant.

William II died on 18 November 1189 in his bed at Palermo, still childless. The Sicilian nobility, recoiling from the prospect of a German king, set aside Constance's claim and elected William's illegitimate cousin Tancred of Lecce. Henry, then occupied with the Italian regents and the German Welf affairs in his father's absence, had no immediate means of intervention. The news of Barbarossa's death in the Saleph river on 10 June 1190 reached him in Lombardy; he assumed the imperial title without further election. He came south in the spring of 1191, was crowned emperor at Rome on 14 April 1191 by the new pope Celestine III, an eighty-five-year-old elected the day before in haste so that the imperial coronation could take place, and pressed on to attempt the conquest of Sicily. The first campaign failed: a long siege of Naples in the summer broke down on disease in the German camp, and the imperial army returned north with most of its equipment lost. Constance was captured by Tancred's troops on the road north and would have remained a prisoner had not Celestine, on his own initiative, secured her release in return for the formal recognition of Tancred.

The interlude lasted three years. Henry spent them in Germany suppressing the Welf insurrection of Henry the Lion's sons, securing the obedience of the Saxon and Rhenish bishops, and benefiting from the windfall of Richard the Lionheart, returning from the Third Crusade and arrested by Duke Leopold V of Austria at Erdberg outside Vienna in December 1192. Leopold transferred the prisoner to Henry at the Diet of Speyer in March 1193; Henry held Richard at the castles of Trifels and Hagenau for fourteen months on a ransom set at 150,000 marks of silver, twice the annual revenue of the English crown. The money raised by Eleanor of Aquitaine across the Angevin lands was paid at Mainz in February 1194; Richard was released on 4 February after taking an oath of fealty for England that was a formal mockery of the substance but provided the imperial chancery with a useful precedent. The portion of the ransom that went to Leopold paid for the new town walls of Vienna and the towns of Hainburg and Wiener Neustadt; the portion that went to Henry equipped his second Sicilian expedition.

Tancred died on 20 February 1194, leaving the throne to his small son William III. The opposition in Sicily collapsed. Henry crossed the Alps in May, swept down the peninsula collecting submissions, was acknowledged on the Sicilian mainland in October, and entered Palermo without resistance on 20 November. On Christmas Day 1194, in the cathedral of Palermo, he was crowned king of Sicily; the next day, far to the north at Jesi in the Marches of Ancona, Constance gave birth to their only child Frederick — in the open of the marketplace, by her own arrangement, before a tent set up in the square with the women of the surrounding towns invited in as witnesses, because, at forty, she had had to confront ill-natured rumours that the pregnancy was simulated. The conjunction of dates, Christmas at Palermo and the day after at Jesi, was treated by both parents as providential and noted by every chronicler.

The administration he imposed on Sicily was harsh by the standards of the Norman court that had preceded it. He suppressed a conspiracy of the great Sicilian and Apulian magnates in the early months of 1195 with executions, mutilations, and confiscations on a scale not seen in southern Italy since the Norman conquest; the young William III was sent to a German monastery to be blinded, and disappears from the records. The Norman treasure of Palermo — the gold, the jewels, the dies for the magnificent royal coinage — was sent north to Trifels to fund the next stage of the imperial programme. He named his own German officers to the principal Sicilian offices, kept Constance herself at the Apulian court he set up at Bari rather than at Palermo, and treated the kingdom as the personal demesne of the German crown.

That programme, in the eighteen months that remained of his life, was unprecedented in its scope. He took the cross at Bari in April 1195 and set in motion the recruitment of a German army of some sixty thousand men for a new crusade, to leave in the spring of 1197. He attempted at the Diet of Würzburg in April 1196 to make the German crown hereditary in the male line of the imperial family, an experiment that the princes rejected after months of negotiation; he then secured the election, on the conventional terms, of his two-year-old son Frederick as king of the Romans at the Diet of Frankfurt in December 1196. He demanded and obtained from the Byzantine Emperor Alexios III a payment in tribute of sixteen hundred pounds of gold a year, the so-called Alamanikon; he received the homage of the kings of Cyprus and Armenia; he was negotiating a marriage of his cousin Philip of Swabia with the Byzantine princess Irene Angelina, daughter of the deposed Isaac II, with a clear view to claiming the Greek empire on the strength of it. The successor state to the Norman Mediterranean of Roger II was to be an Empire of the central sea, ruled from Palermo and Aachen by a single dynasty.

He never sailed on the crusade. In the late summer of 1197 the great German contingents began to arrive in Sicily; the fleet assembled at Messina. Henry himself, hunting on a hot day in the country near Messina, drank cold water and went down with a fever — dysentery in the report of his physicians, malaria in the diagnosis of some modern historians, on the testimony of his enemies poison administered at the table of Constance, the last with little evidence beyond the convenience of the explanation for the chronicler Otto of St Blasien. He died at Messina on 28 September 1197, aged thirty-one. He was buried in the porphyry sarcophagus that had been prepared for him in the cathedral of Palermo, between the tomb of his father-in-law Roger II and that which he had ordered for himself, where he lies still. The empire he had welded together fell apart in the following weeks. Constance brought their three-year-old child Frederick north to Foggia, took up the regency of Sicily alone with the protection of the new pope Innocent III, died herself in November 1198 after fourteen months of widowhood, and committed the boy to papal wardship. The German lands divided between the elections of Otto IV and Philip of Swabia. The Mediterranean monarchy that was to have been the union of the empire and the Norman kingdom was deferred for a generation, until the small king of Jesi grew up, and reclaimed the inheritance his father had won in the year of his birth.
