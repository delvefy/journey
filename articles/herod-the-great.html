Herod the Great

c. 73 BC – 4 BC · king of Judea, builder of the Temple, creature of Rome

Herod, called the Great by Josephus and known in Christian memory as the king of the massacre of the innocents, ruled Judea from 37 BC until his death in 4 BC, a reign of thirty-three years that re-shaped his kingdom in stone and bound it tighter than any of his predecessors to the will of Rome. He was the son of an Idumean Arab father and a Nabatean mother, an Edomite by ancestry, only Jewish by the forced conversion his grandfather's generation had undergone under the Hasmoneans; he was never accepted by his subjects as one of them, and never trusted by them, but he held them for three decades against external rivals, internal conspiracies, and his own deepening paranoia, and left behind a kingdom and a capital that astonished even his Roman patrons.

His father Antipater the Idumean had risen to the chief ministry of the failing Hasmonean kingdom under Hyrcanus II, the last priest-king, and had aligned the dynasty with Pompey and then with Julius Caesar; he was rewarded with Roman citizenship for the family and with appointments for his sons to provincial governorships in Judea and Galilee. The young Herod, made governor of Galilee in his twenties, suppressed a band of insurgents in the hills and was tried by the Sanhedrin in Jerusalem for executing them without trial; the trial was halted by Hyrcanus and Herod was sent away to Syria, beyond the council's reach. The lesson never left him.

The Parthian invasion of 40 BC overthrew Hyrcanus, killed Herod's brother Phasael, and installed the last Hasmonean, Antigonus, on the throne in Jerusalem. Herod fled to Rome, where, in the senate house, Mark Antony and Octavian — the future Augustus — together proposed and the senate confirmed his appointment as king of the Jews. He returned with Roman legions, took three years to recover the country, and in 37 BC entered Jerusalem at the head of Sosius's army, captured Antigonus, and had him sent to Antony at Antioch and beheaded — the first Roman execution of a reigning king. He cemented his claim by marrying Mariamne, granddaughter of Hyrcanus, the Hasmonean princess whose lineage he could not match but whose blood his children would carry.

The thirty-three-year reign that followed was a long maneuver among Roman patrons: Antony in his first decade — to whose mistress Cleopatra Herod owed the loss of his richest groves of balsam around Jericho — and then, after Actium, Augustus, before whom Herod, in one of the great political performances of the ancient world, presented himself stripped of his diadem and offered the loyalty he had given Antony, promising to give Augustus the same. Augustus restored him the diadem, restored his lost territories, and added new ones; the two men corresponded for the rest of Herod's life on terms of an unusual personal trust, and Augustus visited his kingdom and his works.

The works were the heart of his self-presentation. He built, in three decades, on a scale unmatched in the Levant before or after: the fortified palace-mountains of Masada by the Dead Sea, Herodium south of Jerusalem with its concentric round walls, Machaerus east of the Jordan; the great deepwater harbor of Caesarea Maritima on the coast, named for Augustus, with breakwaters of hydraulic concrete and a temple to Roma and Augustus on the headland; new cities, gymnasia, theaters, hippodromes, aqueducts, baths; foreign endowments at Athens, Sparta, Rhodes, Pergamum, and the Olympic Games, of which he became patron and presiding judge. Above all, beginning in the eighteenth year of his reign, he rebuilt the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, doubling its platform with massive retaining walls of which a single section, the Western Wall, survives, and raising on it a sanctuary of white limestone and gold that Talmudic memory called the most beautiful building anyone had ever seen.

The household within which all this was built was a nightmare. He had ten wives in his lifetime and many children; the elevations and demotions of his heirs, the conspiracies real and imagined among them and their mothers, and his own deepening suspicion drove a slow extermination of the royal family. He executed his beloved Mariamne in 29 BC, on a charge of adultery, and then mourned her for years. He executed her mother Alexandra, her grandfather Hyrcanus, her two sons Alexander and Aristobulus, his brother-in-law Joseph, and finally, five days before his own death, his eldest son Antipater. Augustus is said to have remarked that it was safer to be Herod's pig than his son — a pun in Greek that turned on Herod's outward observance of the Jewish abstention from pork.

His last years were tortured. He suffered, on Josephus's account, a wasting disease that filled him with ulcers, fever, and a hunger he could not satisfy; he tried the waters of Callirrhoe and was returned to Jericho to die in pain. His final order, frustrated by his death before it could be carried out, was to have a large number of the leading men of the nation locked into the hippodrome and killed on the day of his own death, so that the country would weep at his passing whether it wished to or not. The gospel of Matthew places into these final months his order, after the visit of the magi at Bethlehem, of the killing of the male infants of that village — an act, if historical, in the line of his many other paranoid eliminations.

His death in the spring of 4 BC threw his kingdom into immediate disorder. He had divided his realm in his last will among three surviving sons — Archelaus, Antipas, and Philip — and Augustus, after hearing their rival appeals at Rome, broadly upheld the division, but never restored the royal title to any of them. The reign of any reunited Jewish kingdom under his dynasty would not return until the brief three years of his grandson Herod Agrippa I half a century later. The Temple Herod had rebuilt stood for a single lifetime after his death, until the Roman legions of Titus burned it in AD 70. His harbor at Caesarea silted and slipped under the sea. The Western Wall of the Temple platform, on which his masons cut the largest building stones ever quarried in antiquity, is still standing.
