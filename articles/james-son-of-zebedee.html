James, son of Zebedee

d. c. AD 44 · apostle, brother of John, first martyr of the Twelve

James, son of Zebedee, called in tradition James the Greater to distinguish him from the other apostle of the same name, was a Galilean fisherman of Bethsaida or Capernaum and the elder brother of John the apostle. He was among the first four disciples Jesus called from the lakeshore, the inseparable companion of his brother in every gospel list of the Twelve, and one of the inner circle of three — Peter, James, and John — whom Jesus chose to be present at the most charged moments of his ministry. He was put to the sword by Herod Agrippa I around AD 44, the first apostle to die for his faith and the only one whose execution is recorded within the New Testament itself.

The gospels introduce him alongside his brother, mending nets in a boat with their father Zebedee, when Jesus, having just called Peter and Andrew from a neighboring boat, summons them as well. They leave their father with the hired men and follow. Their mother, possibly the Salome named among the women who followed Jesus from Galilee, accompanies the movement; Matthew's gospel records her approaching Jesus to ask that her two sons be seated on his right and his left in his kingdom, a request the other apostles meet with indignation. Jesus's reply is grim: they will indeed drink the cup he is about to drink, but the seats are not his to give.

Jesus called the two brothers Boanerges, sons of thunder, a name that may reflect a temperament displayed in two later episodes. When a Samaritan village refuses to host Jesus on his way to Jerusalem, the brothers ask whether they may call down fire from heaven upon it; he rebukes them. When an unauthorized exorcist is found casting out demons in Jesus's name, John, with James no doubt at his side, tries to stop him; Jesus tells them to let him be. They appear at the inner-circle moments: the raising of the daughter of Jairus, the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor, where Moses and Elijah appear and the voice from the cloud names Jesus the beloved son; and the prayer in Gethsemane, where they sleep while their teacher kneels alone.

After the resurrection the gospel of John records a last appearance by the sea of Galilee at which James was among the seven disciples who recognized the risen Jesus from the boat after a long night's fishing. He is named at the listing of the apostles in the opening of Acts and is presumed to be in the assembly that received the Spirit at Pentecost, but the narrative of Acts thereafter shifts to Peter and the later Paul, and James the son of Zebedee passes out of view for some fifteen years.

He reappears in the twelfth chapter of Acts, in a single deadly sentence: "About that time Herod the king laid hands on some who belonged to the church to harm them. He killed James the brother of John with the sword." The Herod was Herod Agrippa I, the grandson of Herod the Great and a friend of the Roman emperors Caligula and Claudius, who had been granted the whole of Judea, Galilee, and the territories of his uncles only three years earlier. He was a pious public observer of the Jewish law, and the persecution of the Christians under him is plainly a calculated appeal to the religious sentiment of his subjects in the capital. He went on to arrest Peter as well, putting him under quadruple guard for execution after the Passover; Peter's escape, recounted in the next paragraph, is one of the great prison-break stories of antiquity, but James was already dead.

He is, then, the first of the Twelve to drink the cup Jesus had said he would drink. He is also the only one of them whose death is dated by an external event: the persecution under Herod Agrippa I, securely placed in the spring of AD 44 by Josephus's account of the king's death the same year at Caesarea. His martyrdom is bare in the text — a single verb, no resistance recorded, no last words preserved. The book of Acts moves on within a sentence to Peter's imprisonment.

A great later tradition, with no anchor in any source earlier than the seventh century, sent the apostle James on a mission to the Iberian peninsula before his martyrdom, made him the evangelizer of Roman Hispania, and identified his bones, in the early ninth century, with a burial site in the northwestern corner of the peninsula at a place called Compostela. The shrine raised over that grave became, by the high Middle Ages, the third great pilgrimage destination of Christendom after Rome and Jerusalem; the routes to it, the Camino de Santiago, drew millions across the centuries and shape the cultural map of northern Spain to this day. The historicity of the Iberian mission is almost universally rejected by modern scholarship, which finds no trace of it in any source within five centuries of his life; but the cult that grew from it has been one of the largest sustained pilgrimages in the history of religion.

What survives from history is the simpler picture: a Galilean fisherman who left his nets on a morning by the lake, walked with his teacher up to a mountain in Galilee and once or twice into Jerusalem, was present at the raising of a girl and the agony in a garden, was named with his brother John as a son of thunder, and was struck down at the order of an Idumean king on the eve of a Passover, perhaps a dozen years after his teacher had been crucified by another Herod's client governor at the same season. He has no preserved writings, no epitaph, no last words. He is remembered, in the brief sentence of Acts, only by what was done to him.
