Antonia the Younger

36 BC – AD 37 · Roman matron, daughter of Mark Antony, mother of an emperor, grandmother of two

Antonia the Younger, born on the last day of January 36 BC and dead at Rome on the first of May AD 37, was the younger daughter of the triumvir Mark Antony and Octavia the Younger, sister of Augustus. She was one of the great matronly figures of the Julio-Claudian dynasty and, in the ancient tradition, the only person in three generations of that house to retain through every crisis of the early principate a reputation entirely untouched by scandal. She married once, the younger stepson of Augustus, Drusus the Elder; was widowed at twenty-seven and never remarried; raised her three surviving children in her own house at Rome; lived to see her son the popular general Germanicus die abroad and her daughter Livilla executed for treason at her own hand; and at her death left behind two grandsons who would become emperors — Caligula directly and Claudius four years later — and the great-grandchildren who would carry the principate to its end.

She was born in the brief and unstable interval of her parents' marriage, the daughter of the political pact of Brundisium of 40 BC by which Octavian had given his sister to Mark Antony as the human pledge of their reconciliation. She was the second daughter; her elder sister Antonia the Elder had been born about 39 BC. Her birthplace is not fixed in the sources; the family was at Athens in this period, and the likeliest setting is the Antonian household there. Her parents' marriage broke down within the year of her birth, Antony returning to Cleopatra at Alexandria and Octavia coming back to Rome with the two daughters; within a decade Antony was dead at Alexandria and Octavian was Augustus. She was four when her father killed himself, seven when her uncle settled the principate. She was raised in her mother's household at Rome with her elder sister, her older half-siblings by Octavia's first marriage to Gaius Claudius Marcellus Minor, and the orphans of Antony and Cleopatra whom her mother had received into the same nursery after Alexandria.

About 16 BC, in her early twenties, she was married to Drusus the Elder, the favored younger stepson of Augustus and the brother of Tiberius, then a rising military prince of about twenty-two. The marriage was, by every contemporary report, an affectionate one, and was brief. Three of their children survived to adulthood: Germanicus, born in 15 BC, who would become the most popular Roman general of his generation; Livilla, born about 13 BC, who would marry into the dynastic line and be put to death thirty years later by her own mother; and the youngest, Tiberius Claudius Drusus, born at Lugdunum in 10 BC, the lame, stammering, scholarly child whom the family treated as an embarrassment and who, by an almost impossible accident, would be hailed emperor in AD 41.

Drusus the Elder died in 9 BC of injuries from a riding fall on a road east of the Rhine, leaving Antonia a widow at twenty-seven with three small children. She refused to remarry despite reported encouragement from Augustus, who was anxious to lose neither her dynastic position nor the precedent of an unmarried imperial widow. She moved into her own household at Rome and remained there for the next forty-six years, a wealthy woman in her own right — among the largest landowners in Egypt, heiress to her father's surviving estates, by report the patroness of one of the great freedman households of the city — who presided over what was, for the next two generations, the unofficial center of female political influence in the Julio-Claudian house.

She was known to history as a model of old-republican feminine virtue. Valerius Maximus called her the rarest example of a Roman matron of the age, and the comparison — against her aunt-in-law Julia the Elder, who was being exiled to Pandateria for adultery in the very years when Antonia was raising her children in dignified widowhood — was unavoidable in the ancient tradition. She educated her own children carefully, particularly Claudius, in Greek as well as Latin; her household included the young Jewish prince Herod Agrippa I, who had been sent to Rome from Palestine as a boy and grew up with her sons, and to whom Antonia was over the next two decades a protector, a creditor, and a quietly insistent political ally.

Her great public moment came in the autumn of AD 31. The praetorian prefect Lucius Aelius Sejanus had for several years been the effective governor of Rome on behalf of the absent emperor Tiberius at Capri. He had earlier been the lover of Antonia's daughter Livilla, was widely believed to have been her accomplice in the poisoning of her husband Drusus the Younger in AD 23, and was now seeking permission from Tiberius to marry Livilla openly, which would put him in direct line for the principate. Antonia, through her freedman Pallas, sent a long private letter to Tiberius at Capri laying out the entire history: the affair, the original poisoning, the conspiracy. Tiberius, on receiving it, summoned a new praetorian prefect to Capri, gave him the order, and removed Sejanus in a single afternoon at a Senate meeting on the eighteenth of October. Sejanus was strangled in the Mamertine prison the same day, his children executed in their cells, his ex-wife Apicata's suicide note completing the case against Livilla. Livilla, when the conspiracy unravelled, was handed by Tiberius to Antonia herself for whatever punishment she chose; Antonia, by Cassius Dio's report, locked her daughter in a room in her own house and starved her to death.

She lived a further six years, surviving Tiberius. Her grandson Caligula, the son of Germanicus and Agrippina the Elder, became emperor on the sixteenth of March AD 37. He offered Antonia the title of Augusta, which she refused as she had refused it from Tiberius, and the public honors due to a grandmother of the principate. Six weeks later, on the first of May AD 37, she died. The circumstances are disputed: Suetonius reports that Caligula drove her to suicide by withdrawing his affection from her after she lectured him in private on his cruelty; other sources record a natural death of an old woman of seventy-three. Caligula gave her a public funeral and divine honors and gave her the title posthumously that she had refused to accept in life.

The Julio-Claudian dynasty from AD 14 onward was, except for Tiberius himself, almost entirely her line. Through Germanicus came his sons Caligula and Drusus and Nero Julius Caesar and his daughters Agrippina the Younger, Drusilla, and Julia Livilla — and through Agrippina the Younger came the emperor Nero. Through Claudius came his children Britannicus and Octavia, and through his fourth marriage the imperial throne itself for thirteen years. The four Julio-Claudian emperors after Tiberius were all her grandsons or great-grandsons or great-great-grandsons, and only hers. The dynastic story of the Roman first century, from her widowing in 9 BC to the death of Nero in AD 68, runs through her house.
