Catherine of Valois

1401 – 1437, French princess, Queen of England by her marriage to Henry V, grandmother of Henry VII by her marriage to Owen Tudor

Catherine was born at the Hôtel Saint-Pol in Paris on 27 October 1401, the youngest daughter of Charles VI of France and Isabeau of Bavaria. She was born into the long crisis of her father's reign — the king's recurrent madness was already nine years old, the open warfare between Armagnacs and Burgundians would break out within six years — and her childhood was disrupted by the political shifts of every regency. She and her sisters Marie, Joan, Michelle, and Mary spent long periods in the Augustinian convent of Poissy, which her father had founded and where Marie became a nun, and short periods at the royal court at the Hôtel Saint-Pol and the Louvre.

Her hand was a major piece of French diplomacy from early adolescence. Henry V of England, demanding from 1413 the Plantagenet claims in France according to the Treaty of Brétigny, demanded also her marriage; one of the early breaking points of the negotiations in 1414 was Henry's demand for a dowry of two million crowns. The English invasion of 1415 made the matter moot for a time. The conquest of Normandy by 1419 made it inevitable. After the murder of John the Fearless at Montereau and the Burgundian alliance with England, the Treaty of Troyes of 21 May 1420 gave Catherine to Henry as part of the legal apparatus of the disinheritance of her brother the Dauphin: she was nineteen, Henry was thirty-three, the marriage took place at Troyes Cathedral on 2 June, and the wedding feast was held in soldierly austerity in the bishop's palace at her husband's order, with simple meats and no minstrels — the king said the kingdom was at war and the festivity inappropriate.

She crossed to England in February 1421 with Henry V, was crowned at Westminster on 23 February, and was taken on a slow progress through southern and western England. She accompanied her husband back to France in May 1422, and on 6 December 1421 had borne, at Windsor, a son who was christened Henry. The infant Henry, by the terms of Troyes and by the rapid succession of deaths in the next ten months, became, on 31 August 1422 on his father's death, King of England at nine months old, and on 21 October, on his maternal grandfather's death, King of France as well.

Catherine returned to England with the body of her husband and was given precedence at his funeral at Westminster, where the bier was attended by an effigy with crowns of England and France. She was twenty-one and a widow. She lived for some years in the royal household with her infant son, then increasingly apart. The English political class regarded her with suspicion. The Council, dominated by her husband's surviving brothers John, Duke of Bedford, and Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, did not want her to remarry into any English noble house in a way that might create a step-claimant to the throne, and in 1428 the parliament passed a statute requiring her remarriage to be approved by the king and council.

By the time the statute passed it was probably already too late. Some time in the late 1420s, possibly as early as 1428 and possibly not until the early 1430s, the dowager queen had begun a relationship with a Welsh squire of her household named Owen ap Maredudd ap Tudur, anglicised as Owen Tudor, a soldier of obscure standing whose grandfather had been a steward to Llywelyn ap Gruffudd's last court at Snowdonia and whose family was one of the displaced gentry of Anglesey. Owen had served in the French wars, possibly in the household of Sir Walter Hungerford, the steward of Henry V; he became at some point Catherine's keeper of the wardrobe. The chroniclers of the next generation, hostile to the Tudor connection until political circumstances changed, reported the beginning of the affair variously: that Catherine was wooed when she watched Owen swimming in the river by Windsor, that he fell into her lap at a dance, that he was seen at his bath. None of these stories rests on direct evidence. What is certain is that they were married, secretly — the marriage seems to have been canonically valid but legally irregular under the statute of 1428 — and that she bore him at least four children: Edmund Tudor about 1430, Jasper Tudor in 1431, Owen Tudor (who became a Benedictine monk) about 1432, and a daughter of unknown name who died young.

The two of them lived for some years in the queen's manor at Hadham in Hertfordshire, away from the court, and the children were brought up in a household of mixed Welsh and English servants. The marriage was discovered, almost certainly, in the mid-1430s. Catherine, by then in poor health, retired to Bermondsey Abbey south of the Thames late in 1436. She died there on 3 January 1437, possibly in childbirth or its complications, possibly of an unidentified wasting illness, at the age of thirty-five. Her testament, written in the last days, asked her son the king to be gracious to her younger children, "born of my body." She was buried at Westminster Abbey in the Lady Chapel.

What followed was complicated. Owen Tudor was arrested by the council, imprisoned at Newgate, escaped, was retaken, and eventually pardoned; the half-Tudor sons Edmund and Jasper were taken into the household of their half-brother the king. Henry VI, when he came of age, treated his half-brothers with affection. He created Edmund Earl of Richmond in 1452 and married him to Margaret Beaufort, the heiress of the Beaufort line and a great-granddaughter, by legitimised descent, of John of Gaunt. Edmund died young, in 1456, of plague contracted in prison after his capture by Yorkists in the early stages of the Wars of the Roses; his posthumous son Henry Tudor was born to the thirteen-year-old Margaret Beaufort at Pembroke Castle in 1457. That child was the future Henry VII.

Catherine's biological position was, after her death, the central genealogical fact that linked Lancastrian royalty to the Welsh squire who had married her and so to the dynasty that came out of the Wars of the Roses to rule England for one hundred and eighteen years. Her grandson Henry VII won the throne at Bosworth in 1485 and married Elizabeth of York, daughter of Edward IV; their grandson Henry VIII, by his break with Rome, set in motion the chain of events that produced modern Britain. The thread runs back, in straight male-line descent, to Owen Tudor of Anglesey; the thread runs back in female line to Catherine of Valois. The princess of the Hôtel Saint-Pol who had been part of the dowry of Troyes had, by her irregular second marriage in an English manor house, set in train the dynasty that ended the medieval English monarchy and inherited it.

The road continues to Owen Tudor.
