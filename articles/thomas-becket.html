Thomas Becket

c. 1119 – 1170, chancellor of England, archbishop of Canterbury, martyr in his own cathedral

Thomas was born in the parish of St Mary Colechurch in Cheapside, London, on or about 21 December 1119, the son of Gilbert Beket, a merchant of Norman origin from the region of Rouen who had settled in London and prospered there in property and trade, and of Matilda, his wife, also of Norman family. Gilbert had been sheriff of London for a brief term and was a man of substance, with friends among the Anglo-Norman magnates who lodged at his house when they came up to the city; the household into which Thomas was born was urban, bilingual, and well connected without being noble. He was educated first at the Augustinian priory of Merton in Surrey, where the prior Robert was a friend of his father, and then at the schools of London. About 1140 he was sent to Paris to study the arts; the stay was short, perhaps two years, and the later chroniclers, his admirers and his enemies alike, recorded that he distinguished himself in argument without ever taking a master's degree. He returned to London on Gilbert's reversal of fortune — a fire had destroyed much of the family's property — and worked for some years as a clerk in the household of his kinsman Osbert Huitdeniers, a citizen of London.

About 1145 he entered the household of Theobald of Bec, archbishop of Canterbury, where the older Anglo-Norman archbishop was gathering a circle of young clerks to staff the metropolitan administration in the disordered last years of King Stephen. Theobald sent him for further study, to the great canon law school at Bologna and then to Auxerre, and on his return employed him in two missions to the curia of Pope Eugenius III in connection with the disputed succession to the English throne. In 1154 Theobald made him archdeacon of Canterbury, the senior ecclesiastical office in the gift of the see; in January 1155, when Theobald, the new king Henry II, and the chancellor's office had all to be coordinated in the work of restoring royal government after the Anarchy, the archbishop recommended his archdeacon for the chancellorship. Henry, then twenty-one, took to the man at once; Becket, in his thirties, became chancellor and shortly afterwards the king's closest personal companion.

The seven years that followed are described in the lives written within a generation of Becket's death by men who had known him in that office — William FitzStephen, John of Salisbury, Edward Grim, Herbert of Bosham — with a mixture of admiration and embarrassment. He kept the grandest household in the realm after the king's, fed a high table of guests at his own expense, hunted with hawks and hounds, took part in military campaigns (he led a contingent of seven hundred household knights in the king's Toulouse expedition of 1159), and conducted in 1158 an embassy to Paris on the king's behalf with a retinue of two hundred and fifty mounted attendants and a train of pack-animals so splendid that the French country people are reported to have asked what kind of king the English must be when his chancellor travelled so. In administration his work was less ostentatious but more durable: he was at the centre of the early business of the restored exchequer, supported the king's policy of recovering alienated royal demesne, and was, in the early views of churchmen who later quarrelled with him, a too willing instrument of royal pressure on the church.

In April 1162, Archbishop Theobald died. Henry, after a delay of weeks, named Thomas as his successor. The chapter of Canterbury was uneasy; Becket himself, by some accounts, warned the king that the ecclesiastical and the chancellor's offices could not be held together on the terms the king might want. He had not been ordained priest. He was ordained on Saturday 2 June 1162, consecrated archbishop the next day (the octave of Pentecost), and within months had resigned the chancellorship and transformed his manner of life. The hawks went; the kitchens were cut back; he wore a hair shirt under the archiepiscopal robes and washed the feet of thirteen poor men every morning. The transformation was sincere by all the surviving testimonies, and it was complete.

The first quarrel came over the trial of clerics accused of criminal offences. Henry held that a clerk found guilty in the church's court should then be turned over to a royal court for the secular penalty (mutilation or hanging) appropriate to the offence; Becket and the canonists generally held that no clerk could be tried or punished twice for the same offence. At the council of Clarendon in January 1164, Henry produced a list of sixteen "ancient customs of the realm" — the Constitutions of Clarendon — that codified the royal claim against the church on the criminous-clerk question and on a series of related matters: the prohibition of appeals to Rome without royal licence, the procedure for ecclesiastical elections, the disposition of vacant sees. Becket, isolated among the bishops, gave the assent the king demanded, then immediately withdrew it; the rupture was final. At the council of Northampton in October 1164, where Henry pressed against him a series of charges of contempt and of misappropriation of royal funds during the chancellorship, Becket refused to submit, slipped out of the king's lodgings at night, made his way to the coast disguised as a lay brother, and crossed to Flanders.

He spent six years in exile in France. The Cistercians of the abbey of Pontigny took him in for the first two years; the abbey of St Columba at Sens, the residence of Pope Alexander III during his own exile from Rome under the antipopes, sheltered him for the next four. Alexander, dealing simultaneously with the schism of Victor IV and Paschal III sponsored by the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa, was not in a position to dispense full support, and his relations with Becket were intermittently strained; but he gave him the formal backing of the papal authority and treated his cause as inseparable from the wider struggle of the Church against secular encroachment. The French king Louis VII, who had his own reasons to obstruct his Angevin neighbour, gave Becket sanctuary, an income, and the platform of his court. Becket meanwhile excommunicated successively the archbishop of York, the bishop of London, and the bishop of Salisbury for their parts in the Constitutions and in the coronation, on 14 June 1170, of Henry's eldest son as "young king" by Roger of York — a ceremony that violated the immemorial right of Canterbury to crown the kings of England.

A meeting at Fréteval on the Norman-French frontier on 22 July 1170 produced a tentative reconciliation: nothing was conceded on substance, the Constitutions remained unmentioned, but the archbishop was to return to Canterbury and the alienated revenues of the see were to be restored. Becket landed at Sandwich on 1 December 1170. He renewed the excommunications of the bishops who had participated in the coronation; they crossed at once to the king in Normandy. At the royal Christmas court at Bures, hearing their complaint, Henry uttered, in some form, the words of fury that the chroniclers report variously and that became, in the version of Edward Grim, "What miserable drones and traitors have I nourished and promoted in my household, that they let their lord be treated with such shameful contempt by a low-born clerk!" Four of the household knights — Reginald FitzUrse, Hugh de Morville, William de Tracy, and Richard le Bret — took the words as a commission. They left the court, crossed the Channel, gathered men at Saltwood and at Ranulf de Broc's manor at Saltwood, and rode to Canterbury on Tuesday 29 December.

That afternoon they came into the archbishop's hall, demanded the absolution of the excommunicated bishops, were refused, and went out to arm themselves. As Becket's clerks were leading him from the palace to the cathedral for vespers, the knights returned through the cloister and into the north transept where the steps go up to the choir. He turned to face them at the foot of those steps. The first blow grazed his head and cut the arm of his cross-bearer Edward Grim, who tried to ward it off; the second laid open his scalp; the third struck him to his knees, and the fourth struck off the crown of his head. The fifth, by Hugh of Horsea (a sub-deacon who had ridden with the knights), scattered his brains on the pavement, with the words "He shall not get up again." It was the late afternoon of 29 December 1170, the fourth day of Christmas.

The act was condemned across Latin Christendom within weeks. Henry, when the news reached him in Normandy, shut himself in a chamber for three days and refused food. On 21 February 1173, at Segni, Pope Alexander III canonised Thomas of Canterbury, only twenty-six months after the murder; on 12 July 1174 Henry walked barefoot through the streets of Canterbury and was scourged by the seventy monks of the cathedral priory and the five bishops who attended him at the tomb. The shrine that the pilgrims of the next four centuries would visit — immortalised in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales — was built above the place of the murder; the relics were translated to it in 1220 at the great ceremony presided over by Stephen Langton and the boy Henry III. The shrine was destroyed by Henry VIII in 1538, and the saint's name struck from the calendar of England; the spot on the cathedral pavement where he fell, marked since then by a candle and a wall-plate, remains.

The road continues to Pope Alexander III.
