Isabeau of Bavaria

c. 1370 – 1435, Queen of France, wife of the mad Charles VI, signer of the Treaty of Troyes

Élisabeth of Bavaria-Ingolstadt, called in France Isabeau, was born about 1370 at Munich, the daughter of Stephen III, Duke of Bavaria-Ingolstadt, of the Wittelsbach house, and of Taddea Visconti of Milan. Her mother was the niece of Bernabò Visconti, the lord of Milan whom her cousin Gian Galeazzo had recently deposed; her father was the head of a junior branch of the Wittelsbachs whose claim was to the smaller Bavarian duchies of southern Germany. She grew up in Munich and Ingolstadt, speaking German and a little Italian, learning the courtly French that her marriage would later require.

The marriage was made in haste. The young king Charles VI of France was looking for a bride from outside the major dynastic competitors; his uncle Philip the Bold of Burgundy, eager to keep him out of the orbits of the Habsburgs and the English, proposed the Bavarian alliance. Isabeau was sent through the imperial lands disguised in mourning dress for travel and ostensibly visiting an aunt; at Amiens in July 1385 she was presented to the seventeen-year-old king, who saw her, decided in the half-hour after his first sight of her, and was married to her in the cathedral on 17 July 1385. She was about fifteen. She was crowned at Saint-Denis in 1389 with great Burgundian-sponsored ceremony.

For the first seven years of the marriage she gave birth almost every year and was largely outside politics. She bore Charles twelve children between 1386 and 1407, of whom four sons and five daughters survived to adulthood: among them Charles VII, the future king, born in 1403, and Catherine of Valois, born in 1401, who would marry Henry V of England. Of her early children three died in infancy and one daughter Isabella was married at six to Richard II of England.

The king's first descent into madness in August 1392 changed her life and the political configuration of France. From that point and intermittently for the next thirty years Charles VI was, during long stretches, incapable of governing, sometimes incapable of recognising his wife, sometimes refusing to be touched on the conviction that he was made of glass. The political vacuum was contested between the king's uncle Philip the Bold of Burgundy and the king's younger brother Louis of Orléans. Isabeau, after a period of holding the balance, increasingly aligned herself with Louis. She was named in 1402 to preside over the royal council in the king's incapacity; she controlled, with Louis, the royal seal and the household budget; she held audiences for foreign ambassadors at the Hôtel Barbette in the Marais, the residence she had bought from Louis's mother and where, on the evening of 23 November 1407, Louis was returning from visiting her when he was murdered by John the Fearless's agents in the street outside.

The alliance with Louis gave the Burgundian propagandists their most useful theme. From the 1400s onward, in the pamphlets of the Cabochian rising of 1413 and continuously through the Burgundian press in the next decade, she was accused of adultery with her brother-in-law, of squandering the royal treasury on jewels and entertainments, and (in the most extreme versions) of having had Louis's children rather than the king's. The accusations have left the historical record so thoroughly that her modern rehabilitation, undertaken seriously only in the late twentieth century, has had to work against six centuries of black propaganda. The contemporary documents she signed, the chamberlains' accounts, and the surviving registers of her household show a queen burdened with constant pregnancies, intermittently widowed in fact by her husband's illness, attempting to hold the royal council together between two factions that wanted only her removal. She paid for the architectural reconstruction of the Augustine convent in Paris; she patronised the writer Christine de Pizan, who dedicated her City of Ladies in 1405 to the queen with a long preface defending women against the misogynist humanist tradition of the time; she was the protector of the poet Eustache Deschamps.

After Louis's murder in 1407 her political position deteriorated. The civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians left her, by the early 1410s, holding the centre between them with diminishing means. In 1417 the Armagnac faction, which then held Paris and the king, accused her of immorality, exiled her to Blois and then to Tours, and removed from her custody her household, her treasure, and her remaining children. The Burgundians, under John the Fearless, rescued her in the autumn of 1417 and brought her to Troyes; she allied with him openly from then on. When in May 1418 the Burgundian troops took Paris and massacred the Armagnacs in the prisons, she rode back into the city with John the Fearless.

By 1420 the political situation had simplified. The Dauphin Charles, since the murder of John the Fearless at Montereau in September 1419 in his presence, was the implacable enemy of the Burgundians and, in their telling, of his mother. Henry V of England held Normandy and was at the walls of Paris. The Treaty of Troyes of 21 May 1420 was negotiated by John the Fearless's son Philip the Good, by Isabeau, and by the Anglo-Burgundian council in the king's name. By it Henry V was married to Isabeau's daughter Catherine of Valois, made regent of France, and named heir to the French crown to the exclusion of the Dauphin, whom the treaty's preamble described as having committed great enormities. Isabeau signed the treaty as queen of France and crowning matriarch; the signature has been held against her ever since.

Whether she had any meaningful alternative is the question her later defenders have asked. The Burgundian propaganda and most subsequent French historiography treated her as the willing instigator of the disinheritance of her own son; the available evidence suggests rather that she signed what was put in front of her by the faction that then controlled Paris and that, with the Dauphin in arms south of the Loire, the Burgundians and English in Paris, and the king in one of his more disturbed periods, no other document was going to be signed.

Henry V died at Vincennes on 31 August 1422; Charles VI died at the Hôtel Saint-Pol on 21 October. The Anglo-Burgundian regency of Bedford governed in the name of the infant Henry VI; Isabeau, now in her early fifties, was put aside. She lived for another thirteen years in semi-retirement at the Hôtel Saint-Pol, increasingly stout, increasingly ill, watched but no longer feared, while her son Charles VII slowly recovered the kingdom from Bourges with the help, after 1429, of Joan of Arc. She did not live to see the reconciliation of her son with the Burgundians at Arras in 1435 or his return to Paris in 1436. She died at the Hôtel Saint-Pol on 24 September 1435 and was buried at Saint-Denis beside her husband, without state ceremony.

Her contemporary reputation, made by her enemies, was that of a foreign queen who had ruined her adopted kingdom. Modern reassessments — of Rachel Gibbons in English and Tracy Adams in particular — have made her a study in what could and could not be done by a queen acting under the constraint of a mentally ill husband and a factional council. The Treaty of Troyes remains the central document with which the verdict on her must reckon. The argument over whether she could have refused to sign it has been going on for six hundred years and has not concluded.
