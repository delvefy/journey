Jacques de Molay

c. 1244 – 1314, last Grand Master of the Knights Templar, burned on the Île aux Juifs

Jacques de Molay was born about 1244 at the small village of Molay in the county of Burgundy, in the lordship of Rahon, the son of a minor noble family. Almost nothing is known of his upbringing. He was received into the Order of the Temple at Beaune in 1265 by the preceptors Humbert de Pairaud and Aimé d'Oselier — an ordinary reception at which, in his later testimony before Clement V, he firmly denied that any of the blasphemous rites later attributed to the order had been required of him. He went to the East shortly after; he was at Acre in 1270 and remained in Outremer or Cyprus for most of the next quarter-century, rising through the conventual hierarchy in obscurity.

The Templar fortress of Acre fell to the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Khalil on 18 May 1291, the last major Christian stronghold of the Holy Land. The order withdrew to Cyprus under the Grand Master Thibaud Gaudin, who died about a year later. The chapter that met on the island in the spring of 1292 elected Molay Grand Master, by his own later account against his will. He was perhaps fifty, an old man by the standards of an order recruited from younger sons. His task was twofold: to keep the Templars relevant to a crusading project that no European prince now intended to undertake, and to defend the order's vast holdings of land and treasure in the West against the kings, dukes, and bishops who increasingly saw them as a parasitic and dispensable foreign body within their realms.

For the first task, Molay spent his early years as Grand Master on Cyprus, conducting small naval raids against the Egyptian coast and a brief reoccupation of the island of Ruâd off Tortosa in 1300–1302 in cooperation with the Mongol Ilkhanate. The garrison of Ruâd was overrun by the Mamluks in 1302 and the survivors enslaved; the Mongol alliance, on which Molay had pinned his hopes, fizzled out when the Ilkhan Ghazan died in 1304. Molay sailed to the West in late 1306 at the summons of Clement V, who had been considering a project of merging the Templars with the Hospitallers and wanted the two Grand Masters' views. Molay's memorandum opposed the merger; he proposed instead a new Crusade with a sealed force of fifteen thousand Christian knights commanded by a sovereign prince, and a renewal of his order's privileges. The memorandum was the last document he wrote in freedom.

He landed at Marseille in late 1306 and travelled north with a retinue of sixty knights and a baggage train of pack-horses bearing the order's accumulated cash from the East. Philip IV of France, debt-encumbered and revenue-hungry, received him with elaborate ceremony and named him godfather of his royal son Robert — a gesture of trust which historians have generally read as cover for what was being planned. Through the summer of 1307 Philip's lawyers, principally Guillaume de Nogaret and Guillaume de Plaisians, were preparing the charges; royal officers were quietly being briefed; sealed orders had been sent to every senior bailiff in France for a coordinated arrest. Clement V, alarmed at rumours that had reached him, summoned Molay to Poitiers for an interview but allowed him to return to Paris. On Friday 13 October 1307, in the small hours of the morning, the order's officers throughout France — perhaps two thousand men — were arrested in a single operation. Molay was taken at the Paris Temple.

The charges, drafted by Nogaret, accused the Templars of denying Christ and spitting on the cross at their reception, of obscene initiation kisses, of homosexuality enjoined as a duty of the order, of idol-worship of a bearded head sometimes called Baphomet, and of secret apostasy. Under interrogation conducted by the king's inquisitors and by the royal officers of the prison — with torture, frequently to the point of death; thirty-six men died at Paris in the first weeks — the great majority of the Templars confessed in some degree. Molay confessed on 24 October 1307 to the spitting on the cross and denial of Christ, but never to the more sensational charges of sodomy and idolatry. He repeated the confession before an assembly of Paris masters of theology in late October.

The proceedings were a jurisdictional scandal: the Templars were a papal order, exempt from royal courts. Clement V protested, suspended the inquisitorial commissions in February 1308, then in November agreed to a parallel ecclesiastical inquiry. He summoned Molay to Poitiers in mid-1308 and again to Chinon, where Molay retracted his confession before a papal commission — the so-called Chinon Parchment, rediscovered in the Vatican archives only in 2001, records the absolution that Clement's cardinals granted him for his original denial of Christ. The papal proceedings dragged on for six more years. Defended at Paris in 1310 by the heroic Templar lawyer Pierre de Bologna, fifty-four of the rank-and-file Templars who had retracted their confessions were burned at the stake outside the city as relapsed heretics on the order of the Archbishop of Sens, a brother of one of Philip's chamberlains. The collective defence collapsed.

The Council of Vienne, meeting from October 1311, considered the case for seven months and refused, by a majority of the bishops present, to condemn the order. Clement therefore acted by his own apostolic authority: by the bull Vox in Excelso of 22 March 1312 he suppressed the Templars on the ground not of guilt proven but of defamation past repair. The property went, formally, to the Hospitallers. Molay and his three senior subordinates — the Visitor Hugues de Pairaud, the Preceptor of Aquitaine Geoffroi de Gonneville, and the Preceptor of Normandy Geoffroi de Charny — were reserved to papal judgement and kept in confinement for two more years.

On 18 March 1314 the four were brought out before a scaffold erected on the parvis of Notre-Dame de Paris to hear, from a papal commission of three cardinals, the verdict: perpetual imprisonment, on the basis of their original confessions. Molay then spoke. He retracted his confession publicly and entirely, declared the order innocent of the charges against it, and accused himself only of cowardice in having ever, under torture, said otherwise; Geoffroi de Charny seconded him. The cardinals adjourned the session in confusion; Philip, informed in the Palais de la Cité across the way, summoned a council the same afternoon, declared the two relapsed heretics, and ordered them burned the same evening, without further reference to the pope. The pyre was raised on the Île aux Juifs, a small islet in the Seine between the gardens of the royal palace and the Hôtel de Nesle. Witnesses recorded that the two men died with dignity, asking only that they be allowed to face the cathedral of Notre-Dame as they burned.

From the pyre, in the legend that grew up almost immediately and was reported in chronicles within a few years, Molay summoned Pope Clement and King Philip to answer for the order at God's tribunal within the year. Clement died on 20 April 1314, less than five weeks later, of a long-standing intestinal disease; Philip died on 29 November the same year after a stroke. The legend, magnified in the later French Revolutionary tradition (a man at the foot of the guillotine in 1793 is said to have shouted that Molay was avenged at last), gave Jacques de Molay a posthumous renown which his cautious and modest lifetime could not have predicted. His order was extinguished by a stroke of the papal pen; his name was preserved by the curse he was supposed to have uttered as he burned.
