Eleanor of Aquitaine

c. 1122 – 1204, duchess of Aquitaine, queen of France, queen of England, mother of three kings

Aliénor was born about 1122, probably at one of the ducal castles in Poitou or Bordeaux, the elder daughter of William X, duke of Aquitaine and count of Poitou, and of Aénor of Châtellerault, of one of the great vassal families of the duchy. Through her father she descended from a line of dukes who held more land than the kings of France, governed it with little reference to the Capetian crown, and had given European literature its earliest vernacular lyric: her grandfather William IX, the seventh duke of Aquitaine, is the first troubadour known by name, the patron of the new courtly verse of the langue d'oc, and the inventor of much of the social atmosphere — love-poetry, polished female company, the easy mixture of Latin and Romance learning — in which his granddaughter grew up. She was educated to that standard. She read Latin, spoke fluent Occitan and the French of the northern courts, knew the chansons de geste and the troubadour repertoire, hunted and rode, and had no formal training in arms or law but watched both administered around her from infancy.

Her father died on Good Friday 1137 at Santiago de Compostela on a pilgrimage; she was about fifteen, the heiress to a duchy as large as the kingdom of France itself, and immediately the most valuable unmarried woman in western Europe. Within months she was contracted to Louis, the seventeen-year-old heir of the French king Louis VI, and the wedding was held at the cathedral of Saint-André in Bordeaux on 25 July 1137. The king-emperor Louis VI died on 1 August while the news of the marriage was still on the road north; Eleanor entered Paris as queen of France. The marriage was the longest political union of her life, fifteen years, and the unhappiest. Louis VII was monkish, scrupulous, slow to assert himself; Eleanor came from a duchy where ducal authority was confident and the queen's voice carried weight; the chroniclers at the curt French court found her free Aquitanian manners scandalous, and Louis's confessors found their influence over the king diminished by her presence. There were two daughters — Marie, born about 1145, and Alix, about 1150 — and no son.

In 1147 she went with Louis on the Second Crusade, which Bernard of Clairvaux had preached in response to the fall of Edessa to the Saracens. The expedition was a disaster of starvation, bad weather, and Turkish ambush in Anatolia. The army reached Antioch in March 1148, where the prince of Antioch was Eleanor's uncle Raymond of Poitiers, a younger son of William IX and a man of her own court and language. The visit produced a scandal that the eastern and the northern French chroniclers report with different emphases: Eleanor sided with Raymond's strategy of campaigning against Aleppo, Louis with the western preference for a march on Jerusalem, and an accusation, never substantiated, of incest between her and her uncle was current at the French camp. Whatever the truth, the marriage was finished. Louis dragged her on to Jerusalem and back to France by sea; the relationship was broken; and in March 1152 a council of French and Aquitanian prelates at Beaugency annulled the marriage on the ground of consanguinity in the fourth degree, a relationship which had been known and dispensed with from the beginning.

Eight weeks later, on 18 May 1152 at the cathedral of Poitiers, she married Henry, Duke of Normandy and Count of Anjou, eleven years her junior, the great-grandson of William the Conqueror through his mother Matilda and the heir to a Norman-Angevin inheritance that included England by claim. The marriage doubled her political stakes and effectively doubled his. In December 1154, when the agreed succession from King Stephen at last opened, Henry was crowned king of England as Henry II at Westminster; Eleanor was crowned queen consort beside him. Together they ruled an Angevin federation that ran from the Pyrenees to the Tweed.

The second marriage was fertile and politically formidable. Between 1153 and 1166 Eleanor bore Henry eight children: William, who died at three; Henry the Young King, crowned in his father's lifetime; Matilda, who married Henry the Lion of Saxony; Richard, the favourite; Geoffrey, count of Brittany; Eleanor, who married Alfonso VIII of Castile; Joan, who married first the king of Sicily and second the count of Toulouse; and John, the youngest, born in 1166. The early years of the marriage were active and itinerant; Eleanor crossed and recrossed the Channel with Henry, governed in his absence, presided over courts in Normandy and at Westminster. After 1167, with Henry increasingly absent on his continental wars and a public mistress in the person of Rosamund Clifford, she withdrew to Poitiers, where she held her own ducal court in the years that the later legend would call the Court of Love — the actual evidence is thinner than the later embroidery, but Poitiers under Eleanor was certainly the centre of an Aquitanian aristocratic culture in which troubadour song flourished and the young heirs of the great houses received their education.

In 1173 her three eldest sons rose against their father in the Great Revolt, and Eleanor backed them with the resources of the duchy and with diplomatic support from Louis VII. The rising collapsed within a year. Eleanor, attempting to escape from Poitou in male disguise to join her sons in Paris, was caught by Henry's men, brought to Chinon, then to England, and confined under various forms of guard at Salisbury, Winchester, and other royal castles for the next sixteen years. She was now into her fifties. She was permitted some movement — brought out to attend court at the great festivals, allowed visitors of her own household — but she was a prisoner of state and her property in Aquitaine was administered without reference to her. Her elder sons died one by one during the captivity: William long ago, Young Henry of dysentery in 1183, Geoffrey at a Paris tournament in 1186.

Henry II died on 6 July 1189. Richard's first act on hearing the news was to send to England to release his mother. She was about sixty-seven; she lost no time on retirement. Through the summer of 1189 she rode the circuit of the English royal castles, took the oaths of the sheriffs and the bishops in Richard's name, released political prisoners, and remitted some of the more hated levies of the late reign. She crowned her son at Westminster in September. When Richard sailed for the Third Crusade in the summer of 1190 she was the principal effective regent of his lands, working with the justiciars in England, the seneschals in Normandy and Anjou, and her own officers in Aquitaine. She brought Richard his bride Berengaria of Navarre as far as Sicily in 1191 (an embassy of nearly five hundred miles in winter through Italy, undertaken at sixty-nine). On Richard's captivity in Germany in 1193 she organised the collection of the ransom across the two kingdoms and personally delivered it at Mainz in February 1194.

After Richard's death in April 1199 the inheritance was contested between her youngest son John and her grandson Arthur of Brittany, the son of the dead Geoffrey. Eleanor, then seventy-seven, threw her authority behind John, made the formal acknowledgment of him as her overlord in Aquitaine to remove him from succession by another route, and rode south to Spain in the winter of 1200, at seventy-eight, to fetch her granddaughter Blanche of Castile for the dynastic marriage with the future Louis VIII of France that closed the peace with Philip Augustus. In 1202, when Arthur's forces besieged her at the castle of Mirebeau in Poitou, she held out until John's relief column came up; the relief brought Arthur into John's hands and ended his cause. She retired then at last to the abbey of Fontevraud, took the veil among the nuns whose patron she had been for fifty years, and died there on 1 April 1204, aged about eighty-two. She was buried with her husband Henry II and her son Richard in the choir of the abbey church; her painted tomb effigy, lying beside theirs in the gown of a duchess and holding a book, survives.
