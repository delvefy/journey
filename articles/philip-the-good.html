Philip the Good

1396 – 1467, Duke of Burgundy, builder of the Burgundian state, founder of the Order of the Golden Fleece, patron of Jan van Eyck

Philip was born at Dijon on 31 July 1396, the only surviving son of John the Fearless of Burgundy and Margaret of Bavaria. He grew up in a court already in violent conflict with the rival Orléanist faction that dominated the household of his cousin the mad Charles VI in Paris. He was seven when, in 1407, his father had Louis of Orléans murdered in a Paris street, opening the long civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians that defined French politics for the next thirty years. He was twenty-two on 10 September 1419 when his father, in turn, was hacked to death on the bridge of Montereau in the presence of the Dauphin Charles, the future Charles VII, during a peace conference that had been intended to reconcile the parties.

The murder at Montereau was the decisive event of his life and of his policy for the next sixteen years. He inherited the duchy at once, ranged his lands against the Armagnac party of the Dauphin, and committed himself by the Treaty of Troyes of May 1420 to the alliance with Henry V of England that disinherited the Dauphin from the French crown. The Burgundian skull, his father's, with the great cleft above the eye, was kept on display at the Charterhouse of Champmol outside Dijon, where Philip's grandfather had founded the family mausoleum, as a permanent reminder of what he owed the Armagnacs. Par le trou de cette tête, English visitors were told, les Anglais sont entrés en France — the English came into France through this hole in the head.

For fifteen years he held to the English alliance. He provided the army that besieged Compiègne in 1430 and there captured Joan of Arc, whom he sold the following winter to the English at Rouen. His agents fought Charles VII's reconquest of the Île-de-France through the late 1420s. Yet the alliance brought him no territorial gains in France beyond what he could win for himself, and after the death of John, Duke of Bedford, in 1435 the practical case for it dissolved. In September 1435, at the Congress of Arras, with papal mediators presiding, Philip made his peace with Charles VII. The terms were exceptionally generous: he kept all his territorial gains, was personally exempted from doing homage to the French crown for life, and was given immediate possession of the Somme towns. Paris fell to the French the next year.

The rest of his reign was the methodical construction of a territorial state. By inheritance, purchase, marriage, and judicious pressure on his neighbours he absorbed, one by one, the duchies and counties between his northern and southern blocks. He acquired Namur in 1421 by purchase from its bankrupt count; the counties of Hainaut, Holland, and Zeeland from his impoverished cousin Jacqueline of Bavaria by stages between 1428 and 1433; Brabant and Limburg by inheritance in 1430 from his cousin Philip of Saint-Pol; Luxembourg by purchase from Elizabeth of Görlitz between 1441 and 1443. He neutralised the prince-bishop of Liège, broke the city's rebellion at Gavere in 1453, and installed his nephew Louis of Bourbon as bishop. By the end of his reign the Burgundian state ran almost without interruption from Mâcon and the Saône valley north through Franche-Comté, Luxembourg, and Brabant to the North Sea, with footholds in Picardy and Alsace.

The court he built was the model of every European prince for the next century. He held it at Bruges, Ghent, Brussels, and Lille in rotation; the household ordinances of 1426 and 1458, copied across the continent, regulated the offices of the kitchen, the wardrobe, the chapel, the chamber, and the stables in detail unprecedented since the late Roman empire. He maintained a permanent musical chapel that, under Gilles Binchois and later Antoine Busnois, was the principal centre of European polyphony; he employed Jan van Eyck from 1425 until his death in 1441 first as varlet de chambre and then as court painter, sending him on confidential missions to Portugal and Aragon and receiving from him the great altarpieces (the Ghent Altarpiece, the Madonna of Chancellor Rolin) that defined northern Renaissance painting. After Van Eyck he patronised Rogier van der Weyden of Brussels, the manuscript illuminators of Bruges, the sculptor Claus Sluter and his successor at Champmol, and a generation of tapestry-makers and silver- and goldsmiths at Tournai and Arras.

On 10 January 1430, at his third wedding at Bruges to Isabella of Portugal, he founded the Order of the Golden Fleece, an order of chivalry intended at once to rival the English Garter and the French Saint-Michel and to bind the higher Burgundian nobility, with their disparate dynastic loyalties, to the duke and to each other. The original twenty-four members were the picked great lords of his territories; the order's archive and statutes have an extraordinary continuity, the order itself surviving still in the Spanish and Austrian Habsburg branches as a working chivalric distinction.

Isabella of Portugal bore him in 1433 his only surviving son, Charles, called the Bold. The court chronicler George Chastellain and the brilliant Burgundian propaganda apparatus made of the duke and duchess the symbolic centre of an idea of Christian princely virtue. In February 1454 at Lille, in response to the fall of Constantinople to the Ottomans the previous year, he hosted the Feast of the Pheasant, a vast banquet of vows in which he and his nobles, dressed as crusaders, swore on a live pheasant to recover the holy places. The crusade was never launched. The vows survive in the records of his court as evidence of an ideology that could no longer find its way to action.

His later years were difficult. He fell out repeatedly with his son Charles, who was impatient with his father's accommodations with France; he was for several years effectively superseded as duke by his son with the support of the Croÿ family at court, then reconciled. He intervened in French politics by sheltering the Dauphin Louis, who had quarrelled with Charles VII and lived at the Burgundian court from 1456 to 1461. When Louis became king in 1461 Philip thought he had earned a friendly French neighbour; Louis XI proved otherwise. Philip died at his palace at Bruges on 15 June 1467 at almost seventy-one, leaving the duchy at its largest extent ever, the court at its most splendid, and a son who would in ten years lose it all on the field of Nancy.

His standing among his contemporaries was as the type of the magnificent prince. The chroniclers Olivier de la Marche and Georges Chastellain wrote of his household with the awe due to a model. The Habsburgs inherited his administrative and ceremonial structures through Mary of Burgundy and used them for centuries; the standing of a Spanish or Austrian court, down to the late eighteenth century, descended in detail from Philip's ordinances. The state he built was destroyed by his son; the cultural form he gave it survived.
