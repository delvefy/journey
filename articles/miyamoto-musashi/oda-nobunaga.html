Oda Nobunaga

Japanese warlord, first unifier of Japan (1534–1582)

Oda Nobunaga was the warlord who broke the long anarchy of Japan's warring-states age and set the country on the road to unification. Born in 1534 as heir to a minor lordship in Owari Province, he was thought wild and unpromising in youth, yet he seized his inheritance and revealed a ruthless brilliance that would terrify and transform his country.

His rise began in 1560 at Okehazama, where with a small force he ambushed and destroyed the vast invading army of Imagawa Yoshimoto. Forging an alliance with the young Tokugawa Ieyasu and binding able men to his service, Nobunaga marched on the capital in 1568, installed and then in 1573 deposed the last Ashikaga shogun, and made himself the dominant power in central Japan.

Nobunaga was a relentless innovator. At Nagashino in 1575 his massed gunners shattered the famed cavalry of the Takeda. He crushed the warrior-monks who had defied secular rulers for centuries, burning the great monastery of Mount Hiei and grinding down the militant Buddhist leagues. He welcomed European missionaries and firearms, promoted men by talent rather than birth, and patronized the tea ceremony as an instrument of prestige.

His career ended abruptly in 1582, when his trusted general Akechi Mitsuhide turned against him and surrounded him at the temple of Honno-ji in Kyoto. Trapped and beaten, Nobunaga took his own life. Though he did not live to complete the unification, he had made it inevitable, and his ferocity, ambition, and modernizing genius left him among the most commanding figures in Japanese history.
