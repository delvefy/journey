Plutarch

Greek biographer and essayist (c. 46–120)

Plutarch was a Greek writer of the Roman imperial age whose biographies have shaped the way the West remembers the heroes of antiquity. Born in the small town of Chaeronea, he was a learned man of many parts, a Platonist philosopher, a priest at the oracle of Delphi, and a Roman citizen who moved easily between Greek and Roman worlds.

His masterpiece is the Parallel Lives, in which he paired a famous Greek with a famous Roman, Alexander with Caesar, Demosthenes with Cicero, and many more, and set their careers side by side to draw out lessons of character. He cared less for the chronicle of battles than for the revealing anecdote, the word or gesture that laid bare a man's soul, and through these vivid portraits the statesmen and generals of the ancient world still live.

Alongside the Lives he composed the Moralia, a vast and miscellaneous collection of essays on morals, religion, politics, and the customs of mankind, ranging from the serious to the charming.

Few authors have been more quietly influential. His Lives became a treasure-house for later ages: Shakespeare built his Roman plays upon them, and statesmen and revolutionaries from the Renaissance to the Enlightenment formed their notions of virtue and greatness by reading him. Among the lives he wrote, none was more famous than that of the world-conqueror Alexander the Great.
