Marie Curie

Polish-French physicist and chemist (1867–1934)

Marie Curie was the pioneering physicist and chemist whose work on radioactivity opened the atomic age and who became the most celebrated woman in the history of science. Born Maria Sklodowska in Warsaw under Russian rule, she made her way to Paris to study at the Sorbonne, enduring poverty to pursue the learning denied to women in her homeland.

There she married the physicist Pierre Curie, and together they investigated the mysterious rays given off by uranium, a phenomenon she named radioactivity. Through years of grinding labour, processing tons of ore in a leaky shed, they isolated two new elements, which she called polonium, for her native land, and radium.

In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics, the first ever awarded to a woman. After Pierre's sudden death she carried on alone, taking his chair to become the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne, and in 1911 she won a second Nobel, this time in chemistry, the only person ever to be so honoured in two different sciences.

During the First World War she equipped and drove mobile X-ray units to the front to aid wounded soldiers. Her devotion to her work cost her dearly: the radiation she had handled for decades destroyed her health, and she died in 1934. Her name endures as the very symbol of scientific genius and perseverance.
