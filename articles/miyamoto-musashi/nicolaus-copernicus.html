Nicolaus Copernicus

Polish astronomer (1473–1543)

Nicolaus Copernicus was the Polish astronomer who set the earth in motion and changed forever humanity's picture of the cosmos. A man of many parts, educated in Poland and Italy, he served as a cathedral canon and worked as administrator, physician, and economist, pursuing astronomy in the hours he could spare.

For fourteen centuries the heavens had been understood through the system of Ptolemy, with a motionless earth at the centre of all things. Copernicus, troubled by the cumbersome devices that system required, proposed instead a startling alternative: that the sun stood at the centre, and that the earth was merely one of the planets, turning on its axis each day and circling the sun each year.

He worked out this heliocentric scheme in mathematical detail but long hesitated to publish, wary of controversy and of contradicting both ancient authority and the philosophy of Aristotle. Only at the very end of his life did his great book, On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, appear in print, in 1543; tradition holds that he saw a printed copy only on the day he died.

The idea seemed at first a mere calculating device, but it was revolutionary, and the generations that followed would take it up in earnest. Galileo would defend it, and Kepler refine it, until the moving earth became the foundation of modern astronomy. The shift still bears his name: the Copernican revolution.
