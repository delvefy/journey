Julius Caesar

Roman general and dictator (100–44 BC)

Julius Caesar was the Roman general and statesman whose career broke the old Republic and opened the way to the empire. Born in 100 BC to an ancient but unremarkable patrician family, he climbed the ladder of Roman politics with daring and debt, and joined Pompey and Crassus in the informal pact of power known as the First Triumvirate.

His conquest of Gaul over the 50s BC, recorded in his own lucid commentaries, won him glory, riches, and the loyalty of hardened legions. When his rivals in the Senate demanded he disband his army, he instead led it across the river Rubicon into Italy, plunging Rome into civil war. He crushed Pompey, pursued his enemies across the Mediterranean, and in Egypt formed his famous alliance with Cleopatra.

Master of the Roman world, Caesar had himself made dictator, eventually for life. He reformed the calendar that still bears his name in its July, relieved debt, enlarged the Senate, and extended citizenship, but his concentration of power and the suspicion that he aimed at kingship alarmed the old aristocracy.

On the Ides of March in 44 BC a conspiracy of senators, among them Brutus and Cassius, stabbed him to death in the Senate house. His murder did not restore the Republic but unleashed new wars, from which his adopted heir Octavian would emerge as the first emperor. So great was his name that it became, in tongues across Europe, the very word for ruler.
