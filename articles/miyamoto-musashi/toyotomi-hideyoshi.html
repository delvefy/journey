Toyotomi Hideyoshi

Japanese unifier and imperial regent (1537–1598)

Toyotomi Hideyoshi rose from the humblest origins to rule all Japan, the second and in many ways the most astonishing of the country's three great unifiers. Born around 1537 to a peasant or foot-soldier family in Owari, he entered the service of Oda Nobunaga as a lowly attendant and climbed by sheer ability to become his master's most successful general.

When Nobunaga was killed in 1582, Hideyoshi reacted with decisive speed, defeating the assassin Akechi Mitsuhide at the battle of Yamazaki and so making himself Nobunaga's heir in fact. Overcoming his remaining rivals, he completed the conquest of Japan by 1590 with the subjugation of the Hojo at Odawara, ruling from his magnificent castle at Osaka.

Barred by his common birth from the title of shogun, he governed instead as imperial regent. He ordered a great sword hunt that disarmed the peasantry, conducted sweeping land surveys, and fixed the boundaries between warrior and farmer, freezing the social order. A lavish patron of the arts and the tea ceremony, he raised Sen no Rikyu to eminence and then, in a fit of suspicion, commanded the master to take his own life.

His ambition outran Japan itself: in the 1590s he launched two vast invasions of Korea, costly campaigns that ended in failure and were abandoned after his death. He died in 1598, leaving an infant son and a council of regents whose rivalries would soon shatter his settlement. For all that, his ascent from nothing to mastery of a nation remains one of history's great careers.
