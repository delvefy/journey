Umar ibn al-Khattab

Second caliph and conqueror (c. 584–644)

Umar ibn al-Khattab was the second caliph and one of the most powerful rulers in the history of Islam, the man under whom the new faith burst out of Arabia to become a world empire. A formidable Meccan who had at first bitterly opposed Muhammad, he underwent a dramatic conversion and became one of the Prophet's staunchest and most respected Companions.

On the death of Abu Bakr in 634, Umar succeeded him as Commander of the Faithful. Within a single decade his armies shattered two of the great powers of the age. They overthrew the Sasanian Persian empire at Qadisiyya and seized its capital, and they wrested Syria, Palestine, and Egypt from the Byzantines after the crushing victory at Yarmouk. Umar himself came to receive the surrender of Jerusalem, entering the holy city in humility.

He proved as gifted in governing as in war. He organized the conquered lands into provinces, founded garrison cities, established a register to pay stipends to the faithful, regulated taxation, and instituted the Islamic calendar that dates from the emigration to Medina. His reputation for stern justice and simple living became legendary.

In 644 he was struck down by the dagger of a disgruntled captive while leading the dawn prayer, and died soon after, having named a council to choose his successor. Sunnis revere him as a paragon of justice and resolve, and his caliphate is remembered as the moment Islam became a great civilization.
