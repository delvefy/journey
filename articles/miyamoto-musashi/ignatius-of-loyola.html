Ignatius of Loyola

Founder of the Society of Jesus (1491–1556)

Ignatius of Loyola was the soldier-turned-saint who founded the Society of Jesus and became one of the central figures of the Catholic renewal of the sixteenth century. Born in 1491 to a noble Basque family, he spent his youth as a courtier and man of arms until, at the siege of Pamplona in 1521, a cannonball shattered his leg and ended his military career.

During a long and painful convalescence he read what books he could find, the lives of Christ and the saints, and emerged transformed. Withdrawing to a cave near Manresa, he passed through intense spiritual struggle and began to set down the meditations that became his Spiritual Exercises, a guide to prayer and self-examination that would shape Catholic devotion for centuries.

Convinced he must be educated to serve, he studied at last at the University of Paris, where he gathered a band of devoted companions, among them Francis Xavier. In 1534 they vowed themselves to God's service, and in 1540 Pope Paul III formally approved their new order. Ignatius was chosen its first superior general.

From a small room in Rome he directed a fast-growing body of priests famous for discipline, learning, and obedience to the pope. The Jesuits founded schools across Europe and carried the faith to Asia and the Americas, making themselves the spearhead of the Counter-Reformation. By his death in 1556 Ignatius had built an institution whose influence on education and religion would prove immense and enduring.
