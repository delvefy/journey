Thomas Aquinas

Italian theologian and philosopher (1225–1274)

Thomas Aquinas was the greatest theologian of the Middle Ages and one of the most influential thinkers in the history of Christianity. Born to a noble family in southern Italy, he defied their wishes to join the new order of Dominican friars, devoting himself to a life of study under the renowned scholar Albert the Great.

His life's work was a vast and confident synthesis. The philosophy of Aristotle had recently flooded back into the Latin West through Arabic translations, and where some churchmen saw a pagan threat, Aquinas saw a gift. He set himself to reconcile reason with faith, arguing that the truths discovered by human reason and those revealed by God could not finally conflict, since both came from the same source.

In his immense Summa Theologica he laid out the whole of Christian doctrine with Aristotelian rigour, offering famous arguments for the existence of God and a theory of natural law that still shapes moral and legal thought. He drew, too, on the older authority of Augustine, blending the Platonic and Aristotelian streams of the tradition.

He died in 1274, his great work unfinished, and was later canonized and declared a Doctor of the Church. Dante set him among the lights of Paradise, and his system, known as Thomism, became the bedrock of Catholic philosophy, his fusion of faith and reason an enduring landmark of Western thought.
