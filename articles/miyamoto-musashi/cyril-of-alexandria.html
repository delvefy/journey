Cyril of Alexandria

Patriarch of Alexandria and theologian (c. 376–444)

Cyril of Alexandria was the patriarch who made his city the storm-centre of the great theological battles of the fifth century, a brilliant and ruthless churchman honoured as a Doctor of the Church. Succeeding his uncle as head of the powerful Alexandrian see in 412, he wielded its wealth and its zealous monks as instruments of his will.

His early years were marred by violence. He drove rivals and minorities from the city, quarrelled with the imperial governor, and presided over Alexandria when, in 415, the celebrated pagan philosopher Hypatia was set upon and butchered by a Christian mob, a deed for which his inflammatory hostility has long borne much of the blame.

His enduring fame, however, rests on doctrine. When Nestorius, the patriarch of Constantinople, denied that Mary could properly be called the Mother of God, Cyril rose as the champion of orthodoxy. At the Council of Ephesus in 431, summoned by the emperor Theodosius II, he engineered the condemnation of Nestorius and won acceptance of the title God-bearer, fixing the Church's understanding of Christ as one undivided person.

His formulations shaped Christian teaching on the Incarnation for all time, though their interpretation would spark further bitter divisions after his death. He died in 444, a saint to the orthodox and a byword for fanaticism to his enemies.
