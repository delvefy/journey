Diocletian

Roman emperor who reformed the empire (c. 244–311)

Diocletian was the Roman emperor who hauled the empire back from the brink of collapse and remade its government from top to bottom. Born to humble parents in the Balkans, he rose through the ranks of the army to be proclaimed emperor in 284, at the end of a half-century of civil war, invasion, and ruin known as the Crisis of the Third Century.

Judging the empire too vast for one man, he devised the Tetrarchy, a college of four rulers, two senior emperors and two juniors, to share the burden of defence and administration, taking the eastern half himself. He multiplied the provinces, separated civil from military command, enlarged the bureaucracy and the army, and tried to halt runaway inflation with a sweeping edict fixing maximum prices.

He also transformed the emperorship itself, cloaking it in the trappings of an absolute, almost divine monarchy far removed from the modest style of earlier rulers. In 303 he launched the last and most savage persecution of the Christians, an effort that ultimately failed to break the growing faith.

In a step almost without precedent, Diocletian voluntarily laid down power in 305 and retired to a great fortified palace on the Dalmatian coast, where, it is said, he preferred tending his vegetable garden to the cares of rule. His tetrarchic system did not long survive him, dissolving into the wars from which Constantine would rise, but his reforms gave the empire a new lease of life. He had taken up the work of restoration begun by the soldier-emperor Aurelian.
