Niccolo Machiavelli

Florentine statesman and political philosopher (1469–1527)

Niccolo Machiavelli was a Florentine diplomat and thinker whose hard-eyed analysis of power made him the founder of modern political science and lent his very name to cold, cunning statecraft. Born in 1469, he rose to serve the Republic of Florence as a secretary and envoy, carrying out missions to kings, popes, and warlords, among them the formidable Cesare Borgia, whose methods he studied at first hand.

When the Medici overthrew the republic in 1512, Machiavelli lost his post, was imprisoned and tortured on suspicion of plotting against them, and was finally banished to his small country estate. There, in forced idleness, he turned to writing the works that would immortalize him.

Chief among them was The Prince, a short, startling treatise on how rulers win and keep power, stripped of pious illusion and willing to justify ruthlessness by results. Dedicated to a member of the Medici house he hoped to serve, it drew on Cesare Borgia as its living model and scandalized later readers with its candour. In the longer Discourses he revealed himself a passionate republican, and he also wrote on the art of war, a celebrated comedy, and a history of Florence commissioned by the Medici.

Torn between his love of free republics and his fascination with the strong men who command events, Machiavelli was misunderstood and reviled for centuries. He died in 1527, but his unflinching realism about politics has never ceased to provoke, instruct, and disturb those who read him.
