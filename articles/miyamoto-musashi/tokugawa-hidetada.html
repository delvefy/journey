Tokugawa Hidetada

Second shogun of the Tokugawa shogunate (1579–1632)

Tokugawa Hidetada was the second shogun of the Tokugawa dynasty and the ruler who, in his father's shadow and then in his own right, hardened the new regime into a lasting system of control. Born in 1579 as a son of Tokugawa Ieyasu, he grew up amid the wars of unification; as a youth the overlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi bestowed on him a character of his own name.

His military record was unlucky. Marching to the decisive campaign of 1600, he was delayed besieging a fortress and arrived too late for the great battle of Sekigahara, a failure his father never forgot. Nonetheless, when Ieyasu resigned the title of shogun in 1605, he passed it to Hidetada, signalling that the office would remain hereditary in the Tokugawa house.

Real power stayed with the old man until his death in 1616, after which Hidetada governed in earnest. He took part in the destruction of the Toyotomi at Osaka, issued strict codes binding the conduct of the warrior lords, and curbed the authority of the imperial court. To bind throne and shogunate together, he married his daughter to the emperor.

In 1623 Hidetada in turn handed the shogunate to his son Iemitsu, yet continued, as his father had, to wield authority from retirement until he died in 1632. Less brilliant than the founder, he proved a methodical consolidator whose tightening of the rules helped secure the Tokugawa peace for the generations to come.
