Attila the Hun

Ruler of the Hunnic Empire (c. 406–453)

Attila was the king of the Huns whose name became a byword for barbarian terror, the scourge who made both halves of the Roman Empire tremble. He first ruled jointly with his brother Bleda, but after having him killed he reigned alone over a vast confederation of Huns and subject Germanic peoples stretching across central Europe.

For years he bled the Eastern Empire white, extorting enormous payments of gold from Constantinople, whose great walls alone kept him from the capital itself. Then he turned west. In 451 he poured into Gaul, only to be met and checked by his former ally, the Roman general Aetius, at the head of a Roman and Visigothic army, in the great and bloody battle of the Catalaunian Plains, the one true defeat of his career.

Undaunted, the next year he invaded Italy, sacking its northern cities. As he advanced toward Rome, he was met by an embassy headed by Pope Leo I; what passed between them is unknown, but Attila turned back, and the legend grew that the holy bishop had awed the conqueror into sparing the city.

In 453 Attila died suddenly on the night of a new marriage, and the empire he had built by terror dissolved almost at once among his quarrelling sons. Yet his memory lived on for centuries in the sagas of the Germanic peoples and in the nightmares of the West.
