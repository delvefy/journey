Tokugawa Ieyasu

Founder and first shogun of the Tokugawa shogunate (1543–1616)

Tokugawa Ieyasu was the warlord who completed the unification of Japan and founded the shogunate that would rule it in peace for more than two and a half centuries. Born in 1543 in the small province of Mikawa, he passed much of his childhood as a hostage of greater lords, an apprenticeship in patience that shaped his cautious, calculating character.

Winning his independence, he allied himself with Oda Nobunaga and steadily expanded his domain, surviving a near-fatal defeat by Takeda Shingen at Mikatagahara in 1572. After Nobunaga's death he submitted, warily, to the supremacy of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, accepting a transfer to the eastern Kanto plain, where he built his power around the castle town of Edo, the future Tokyo.

When Hideyoshi died in 1598, leaving an infant heir, Ieyasu moved to seize the realm. In 1600 he crushed a rival coalition at the great battle of Sekigahara, the decisive engagement of the age. Three years later the emperor named him shogun, and he set about constructing a durable order of obedient domains bound to the Tokugawa house.

He soon resigned the title to his son while keeping real authority, and in 1614 and 1615 he destroyed the last Toyotomi loyalists in the sieges of Osaka, extinguishing every rival to his line. The system he built imposed a long stability on Japan and turned Edo into one of the world's great cities. He died in 1616 and was enshrined as a god at Nikko, the patient architect of an enduring peace.
