Ahmad ibn Hanbal

Muslim jurist and theologian, founder of the Hanbali school (780–855)

Ahmad ibn Hanbal was a jurist, theologian, and master of hadith whose name marks one of the four great schools of Sunni law. Born in Baghdad in 780, he gave his life to the study of the traditions of the Prophet, travelling across the Islamic world to gather them and compiling a vast collection, the Musnad, arranged according to those who first reported each saying.

He held that the faith must rest on scripture, the Quran and the sound traditions, rather than on the speculations of reason, and this conviction soon brought him into a famous trial. The caliph al-Ma'mun and his successors embraced the doctrine that the Quran was created rather than eternal, and compelled the scholars of the realm to profess it in an inquisition known as the mihna.

Ahmad refused. Holding the Quran to be the uncreated word of God, he endured imprisonment and flogging rather than affirm what he believed false. His unbending steadfastness under persecution made him a hero to the traditionist majority.

When the inquisition was at last abandoned, his prestige stood higher than that of any scholar of his age, and crowds are said to have thronged his funeral when he died in 855. The legal and theological school that bears his name became the most rigorously traditionalist of the four, and through later followers it would shape currents of Islam down to modern times.
