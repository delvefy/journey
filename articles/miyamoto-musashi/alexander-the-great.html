Alexander the Great

King of Macedon and conqueror (356–323 BC)

Alexander the Great was the Macedonian king whose conquests carried Greek arms and culture from the Adriatic to the edges of India, and who became for all later ages the very model of the conqueror. Son of the formidable Philip II, who had forged a superb army and mastered Greece, and tutored in his youth by the philosopher Aristotle, he inherited the throne at twenty when his father was murdered.

Swiftly crushing rebellion at home, he crossed into Asia in 334 BC to make war on the vast Persian Empire. In a series of dazzling victories, at the Granicus, at Issus, and at last at Gaugamela, he shattered the armies of the Persian king Darius III and brought the greatest empire on earth crashing down. He took Babylon and Persepolis, was hailed in Egypt, where he founded the city of Alexandria, and pressed ever eastward.

Beyond the mountains he defeated the Indian king Porus on the river Hydaspes, but at last his weary troops would march no further, and he turned back. He had created, by the age of thirty, the largest empire the ancient world had known, and dreamed of welding its peoples together.

In 323 BC, back in Babylon, he fell suddenly ill and died at thirty-two, leaving no settled heir. His generals tore the empire apart, but the Hellenistic world his conquests created endured for centuries. Undefeated in battle and insatiable in ambition, Alexander inspired emulators from Caesar to Napoleon.
