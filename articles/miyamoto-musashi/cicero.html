Cicero

Roman orator, statesman, and philosopher (106–43 BC)

Marcus Tullius Cicero was the greatest orator of ancient Rome and one of the most influential prose writers in the history of the West. A man of modest birth who rose by sheer talent, he reached the consulship in 63 BC, the year in which he exposed and crushed the conspiracy of Catiline, delivering speeches studied as models of eloquence for two thousand years.

A devoted champion of the Republic and the authority of the Senate, he steered a perilous course through the age of Pompey, Crassus, and Julius Caesar. But it is his pen as much as his voice that secured his fame. In a flood of speeches, letters, and treatises he created a Latin philosophical vocabulary and carried the thought of the Greeks to the Roman world, modelling his works on the dialogues of Plato and weaving together the teachings of the Academy and the Stoa.

After Caesar's murder he made his last great stand, hurling his ferocious Philippics against Mark Antony and pinning his hopes on the young Octavian. The gamble failed. When Octavian came to terms with Antony, Cicero's name was placed on the proscription lists, and in 43 BC he was hunted down and killed, his severed head and hands displayed in the Forum he had ruled with his words.

Through the Middle Ages and the Renaissance his style and ideas shaped education, rhetoric, and political thought, and the rediscovery of his letters helped awaken the humanism of a later age.
