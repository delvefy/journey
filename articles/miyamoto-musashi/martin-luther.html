Martin Luther

German reformer, founder of Protestantism (1483–1546)

Martin Luther was the German monk and theologian whose protest against the abuses of the Church shattered the unity of Western Christendom and launched the Protestant Reformation. Born in 1483 in Saxony, he became an Augustinian friar and a professor of theology, but he was tormented by a sense of his own sinfulness and his inability to earn God's favour.

Studying the letters of the Apostle Paul, he came to a liberating conviction: that men are justified, made right with God, not by their own works but by faith alone, as a free gift of divine grace. From this insight everything else followed. In 1517 he published ninety-five theses attacking the sale of indulgences, and with the help of the printing press his challenge spread across Germany like fire.

Summoned in 1521 before the young emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms, Luther refused to recant, reportedly declaring that he could do no other. Outlawed and excommunicated, he was sheltered by a sympathetic prince and there translated the New Testament into vigorous German, a work that shaped the language itself.

He went on to reject the authority of the pope, abolish much of traditional practice, marry a former nun, and pour out a flood of sermons, hymns, and polemics, some of them, in his later years, marked by bitter intolerance. By his death in 1546 he had permanently divided the Church and reshaped the religion, politics, and culture of Europe, all upon a foundation he traced back to the Apostle Paul.
