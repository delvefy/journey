Aristotle

Greek philosopher and polymath (384–322 BC)

Aristotle was a Greek philosopher whose work laid the foundations of much of Western thought and science. Born in Stagira in northern Greece, he came as a young man to Athens and studied for some twenty years at the Academy of Plato, the most brilliant of its members, before striking out on his own path.

For a time he served at the Macedonian court as tutor to the boy who would become Alexander the Great. Returning to Athens, he founded his own school, the Lyceum, where he and his followers, walking in its colonnades, pursued an astonishing range of inquiry.

His achievement was encyclopaedic. He created the formal study of logic, wrote on physics, astronomy, and the soul, and all but founded biology by his patient observation and classification of living things. In ethics he taught that virtue lies in a mean between extremes and that happiness is the activity of a life lived well; in the Politics he analyzed the forms of the state; and his works on rhetoric and poetry shaped criticism for centuries.

Where Plato had looked beyond the visible world to eternal Forms, Aristotle sought the truth of things within the world itself, in patient examination of nature. After Alexander's death he withdrew from a hostile Athens and died in 322 BC. Revered simply as "the Philosopher," his thought would dominate medieval learning, Christian, Jewish, and Islamic alike, and it was Thomas Aquinas who wove it most fully into the theology of the Church.
