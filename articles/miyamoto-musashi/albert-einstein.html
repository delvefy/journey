Albert Einstein

German-born theoretical physicist (1879–1955)

Albert Einstein was the German-born physicist whose theories transformed our understanding of space, time, and matter, and whose very name became a synonym for genius. Working as an obscure patent clerk in 1905, he produced in a single miraculous year a series of papers that revolutionized physics, explaining the photoelectric effect, confirming the reality of atoms, and setting out his special theory of relativity, with its famous equation linking mass and energy.

A decade later he completed the general theory of relativity, which recast gravity not as a force but as the curving of spacetime by matter. When an eclipse expedition in 1919 confirmed his prediction that starlight would bend around the sun, he was overnight transformed into the most famous scientist in the world, the successor to Newton whose universe he had revised.

Though he had helped launch the quantum revolution, he could never accept its element of pure chance, and his long debates with Niels Bohr, in which he insisted that God does not play dice, became legendary. He won the Nobel Prize in 1921.

A Jew and a pacifist, he fled Nazi Germany for America, and in 1939 lent his name to a letter warning President Roosevelt that an atomic bomb might be built, a step he later regretted. He spent his final years at Princeton, a revered public conscience, and died in 1955.
