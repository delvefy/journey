Alcibiades

Athenian statesman and general (c. 450–404 BC)

Alcibiades was the most dazzling and most dangerous Athenian of his generation, a statesman and general whose brilliance, beauty, and boundless ambition carried him from triumph to treason and back again. Orphaned young, he was raised in the household of the great Pericles, and as a youth he attached himself to Socrates, who admired his gifts even as he despaired of his character; the two served together in battle and saved each other's lives.

In the long war between Athens and Sparta he rose to lead the city, and it was his soaring eloquence that persuaded the Athenians to launch their fateful expedition against Sicily in 415 BC. Recalled to stand trial on a charge of sacrilege, he chose instead to flee, and, in an astonishing betrayal, defected to Sparta and counselled his enemies on how to defeat his own city.

His career thereafter was a whirl of changing sides. He intrigued with the Persians, then engineered his return to Athens, where he won fresh victories and was welcomed as a saviour, only to be cast out once more after a reverse.

He died in exile in 404 BC, cut down by assassins as the war he had done so much to shape reached its bitter end. Through the pages of Thucydides and Plutarch, and the unforgettable scene in Plato's Symposium where he stumbles in drunk to praise Socrates, Alcibiades has remained the very type of the gifted, faithless adventurer.
