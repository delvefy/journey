Ptolemy

Greco-Roman astronomer and geographer (c. 100–170)

Claudius Ptolemy was the astronomer and geographer of Roman Alexandria whose works ruled the sciences of the heavens and the earth for more than a thousand years. In his great astronomical treatise, known by its Arabic name the Almagest, he gathered and perfected the achievements of earlier observers such as Hipparchus into a complete mathematical model of the cosmos.

His universe was built on the philosophy of Aristotle: the Earth lay motionless at the centre, and around it the sun, moon, planets, and stars wheeled on combinations of circles, the epicycles and deferents by which Ptolemy accounted, with remarkable accuracy, for the wandering of the planets. This geocentric system became the unquestioned framework of astronomy across the medieval Christian and Islamic worlds.

He was no less influential as a geographer. His Geography set out the known world in a grid of latitude and longitude and taught how to project the sphere of the earth onto a flat map, a work that guided cartographers and explorers down to the age of Columbus. He also composed a foundational manual of astrology.

For fourteen centuries the Ptolemaic system stood as the last word on the order of the heavens, edited and preserved by scholars such as Hypatia, until at last Copernicus dared to move the earth from the centre and set the sun in its place.
