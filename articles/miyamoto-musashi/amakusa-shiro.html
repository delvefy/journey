Amakusa Shiro

Leader of the Shimabara Rebellion (c. 1621–1638)

Amakusa Shiro was the charismatic young Christian who led the great Shimabara Rebellion against the Tokugawa shogunate, becoming one of the most haunting figures in Japanese history. Born around 1621 to a family connected to the service of a former Christian lord, he grew up in a region of Kyushu where the foreign faith had taken deep root before it was outlawed.

In 1637 the peasants of the Shimabara Peninsula and the Amakusa Islands, crushed by ruinous taxation and the savage persecution of their religion, rose in revolt. They proclaimed the teenage Shiro their leader, revering him as a heaven-sent boy around whom miracles were said to gather. Tens of thousands of rebels, men, women, and children, rallied to his banner.

The rebels seized and fortified the ruined castle of Hara, where some thirty-seven thousand of them withstood the shogunate's armies through the winter. Unable to storm the walls at first, the government gathered an overwhelming force of over a hundred thousand men and even called on Dutch ships to bombard the fortress from the sea.

In the spring of 1638 the starving defenders were overwhelmed, and almost all were put to the sword, Shiro among them; his severed head was displayed at Nagasaki. The rising so alarmed the rulers of Japan that they completed the country's seclusion and stamped out open Christianity for two centuries. Yet the faith Shiro died for had first been carried to Japan, nearly a century before, by the missionary Francis Xavier.
