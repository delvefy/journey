Hosokawa Tadaoki

Japanese daimyo and tea master (1563–1646)

Hosokawa Tadaoki, also known by his tea name Sansai, was a Japanese warlord, statesman, and renowned master of the tea ceremony whose long life spanned the violent unification of Japan and the peace that followed. He was the son of Hosokawa Fujitaka, himself a celebrated soldier-poet, and he came of age in the service of the great unifier Oda Nobunaga.

At Nobunaga's arrangement, Tadaoki married Tama, the daughter of the general Akechi Mitsuhide. When Mitsuhide turned on Nobunaga and destroyed him in 1582, Tadaoki refused to join his own father-in-law's revolt, choosing loyalty to the new order over family. His wife, who later embraced Christianity and is remembered as Hosokawa Gracia, died in 1600 amid the manoeuvres that preceded the decisive struggle for power.

In that struggle Tadaoki sided with Tokugawa Ieyasu, fighting on the victorious side at the great battle of Sekigahara and earning the rich domain of Kokura on the island of Kyushu. He served three successive masters of Japan in turn, surviving the perils of the age through caution, ability, and shrewd judgment.

Yet Tadaoki is remembered as much for refinement as for war. He was numbered among the seven foremost disciples of the tea master Sen no Rikyu and founded his own tradition of tea, prizing austere beauty amid an era of bloodshed. As lord of Kokura he also gave employment to noted swordsmen, among them Sasaki Kojiro. He died, full of years, in 1646.
