Herodotus

Greek historian, the Father of History (c. 484–425 BC)

Herodotus of Halicarnassus is honoured as the Father of History, the first writer to set out, in a great prose narrative, to inquire into the causes of human events and to preserve the memory of what men had done. His single surviving work, the Histories, takes as its grand theme the wars between the Greeks and the mighty empire of Persia.

A tireless traveller, he journeyed through Egypt, the Near East, and the lands around the Black Sea, gathering stories, describing the customs, monuments, and beliefs of many peoples, and filling his book with digressions, marvels, and shrewd observation. From this wealth of inquiry he wove the rise of the Persian kings, from Cyrus the Great who founded the empire down to Xerxes who hurled it against Greece.

The climax of his work is the great struggle itself, the battles of Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, and Plataea, in which the small, quarrelsome Greek cities turned back the largest empire the world had yet seen. He sought always to understand why men acted as they did, and to weigh the workings of fortune and the pride of kings.

Later historians sometimes faulted him for credulity, repeating tales he could not verify, yet none denied his breadth, his curiosity, or his humanity. A friend of the poet Sophocles, he gave to the West the very idea of history as a reasoned account of the past, and his successors would build upon the foundation he laid.
