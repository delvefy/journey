Cyrus the Great

Founder of the Achaemenid Persian Empire (c. 600–530 BC)

Cyrus the Great was the founder of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, the largest dominion the world had yet seen, stretching from the Aegean Sea to the borders of India. Beginning as the ruler of a small Persian kingdom subject to the Medes, he rose in revolt against his overlord, united the Medes and Persians under his own crown, and embarked on a career of conquest of astonishing speed.

He overthrew the fabulously wealthy king Croesus of Lydia and absorbed the Greek cities of Asia Minor, then turned against the venerable empire of Babylon, which fell to him in 539 BC. In a few years a patchwork of ancient kingdoms had become a single Persian realm.

What set Cyrus apart from earlier conquerors was the spirit of his rule. He presented himself not as a destroyer but as a liberator, respecting the gods, customs, and laws of the peoples he absorbed. Most famously, he permitted the Jews whom the Babylonians had carried into exile to return to Jerusalem and rebuild their Temple, an act that won him lasting honour in the Hebrew scriptures, where he is called the Lord's anointed.

He died around 530 BC on campaign in the far northeast of his empire. Remembered by the Greeks through the admiring portrait of Xenophon and by his own subjects as the father of his people, Cyrus left a model of enlightened kingship and an empire that his successor Darius the Great would organize and expand.
