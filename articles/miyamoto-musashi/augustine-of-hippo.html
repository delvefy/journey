Augustine of Hippo

Church Father and philosopher (354–430)

Augustine of Hippo was the most influential of the Latin Church Fathers and one of the supreme philosophers of the Western tradition. Born in Roman North Africa in 354, he was a gifted student of rhetoric who spent a restless and worldly youth pursuing ambition and pleasure, while his devout mother Monica prayed without ceasing for his soul.

His long road to faith ran through philosophy and false starts. Reading a work of Cicero first kindled his love of wisdom; for years he followed the Manichaean sect, then the teachings of the Neoplatonists. In Milan the preaching of the bishop Ambrose at last opened Christianity to him, and in a famous garden scene, taking up the letters of the Apostle Paul at a child's chant of "take and read," he was converted and soon baptized.

Returning to Africa as bishop of Hippo, he poured out a flood of sermons, letters, and books. In the Confessions he laid bare his own heart before God in the first great spiritual autobiography; in the City of God, written after Rome fell to the Goths, he set the eternal city of God against the passing cities of men.

His doctrines of sin, grace, and the will shaped all later Western theology. Thomas Aquinas built upon him, and a thousand years on it was an Augustinian friar, Martin Luther, who would draw from his teaching on grace the sparks of the Reformation. He died in 430 as barbarians besieged his city.
