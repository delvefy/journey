Ali ibn Abi Talib

Cousin of Muhammad, fourth caliph, first Shia Imam (c. 601–661)

Ali ibn Abi Talib was the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, the fourth of the so-called rightly-guided caliphs, and the figure around whom the Shia branch of Islam took shape. Raised in Muhammad's own household, he was among the very first to embrace the new faith, and his closeness to the Prophet was sealed when he married Muhammad's daughter Fatimah, by whom he fathered Hasan and Husayn.

He won renown as a fearless warrior in the early battles of Islam, and the traditions celebrate his courage, learning, piety, and eloquence. Yet when Muhammad died, the leadership of the community passed first to Abu Bakr, then to Umar, and then to Uthman, while Ali waited many years before his own turn came.

He became caliph in 656, amid the turmoil that followed Uthman's murder, and his reign was swallowed by civil war. He faced a revolt at the Battle of the Camel and then the long struggle at Siffin against Mu'awiya, the governor of Syria, a conflict that fractured the community and gave rise to the breakaway Kharijites.

In 661 one of those zealots struck him down with a poisoned blade as he prayed in the mosque at Kufa. To Sunnis he remains the last of the four upright caliphs; to Shias he is the rightful first successor of the Prophet and the first of the Imams, and his tomb at Najaf is among the most revered shrines in the Islamic world.
