Werner Heisenberg

German theoretical physicist (1901–1976)

Werner Heisenberg was a German theoretical physicist and one of the principal creators of quantum mechanics. As a young man working in the orbit of Niels Bohr, he devised in 1925 a wholly new mechanics of the atom, expressed in the unfamiliar mathematics of matrices, that broke decisively with the older pictures of electrons moving in orbits.

Two years later he announced the principle for which he is best known, the uncertainty principle: that the position and the momentum of a particle cannot both be known with perfect precision, so that nature itself sets a limit to what can be measured. This insight lay at the heart of the interpretation of quantum theory he developed with Bohr, and it won him the Nobel Prize. His formulation was soon shown to be mathematically equivalent to the wave mechanics of Erwin Schrodinger.

Heisenberg's later life was shadowed by the Second World War. Remaining in Germany, he became the leading figure in its wartime nuclear research, and his enigmatic 1941 visit to Bohr in occupied Copenhagen has been debated ever since: whether he sought the bomb and failed, or quietly held back.

Germany built no atomic weapon, and after the war Heisenberg helped rebuild his country's science and pursued a unified theory of matter. He died in 1976, his uncertainty principle having permanently altered how we understand the limits of knowledge.
