Uthman ibn Affan

Third caliph, compiler of the Quran (c. 576–656)

Uthman ibn Affan was the third of the rightly-guided caliphs and the ruler under whom the text of the Quran was given its enduring form. A wealthy merchant of the Umayyad clan of Mecca, he was among the earliest converts to Islam and one of the Prophet's most honoured Companions, married in turn to two of Muhammad's daughters, for which he was called the Possessor of Two Lights.

Renowned for his generosity, he had spent freely to support the struggling early community. When a council chosen by the dying Umar met to select a successor in 644, it turned to Uthman.

Under his rule the conquests rolled on, into North Africa, the Caucasus, and beyond Persia, and a Muslim fleet was built that wrested control of the seas from Byzantium. His most lasting act was to gather the scattered readings of the revelation into a single authoritative edition of the Quran, copies of which he sent throughout the empire, securing the uniform scripture used by Muslims ever since.

Yet his promotion of his Umayyad kinsmen to high office bred resentment, and his later years were troubled by mounting discontent. In 656 rebels besieged his house in Medina and killed the aged caliph as he sat reading the holy book. His murder opened the first great civil war of Islam, for his kinsman Mu'awiya, governor of Syria, rose to demand vengeance against the new caliph Ali.
