Constantine the Great

Roman emperor, first to embrace Christianity (c. 272–337)

Constantine the Great was the Roman emperor who turned the empire toward Christianity and founded the city that would carry Roman power for another thousand years. Hailed emperor by his troops at York in 306 on the death of his father, he spent years in civil war to make himself master of the Roman world.

The decisive moment came in 312 at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome, where, according to the famous account, he saw a sign of the Cross in the heavens with the words "in this sign conquer," and triumphed over his rival Maxentius. The following year he issued the Edict of Milan, granting Christians the freedom to worship and ending the great persecutions; in 324 he defeated his last rival and ruled alone.

Constantine became the patron of the once-outlawed faith. He summoned the bishops to the Council of Nicaea in 325 to settle disputes over the nature of Christ, producing the creed still recited today, and he raised great churches over the holy places of Christendom, while his mother Helena made her renowned pilgrimage to Jerusalem.

In 330 he dedicated a new capital on the Bosphorus, Constantinople, the New Rome, which would outlast the western empire by a millennium. Rising through and then dismantling the tetrarchic system created by Diocletian, he reshaped the army, the coinage, and the government, and bequeathed to history the model of the Christian emperor that rulers from Byzantium to Charlemagne would strive to imitate.
