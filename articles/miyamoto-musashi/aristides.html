Aristides

Athenian statesman, called "the Just" (c. 530–468 BC)

Aristides was an Athenian statesman and general of the Persian War generation whose reputation for fairness earned him the lasting title "the Just." He fought as one of the commanders at the great victory of Marathon, and in the years that followed he stood as the chief rival of the ambitious Themistocles, opposing his rush to build Athens into a naval power.

Their feud led to his ostracism, the subject of a famous anecdote: an unlettered citizen, not knowing him by sight, asked Aristides himself to scratch the name "Aristides" on the shard of pottery, explaining that he was simply weary of hearing the man everywhere called "the Just." Aristides is said to have written his own name without complaint.

Recalled when Xerxes invaded, he set rivalry aside, fought in the fleet at Salamis, and then commanded the Athenian troops in the decisive land battle at Plataea that drove the Persians from Greece for good. In the alliance formed afterward, he was entrusted with assessing the tribute each member city should pay, and he did so with a fairness so evident that all accepted his judgment.

He died around 468 BC, by tradition so poor that the state had to provide for his daughters, a man who had handled the wealth of an empire and kept none of it. Centuries later the biographer Plutarch made his life a study in incorruptible virtue.
