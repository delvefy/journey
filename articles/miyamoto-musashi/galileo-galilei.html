Galileo Galilei

Italian astronomer and physicist (1564–1642)

Galileo Galilei was the Italian scientist often called the father of modern science, a pioneer who insisted that nature be read in the language of mathematics and tested by experiment. Studying the motion of bodies, he overturned the physics of Aristotle, showing that objects of different weights fall at the same rate and laying down the principles of inertia and acceleration that Newton would later build upon.

In 1609 he turned a newly improved telescope to the night sky and unveiled wonders never before seen: mountains on the moon, the four largest moons of Jupiter circling that planet, the phases of Venus, and countless stars in the Milky Way. These discoveries struck at the old earth-centred cosmos and powerfully supported the heliocentric system of Copernicus, which Galileo openly championed.

His advocacy brought him into collision with the Church. Warned to abandon the doctrine of a moving earth, he published anyway a brilliant dialogue defending it, and in 1633 the Roman Inquisition tried him, forced him to recant on his knees, and condemned him to house arrest for the rest of his life. Legend says he murmured of the earth, "and yet it moves."

Confined and going blind, he nonetheless completed his finest work on the science of motion. He died in 1642, his reputation as a martyr of science assured, and the mathematical study of nature he had founded passed to Isaac Newton.
