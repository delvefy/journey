Erwin Schrodinger

Austrian physicist (1887–1961)

Erwin Schrodinger was the Austrian physicist who gave quantum mechanics its most enduring mathematical form. In 1926, inspired by the idea that matter has a wavelike nature, he wrote down the equation that bears his name, describing how the wave that represents a quantum system changes through time. His wave mechanics offered a picture more familiar to physicists than the abstract matrices of Heisenberg, and the two approaches were soon proved to be one and the same theory.

For this achievement he shared the Nobel Prize with Paul Dirac, and he succeeded Max Planck in the most prestigious chair of physics in Berlin, though he left Germany in disgust when the Nazis took power, eventually finding refuge in Dublin.

Uneasy with where quantum theory seemed to lead, Schrodinger devised in 1935, in exchange with Einstein, the most famous thought experiment in all of science: a cat sealed in a box with a device that might or might not have killed it, which the theory seemed to imply was both alive and dead until someone looked. He meant it to show the absurdity of the prevailing interpretation; instead it became the popular emblem of the quantum world's strangeness.

A man of wide curiosity, he also wrote a slim book asking what life is, in physical terms, that helped inspire the founders of molecular biology. He died in 1961, his equation and his paradoxical cat firmly lodged at the heart of modern physics.
