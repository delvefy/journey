Ernest Rutherford

New Zealand-born physicist, father of nuclear physics (1871–1937)

Ernest Rutherford was the New Zealand-born physicist who laid bare the structure of the atom and founded the science of nuclear physics. Trained under J.J. Thomson at Cambridge, he made his first great mark by sorting the rays given off by radioactive substances into the alpha, beta, and gamma types, and by showing, with Frederick Soddy, that radioactivity is the transmutation of one element into another, work that won him a Nobel Prize.

His most celebrated discovery came in 1911. Firing alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil, his team found that a few bounced almost straight back, as though, he said, a shell had rebounded from tissue paper. From this he deduced that the atom is mostly empty space, with nearly all its mass concentrated in a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.

This nuclear atom was the foundation on which his young collaborator Niels Bohr would build a new model of matter. Rutherford went on to be the first to split the atom by artificial means and to identify the proton, and as director of the Cavendish Laboratory he trained a galaxy of future Nobel laureates.

A booming, energetic man who had worked alongside the great names of radioactivity, the Curies among them, he died in 1937, honoured as one of the supreme experimenters in the history of science, with an element later named in his memory.
