Harun al-Rashid

Abbasid caliph at the height of the golden age (763–809)

Harun al-Rashid was the fifth Abbasid caliph and the ruler under whom Baghdad reached the dazzling height of its wealth, power, and culture. Reigning from 786, he presided over an empire that stretched from North Africa to Central Asia, its capital a teeming city of merchants, poets, and scholars, and the richest in the world outside China.

His court became a byword for splendour. He patronized learning and the arts and set in motion the gathering of knowledge that his son al-Ma'mun would carry further. For years the brilliant Barmakid family governed as his viziers, until in a sudden and still-mysterious stroke he destroyed them and seized their wealth.

Harun's fame spread far beyond his own lands. He exchanged embassies and magnificent gifts with the Frankish emperor Charlemagne, sending him, among other wonders, an elephant and an intricate water clock that astonished the Western court, and he treated with and warred against the Byzantines who pressed his northern frontier.

Above all, the memory of his reign was transmuted into legend. In the tales of the One Thousand and One Nights he wanders the night streets of Baghdad in disguise, dispensing justice and reward, the very image of the wise and capricious caliph. His death in 809 opened a bitter civil war between his sons, but the golden age over which he had presided became one of the most romanticized eras in all of history.
