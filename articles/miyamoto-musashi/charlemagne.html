Charlemagne

King of the Franks and Emperor of the Romans (c. 748–814)

Charlemagne, Charles the Great, was the Frankish king who united much of Western Europe under a single rule for the first time since antiquity and earned the title Father of Europe. Succeeding his father Pepin in 768, he spent decades at war, conquering the Lombards of Italy, subduing the Saxons in long and bloody campaigns that forced them to accept Christianity, and crushing the Avars of the Danube, until his realm stretched from the Pyrenees to the Elbe.

On Christmas Day in the year 800, in Rome, Pope Leo III set a crown upon his head and acclaimed him Emperor, reviving in the West the imperial dignity that had lapsed for more than three centuries. Charlemagne thus presented himself as a Christian successor to the Roman emperors, a new Constantine ruling a renewed Christian empire, and his coronation laid the seed of what would become the Holy Roman Empire.

He was more than a conqueror. From his palace at Aachen he summoned scholars from across Europe and fostered a revival of learning, the Carolingian Renaissance, that reformed handwriting, copied and preserved ancient texts, and promoted schools and education throughout his lands. He reorganized administration, coinage, and law, and exchanged embassies with distant rulers, among them the caliph Harun al-Rashid.

When he died at Aachen in 814, his empire passed to his son and then fragmented among his grandsons, but the idea he embodied, of a united Christian Europe heir to Rome, would shape the continent for a thousand years.
