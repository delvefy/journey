Mu'awiya I

Founder of the Umayyad Caliphate (c. 602–680)

Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan was the founder of the Umayyad Caliphate, the first of Islam's hereditary dynasties, and one of the most astute statesmen of the early Muslim world. He belonged to the Umayyad clan of Mecca, sons of the very aristocracy that had at first resisted Muhammad, but he came into the faith and served the Prophet as a scribe.

Appointed governor of Syria, he ruled Damascus for two decades, building a loyal army and the first Arab navy. When his kinsman, the caliph Uthman, was murdered in 656, Mu'awiya refused to accept the new caliph Ali and called for vengeance. Their armies clashed at the indecisive battle of Siffin, and the failed arbitration that followed split the community and deepened the first great civil war of Islam.

After Ali was assassinated in 661, his son Hasan came to terms with Mu'awiya and yielded the caliphate to him, leaving Mu'awiya sole master of the Muslim empire. From his capital at Damascus he governed with skill and pragmatism, conciliating rivals, pressing the frontier against Byzantium, and binding the fractious Arab tribes to his rule.

His most fateful decision was to name his own son Yazid as his heir, founding a dynasty but breaking with the older custom of choosing a leader by consensus. The move was bitterly resented, and soon after Mu'awiya's death in 680 it would lead to the killing of the Prophet's grandson Husayn, a wound in Islam that has never wholly healed.
