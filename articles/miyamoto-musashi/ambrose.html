Ambrose

Bishop of Milan, Father of the Church (c. 339–397)

Ambrose was the bishop of Milan whose eloquence and courage made him one of the most commanding churchmen of the late Roman world and one of the four great Latin Doctors of the Church. A Roman provincial governor by training, he was thrust into the bishopric in 374 when, sent merely to keep order at a contested election, he was suddenly acclaimed bishop by the crowd, though he had not yet even been baptized.

He grew into the role with formidable energy, mastering theology, championing Nicene orthodoxy against the Arians, and preaching sermons of such power that they helped win the restless Augustine to the faith; it was Ambrose who baptized him.

Most famously, Ambrose insisted that even emperors stood under the moral authority of the Church. He refused to surrender a basilica to heretics, faced down imperial demands, and, after the emperor Theodosius ordered a massacre of the citizens of Thessalonica, barred him from communion until he performed public penance for the deed, an act long remembered as a turning point in the relations of Church and state.

A reformer of worship who promoted congregational hymn-singing, and a writer whose works shaped Western Christianity, Ambrose died in 397, mourned as a pillar of the faith and a model of the fearless bishop.
