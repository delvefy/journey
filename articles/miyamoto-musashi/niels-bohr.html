Niels Bohr

Danish physicist, founder of quantum theory (1885–1962)

Niels Bohr was the Danish physicist who, more than any other, shaped the strange new world of the quantum. Coming to work with Rutherford just as the nuclear atom was discovered, he proposed in 1913 a daring model in which electrons circle the nucleus only in fixed orbits and leap between them by absorbing or emitting precise packets of energy. The scheme explained the riddle of atomic spectra and won him the Nobel Prize.

From his institute in Copenhagen, which became the gathering place of the world's young physicists, Bohr presided over the creation of quantum mechanics. With his protégé Werner Heisenberg he developed the interpretation that bears the city's name, insisting that at the deepest level nature is governed by probability and that complementary descriptions of reality must be held together.

This conviction set him against Albert Einstein in a series of profound and good-natured debates over the meaning of the new physics, debates that probed the very foundations of knowledge and that physicists still ponder.

When the Nazis occupied Denmark, Bohr, of partly Jewish descent, escaped by sea and air and lent his counsel to the Allied atomic effort, though afterward he campaigned earnestly for international openness and the peaceful control of nuclear weapons. He died in 1962, revered as a sage of science, with an element named in his honour.
