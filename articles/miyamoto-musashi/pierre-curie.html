Pierre Curie

French physicist (1859–1906)

Pierre Curie was a French physicist of the first rank whose name is joined forever to his wife Marie and to the discovery of radioactivity. Even before their partnership he had made notable contributions to science: with his brother he discovered piezoelectricity, the generation of electric charge by crystals under pressure, and he established the law, still bearing his name, governing how a material's magnetism changes with temperature.

In 1895 he married Marie Sklodowska, and their union became one of the most fruitful collaborations in the history of science. Turning to the strange rays emitted by uranium, the pair toiled together to isolate two new radioactive elements, polonium and radium, and to probe the nature of the radiation itself, even at the cost of burns to their own bodies.

For this work Pierre shared the Nobel Prize in Physics of 1903 with Marie and with Henri Becquerel, and he was at last given a professorship at the Sorbonne. The honours had come slowly to a man indifferent to fame, content with his laboratory.

His life was cut short in 1906 when he slipped on a rainy Paris street and was crushed beneath a horse-drawn wagon. He was only forty-six. The science of radioactivity he had helped found passed into the hands of a new generation, and above all to Ernest Rutherford, who would lay bare the structure of the atom.
