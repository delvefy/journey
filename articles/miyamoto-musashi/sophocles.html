Sophocles

Athenian tragic dramatist (c. 497–406 BC)

Sophocles was one of the three great tragic poets of ancient Athens, and to many the most perfect of them. Living nearly the whole of the fifth century BC, he saw the city rise to its golden age and decline into war, and through it all he wrote for the stage, composing well over a hundred plays and winning the dramatic competitions more often than any rival.

Only seven of his tragedies survive whole, but they are among the supreme works of world literature. In Oedipus the King he told of the man who, struggling to escape a dreadful prophecy, fulfils it unknowing, killing his father and marrying his mother; in Antigone he set the claims of conscience against the commands of the state. His mastery of dramatic irony and his profound, dignified vision of human suffering set the standard for tragedy ever after.

He enriched the art itself, adding a third actor to the stage and deepening the portrayal of character, so that his people seem to act from within rather than at the mercy of the gods alone.

Sophocles was no mere recluse of the theatre. A respected citizen of Periclean Athens, he served as a general and held high public office, and he counted among his friends the historian Herodotus. He died, full of years and honour, around 406 BC, and the Athenians long revered his memory.
