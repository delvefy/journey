Abu Bakr

Companion of Muhammad, first caliph (c. 573–634)

Abu Bakr was the closest friend and companion of the Prophet Muhammad and, after the Prophet's death, the first of the caliphs who led the Muslim community. A respected merchant of Mecca, he was among the earliest converts to Islam, lending his wealth and standing to the persecuted faith and purchasing the freedom of enslaved believers. His devotion was so complete that he accompanied Muhammad on the perilous emigration to Medina, the two hiding together in a cave from their pursuers.

When Muhammad died in 632, leaving no undisputed successor, the community turned to Abu Bakr, who was acclaimed as caliph, the deputy of the Prophet. The choice was not accepted by all at once, for some looked to Ali, but Abu Bakr's authority soon held.

His brief reign was decisive. Many Arabian tribes, thinking their allegiance had died with Muhammad, fell away or rose in revolt, and Abu Bakr crushed these uprisings in the hard-fought Wars of Apostasy, holding the young state together. He then loosed his armies against the great empires of Byzantium and Persia, beginning the astonishing conquests that would transform the world.

He also took the first steps to gather the scattered revelations into a single text of the Quran. Ruling only two years, he died in 634, having named Umar to follow him. Sunnis honour him as the truthful one, the foremost of the Prophet's Companions and the model of a faithful successor.
