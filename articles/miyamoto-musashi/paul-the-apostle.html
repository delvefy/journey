Paul the Apostle

Apostle to the Gentiles, founder of Christian theology (c. 5–c. 65)

Paul the Apostle was, after Jesus himself, the most influential figure in the history of Christianity, the missionary and thinker who carried the new faith out of Judaea and into the wider world. Born Saul, a Jew of Tarsus and a Roman citizen, he was trained as a strict Pharisee and at first persecuted the followers of Jesus with zeal.

Everything changed on the road to Damascus, where, according to the account he gave, he was struck down by a vision of the risen Christ and rose a believer. From persecutor he became the tireless apostle, travelling thousands of miles across Asia Minor and Greece, preaching, founding churches, and enduring beatings, shipwreck, and imprisonment.

His most consequential conviction was that the message of Jesus was meant for all peoples, not the Jews alone, and that Gentile converts need not first take on the whole Law of Moses. This bold position, which set him at odds even with other leaders of the early movement, transformed a Jewish sect into a universal religion.

In a series of letters to the communities he founded, the earliest Christian writings of all, Paul worked out the ideas of grace, faith, and salvation that would shape Christian theology forever after and that men like Augustine and Luther would later make their own. Taken at last to Rome, he was, by ancient tradition, put to death there under the emperor Nero. His thought has echoed through two thousand years of Western religion.
