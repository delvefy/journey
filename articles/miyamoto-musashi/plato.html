Plato

Athenian philosopher (c. 428–348 BC)

Plato was an Athenian philosopher whose thought lies at the very foundation of Western philosophy. Born into an aristocratic family during the turmoil of Athens's war with Sparta, he fell under the spell of the philosopher Socrates, and the trial and execution of his master by the Athenian democracy in 399 BC marked him for life, turning him from politics toward the search for truth.

He preserved and transformed Socrates's legacy in a series of dialogues of unmatched literary and intellectual power, in most of which Socrates himself is the chief speaker. In the Republic he imagined a just city ruled by philosopher-kings and painted the famous allegory of the cave; in other dialogues he explored love, knowledge, language, and the immortality of the soul.

At the heart of his philosophy lay the theory of Forms, the conviction that beyond the shifting world of the senses lies a realm of perfect, eternal realities of which earthly things are mere shadows. Around 387 BC he founded the Academy in Athens, a school of philosophy and mathematics often called the first university, where he taught for decades; among his students was Aristotle.

His influence is almost beyond measure. Neoplatonism shaped late antiquity, his ideas were woven into Christian and Islamic thought, and a modern philosopher could quip that all later European philosophy is but a series of footnotes to Plato. Through writers such as Cicero his dialogues passed into the broader culture of the West, where they have never ceased to be read.
