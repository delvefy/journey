Tokugawa Iemitsu

Third shogun of the Tokugawa shogunate (1604–1651)

Tokugawa Iemitsu, third shogun of the Tokugawa line, was the ruler who carried his house's authority to its height and sealed Japan off from much of the world for over two centuries. Born in 1604, the grandson of the founder Ieyasu and son of Hidetada, he is said to have reminded the great lords that, unlike his forebears, he had been born to rule rather than having won power by the sword.

He governed by binding the warrior aristocracy ever more tightly to Edo. Under his system of alternate attendance, the domain lords were compelled to divide their years between their provinces and the shogun's capital, leaving their wives and children behind as effective hostages, an arrangement that drained their wealth and guaranteed their obedience.

Iemitsu's most fateful policy was seclusion. Fearful of Christianity and foreign meddling, he expelled the Portuguese, confined the Dutch to a single tiny island at Nagasaki, forbade his own subjects to leave the country, and persecuted native Christians without mercy. When desperate peasants and Christians rose in the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637, rallying behind the youth Amakusa Shiro, his armies besieged and slaughtered them in their tens of thousands.

A devoted grandson, he poured vast resources into rebuilding the shrine at Nikko where Ieyasu was worshipped as a god. By his death in 1651 the shogunate stood unchallenged at home and the policy of isolation was fixed, shaping the course of Japanese life until the nineteenth century.
