Isaac Newton

English mathematician and physicist (1643–1727)

Isaac Newton was the English mathematician and physicist whose discoveries founded modern science and held sway over it for more than two centuries. Born in rural Lincolnshire and educated at Cambridge, he passed the plague years of the 1660s in seclusion at his family farm, and in that short span he laid the seeds of calculus, unravelled the nature of light, and began to conceive his theory of gravity, prompted, by the famous story, by a falling apple.

His experiments with prisms showed that white light is a mixture of colours, and he built the first reflecting telescope. In mathematics he developed the calculus, a tool that transformed the sciences, though a bitter dispute over priority would set him against the German philosopher Leibniz.

His supreme achievement was the Principia of 1687, in which he set out three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, showing that the same force that draws an apple to the ground holds the moon in its orbit and governs the planets. At a stroke he united the heavens and the earth under a single mathematical order, explaining the laws Kepler had found and much besides.

Honoured in his lifetime as no scientist before him, knighted and made master of the mint, he also poured vast energy into alchemy and theology. The universe he described stood unchallenged until, two centuries later, Albert Einstein reimagined space, time, and gravity itself.
