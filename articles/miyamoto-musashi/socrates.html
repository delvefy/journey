Socrates

Athenian philosopher (c. 470–399 BC)

Socrates was the Athenian philosopher who turned thought toward the examination of how one ought to live, and who is honoured as a founding figure of Western philosophy. He wrote nothing himself; we know him through the dialogues of his pupil Plato, the memoirs of Xenophon, and the mocking caricature of the comic poet Aristophanes.

A stonemason's son, he lived through the golden age of Athens and the long agony of its war with Sparta, serving bravely as a soldier. But his true vocation was philosophy in the streets and marketplaces, where he buttonholed citizens and, by relentless questioning, exposed the emptiness of their confident opinions about virtue, justice, and piety. This method of probing question and answer, drawing wisdom out by revealing ignorance, still bears his name.

He professed to know only that he knew nothing, yet the oracle at Delphi had called him the wisest of men. He gathered about him a circle of brilliant and sometimes dangerous young followers, among them Plato and the dazzling Alcibiades, and made himself a gadfly stinging the conscience of the city.

In 399 BC, with Athens defeated and bitter, he was brought to trial on charges of impiety and corrupting the young. Condemned, he refused to flee or beg, and calmly drank the cup of hemlock, discoursing on the immortality of the soul to the end. His death made him the eternal symbol of the philosopher who would rather die than abandon the life of reason.
