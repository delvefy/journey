Darius the Great

King of the Achaemenid Persian Empire (550–486 BC)

Darius the Great was the third and perhaps the ablest of the great kings of Achaemenid Persia, the ruler who turned Cyrus's sprawling conquests into a durable and well-ordered empire. He seized the throne in 522 BC amid the confusion that followed the death of Cyrus's son, putting down a usurper and a host of rebellions, and securing his legitimacy by marrying a daughter of Cyrus and by carving the record of his triumph high on the cliff at Behistun.

His genius lay in organization. He divided the vast realm into provinces under governors called satraps, fixed their tribute, struck a reliable gold coinage, and built the great Royal Road along which royal couriers sped across the empire. He raised the splendid new capital of Persepolis and cut a canal joining the Nile to the Red Sea.

He pushed the frontiers outward, into the Indus valley in the east and across into Europe against the Scythians and Thracians. But it was his collision with the Greeks that history best remembers. When the Greek cities of Asia Minor rose in revolt with Athenian help, Darius sent a fleet to punish Athens.

That expedition met disaster on the plain of Marathon in 490 BC, where the Athenians turned back the Persian host. Darius prepared a greater invasion but died in 486 BC before he could launch it, leaving the task, and the grudge, to his son Xerxes.
