Manuel I Komnenos

1118 – 1180, Byzantine emperor, last great prince of the Komnenian dynasty

Manuel was born at Constantinople on 28 November 1118, three months after his father’s accession, the fourth son of John II Komnenos and the empress Eirene of Hungary. He grew up in the shadow of his three older brothers, none of whom he expected to outlive; the imperial succession in his boyhood was understood to lie with the eldest, Alexios. By his early twenties he had served on his father’s Anatolian campaigns and been judged an effective junior commander, but no plan of inheritance involved him. The two elder brothers died within months of each other in 1142, both on campaign in Asia: Alexios at Attaleia of fever, Andronikos shortly afterwards under circumstances the sources do not explain. The third brother Isaac, who as the surviving senior remained at Constantinople, expected the succession by primogeniture. At the camp of Tarsus in April 1143, in the days before his death from a hunting wound, John II nevertheless named the youngest of his sons his successor. Manuel was acclaimed by the army, sent the trusted minister John Axouch ahead to seize the capital before Isaac could move, and entered Constantinople in the autumn as emperor. He was twenty-four.

The reign was thirty-seven years long, conducted in restless personal motion across every frontier of the empire. His father had left a recovered treasury, an intact field army, and a clear strategic doctrine of methodical expansion at the expense of Anatolian, Armenian, and Syrian neighbours. Manuel inherited the resources and abandoned the doctrine. The vision of his reign, conceived very early and held with extraordinary tenacity, was the recovery by the eastern emperor of his theoretical primacy over the entire former Roman world — including the Latin West, including Italy — through a mixture of war, diplomacy, dynastic marriage, money, and the personal charisma of a brilliant young sovereign. The wars his father had been content to win at the edges of the empire became under Manuel campaigns in Italy, in the crusader states, in Hungary, in Croatia, simultaneously and in every direction. The court ceased to be the austere house of his father’s memory and became the most ostentatious of medieval Europe: tournaments on the Latin model in the Hippodrome, horses and ladies and tournaments, knights of the German and French and Italian and Norman aristocracy received and feasted and married to Byzantine cousins, the rhetors of the imperial chancery encouraged to bring out the panegyrics on the new Justinian.

The Second Crusade passed through his empire in 1147–1148, in two great unwieldy armies under his Western allies Conrad III of Germany and the French king Louis VII. He fed them, ferried them across the Bosphorus, watched them be broken by the Seljuks in Anatolia, and emerged with his own position improved at every margin. The Norman king Roger II of Sicily, in the same year, took the opportunity of the crusade to raid Corinth and Thebes, carrying off the Greek silk-weavers who would found the Sicilian silk industry; Manuel pursued an Italian war against him through the 1150s, in which he briefly recovered Bari and Trani in 1155 in alliance with the rebellious Apulian barons, and which ended with the failure of his Italian dream by 1158. His marriage in 1146 to Bertha of Sulzbach, sister-in-law of Conrad III — renamed Eirene at the Byzantine baptism — was contracted to seal his German alliance against the Normans; she bore him a daughter, Maria, and died of fever in 1159. His second marriage, in December 1161, was to Maria of Antioch, daughter of his ally Raymond of Poitiers; the bride, of remarkable Latin beauty and exclusively Frankish manners, gave him his long-awaited son Alexios in 1169.

His policy on the eastern frontier was an extended assertion of imperial overlordship over the principalities of Antioch and Cilicia and the kingdom of Jerusalem. In 1158, after the Armenian Toros II and the Latin prince Reynald of Châtillon had refused to keep the peace, he marched into Cilicia at the head of the field army, broke the Armenian resistance in a week, and at Mamistra accepted the submission of Reynald, who walked barefoot through the gates with the cord of penitents around his neck and the imperial banner of Constantinople carried before him. He entered Antioch the next year as overlord, and Baldwin III of Jerusalem followed within months to negotiate the alliance of his kingdom with the empire on terms that all but acknowledged Byzantine seniority. The marriage of the king’s nephew Amalric I, ten years later, to Manuel’s grandniece Maria Komnene was the principal Latin-Byzantine diplomatic event of the 1160s.

Hungary and the Balkan slavs occupied him through the next two decades. By a series of campaigns from 1162 to 1167 he installed his protege Bela — later Bela III — at the Hungarian court, having brought him to Constantinople as a boy of fourteen and trained him as a Byzantine prince and prospective heir to the eastern throne. After the birth of his own son Alexios in 1169 Manuel sent Bela back to Hungary as king with a Byzantine wife and a Byzantine education, and Hungary remained a quiet ally for the rest of his reign. The Serbs and Croats were brought to nominal vassalage; the principality of Halych on the Carpathian frontier accepted Byzantine arbitration; Bulgarian unrest, which had simmered since the annexations of the previous century, was suppressed in 1166.

The disaster of the reign was the Anatolian campaign of 1176. Through the 1170s the Seljuk sultanate of Rum under Kilij Arslan II had reunified the Turkish emirates of the central plateau and threatened to overrun the recovered western coast. Manuel resolved to do what no Byzantine emperor since Romanos IV at Manzikert a century before had attempted: to march across the plateau to the sultan’s capital at Iconium and impose terms by occupation. The army — perhaps thirty thousand strong — entered the mountain pass of Myriokephalon, near Lake Beysehir, on the morning of 17 September 1176. Kilij Arslan had laid every condition of the engagement in advance: the long line of march was ambushed by Turkish horsemen along the entire length of the pass, the imperial baggage train was destroyed, the cavalry was broken from the front. Manuel escaped only because his Hungarian and Latin retainers held the rearguard. The empire signed a punitive truce; the project of recovering Anatolia by direct campaign was abandoned for good. The historian Niketas Choniates, who as a young court official was present, judged the defeat the political end of the Komnenian programme.

Manuel lived four more years in declining health. His ten-year-old son Alexios was the heir of an empire whose alliances had been built on the personal weight of an adult emperor; the regency of his Latin wife Maria of Antioch would alienate the Greek population of the capital almost from the first day. He died at Constantinople on 24 September 1180 and was buried beside his father in the Pantokrator. Within three years his line was broken: his cousin Andronikos, the most dangerous and most charismatic figure of the family, returned from his Asian exiles, took the capital in a popular revolution against the Latin regency, murdered the boy emperor and married his French widow, and ruled for two years before being overthrown and killed in turn. The Komnenian dynasty ended with him. Manuel’s reign was the high noon of Byzantine ambition, the last age in which the empire could plausibly dream of taking back the West; the disasters that followed in the next quarter-century, ending with the Latin sack of 1204, would be measured against the world he had assembled and lost.
