Louis I, Duke of Orléans

1372 – 1407, brother of Charles VI, husband of Valentina Visconti, murdered in a Paris street by the agents of his Burgundian cousin

Louis was born on 13 March 1372 at the Hôtel Saint-Pol in Paris, the second surviving son of King Charles V and Joanna of Bourbon. His elder brother, the future Charles VI, was four years older; the two were raised together by their father, who was politically the ablest of the Valois kings and who deliberately cultivated in his sons an unusual fraternal closeness. The brothers shared tutors, books, and chambers, and Louis was given the duchy of Touraine and then, in 1392, the duchy of Orléans, both intended as appanages of the royal house to be held by the cadet line in support of the crown.

His youth at his brother's court was extravagant, witty, and politically forward. He was given a thorough humanist education by his father's library and by tutors like Évrart de Trémaugon, was friendly with the great literary figures of the late fourteenth-century French court including Christine de Pizan, and dressed and danced and spent in the manner of an Italian Renaissance prince half a century early. The court was already addicted to spectacle: in January 1393, at a wedding at the Hôtel de Saint-Pol, Louis was a participant in the catastrophic Bal des Ardents, the Ball of the Burning Men, when six dancers including the king himself were set on fire by a torch carelessly held by Louis or by an attendant of his; the king was saved when the Duchess of Berry threw her skirts over his costume, four of the dancers burned to death, and the queen Isabeau collapsed.

His marriage in 1389 to Valentina Visconti, daughter of Gian Galeazzo Visconti, the first Duke of Milan, was a major Visconti-Valois alliance. Valentina brought him the rich county of Asti in Piedmont, the friendship of the great Visconti court, and a claim to the duchy of Milan that would be reactivated by his son Charles d'Orléans and by his grandson Louis XII a century later. The marriage produced four sons, including Charles d'Orléans, the poet-prince captured at Agincourt and held in England for twenty-five years, and John of Orléans, Count of Angoulême, grandfather of the future Francis I. Valentina was intelligent, devoted to Louis, and disliked at court by Queen Isabeau, who suspected her, possibly with justice, of using the cultivated familiar magic of the Lombard courts to soothe her brother-in-law's intermittent madness, possibly without it.

For in August 1392, on the road to Le Mans, Charles VI had suffered the first attack of the mental illness that would recur intermittently for the next thirty years. The king, riding with his troops in summer heat in chain armour, suddenly attacked his own attendants in the woods of Mans, killed four of his own knights, and had to be subdued and carried home. The episode was the first of perhaps forty attacks of varying duration over his life, between which he was lucid and exercised the royal authority normally, during which he did not always know who he was and from time to time believed himself to be made of glass and refused to be touched. The mental incapacity of the king created a permanent political vacuum and made the question of who held the royal council the central question of French politics for thirty years.

Louis stepped into the vacuum. His uncles — Philip the Bold of Burgundy, John of Berry, and Louis of Anjou — had governed during Charles VI's minority and resisted being pushed aside. Philip the Bold of Burgundy in particular regarded the conciliar government as his by hereditary right. From the king's first attacks of madness, Louis acted as the senior royal prince, taking the principal lordships in the council, claiming the principal share of the tax receipts, drawing the queen Isabeau of Bavaria, after the early 1400s, into close personal alliance with him. Rumour, fed by Burgundian propagandists and by the queen's own bad reputation, made the alliance into an adulterous one and the queen's youngest children Louis's own; modern historians have generally rejected the charge as Burgundian black propaganda. The political alliance was real.

By the early 1400s Louis was the de facto first man of France during his brother's lucid intervals as well as his disturbed ones. He raised taxes on the demesne to pay for an extravagant household and a large clientele, built grand châteaux at Pierrefonds, Coucy, La Ferté-Milon, and Montargis, and led, with mixed results, a French armed intervention in support of Pope Benedict XIII at Rome in 1404. His policy in the war with England was forward and aristocratic; he challenged Henry IV of England personally to a duel in 1402; he sponsored Owain Glyndŵr's revolt in Wales; he wanted, when the truce of 1400 lapsed, an open war.

Philip the Bold of Burgundy died in April 1404. His son and successor John the Fearless was three years older than Louis and politically more ruthless. The struggle for the regency now between cousins took on the bitterness of rivalry between near contemporaries. John courted the Paris guilds, denounced Louis's taxation, made himself the popular tribune; Louis countered with refined court spectacle and royal pensions. The two were repeatedly reconciled, at Saint-Denis in late 1405, at Compiègne the next year, and finally at the king's table in November 1407 with the consecrated host between them. Three days later, on the evening of 23 November, Louis was returning from a private visit to the queen at the Hôtel Barbette in the rue Vieille-du-Temple, escorted by a few servants, when a band of masked men with axes set on him. His left hand was severed when he raised it in defence; his head was split open against the cobbles; he was thirty-five years old.

The murderers fled to the Hôtel d'Artois, John the Fearless's residence, and were sheltered there. The investigation by the provost of Paris uncovered the trail within forty-eight hours. John appeared before the royal council on 25 November and acknowledged that he had ordered the killing, declaring that the late duke had been a tyrant whose elimination was a public benefit. Within four months his theologian Jean Petit had pronounced before the king and court the long Latin Justification defending the murder as tyrannicide. Louis's widow Valentina rode to Paris in mourning to demand justice, prostrating herself before her brother-in-law in his moments of lucidity; she could obtain nothing and died of grief a year later, leaving the legend, frequently repeated by her descendants, that the day of her death she had whispered rien ne m'est plus, plus ne m'est rien — nothing matters to me now, now nothing matters to me.

The political consequence of his murder was the civil war between Armagnacs (the new name of the Orleanist party, after Bernard VII of Armagnac, the father-in-law of Louis's son Charles) and Burgundians that ran from 1407 to 1435 and that, by dividing the kingdom against itself, made possible Henry V's invasion of 1415 and the Treaty of Troyes of 1420. The personal score was settled in 1419 when the Dauphin's escort killed John the Fearless on the bridge of Montereau. The political settlement followed in 1435 at Arras, twenty-eight years after the duke's death in the Marais. The bridge between Louis's career and the wars that destroyed France was almost geometric.
