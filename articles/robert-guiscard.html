Robert Guiscard

c. 1015 – 1085, Duke of Apulia, Calabria and Sicily, Norman conqueror of southern Italy

Robert was born about 1015 at the Hauteville fief near Coutances in Normandy, the eldest son of Tancred of Hauteville’s second marriage, to Fressenda; he was therefore the sixth or seventh of the dozen Hauteville brothers and had no realistic prospect of inheritance. About 1047, in his early thirties and after the elder brothers had already made themselves the leading mercenary captains of southern Italy, he followed them south. The country he entered was a patchwork of small Lombard principalities — Salerno, Benevento, Capua — sandwiched between the residual Byzantine catepanate of Apulia on the Adriatic coast and the Muslim emirates of Sicily across the strait. The Normans were established at Aversa under Rainulf Drengot and at Melfi under William Iron-Arm, where they had set themselves up as the military aristocracy of a small republican council; Robert was offered no command and went to make a brigand’s career in Calabria. The chronicler Geoffrey Malaterra preserves the nickname his Calabrian victims gave him: Viscardus, “the cunning one,” an epithet his contemporaries seem to have considered fully earned.

The breakthrough came in 1057. His half-brother Humphrey, count of Apulia, died and on his deathbed entrusted his sons and his county to Robert; the Melfi assembly later that year elected Robert count in their place. Two years later Pope Nicholas II, in an extraordinary inversion of papal policy that had previously regarded the Normans as bandits to be cursed and expelled, came to the council of Melfi in August 1059 and there invested Robert — by then duke by his own assumption — with Apulia, Calabria, and the “future Sicily” as a papal fief, in return for a yearly tribute and a feudal oath. The Treaty of Melfi turned the Hautevilles overnight from excommunicates into the most reliable Italian arm of the reform papacy, and it transformed Robert’s career: he now had a title and a programme. By the end of 1060 the last Byzantine garrisons of Calabria were swept up; Reggio fell in 1060; in 1061 he crossed the strait with his youngest brother Roger and began the conquest of Muslim Sicily, taking Messina that May and Palermo, jointly with Roger after a five-month siege, in January 1072. The remaining Byzantine fortresses of Apulia were reduced in the same decade; Bari, the last and greatest of them, surrendered in April 1071 after a siege of two and a half years, ending five centuries of Byzantine rule on the Italian mainland.

By the mid-1070s the Hauteville lands had absorbed all the Lombard principalities of the south except Salerno, which Robert took in 1076–1077 from his own brother-in-law Gisulf II, replacing him as prince of Salerno and transferring the seat of his own government there. His matrimonial career had advanced this consolidation. About 1051 he had married Alberada of Buonalbergo, a Norman heiress whose connections delivered him retainers; she bore him a son, Bohemond, and a daughter, before the marriage was annulled in 1058 on the convenient ground of consanguinity. He immediately married Sichelgaita, sister of Gisulf II of Salerno, who gave him entry into the Lombard nobility of the south and bore him eight children, of whom the second son Roger, called Borsa, was destined to inherit the duchy. Sichelgaita herself was the most remarkable of his consorts: Anna Komnene, who saw her in Albania in 1081, describes her riding fully armoured beside her husband in the line of battle, rallying the broken Norman centre at Dyrrhachium by sword and voice. She survived him by ten years and acted as the political guarantor of Roger Borsa’s succession against his elder half-brother.

Robert’s relations with Rome alternated between alliance and open war. Pope Alexander II, Nicholas’s successor, renewed the Melfi investiture; his successor Gregory VII excommunicated Robert in 1074 for his refusal to halt the conquest of papal client territories and for his support of imperial sympathisers in Salerno. The two were reconciled at Ceprano in June 1080, by which time Gregory was in open quarrel with the German king Henry IV and badly needed an Italian protector. The protection was called in four years later. In March 1084 Henry IV captured Rome, expelled Gregory, who took refuge in Castel Sant’Angelo, and arranged the coronation of the antipope Clement III at St. Peter’s. Robert marched north from Apulia with a Norman, Lombard, and Sicilian-Muslim army of perhaps thirty-six thousand, broke Henry’s rearguard at the gates of Rome in May, freed the pope, and then over three days allowed his army to sack the city — the worst destruction Rome had suffered since the fifth century, in which the entire region between the Lateran and the Colosseum was burned to the ground. He carried Gregory back with him to Salerno, where the pope died the next year, by tradition with the words “I have loved justice and hated iniquity, therefore I die in exile.”

For the last four years of his life Robert was engaged in the great undertaking of his career, the projected conquest of the Byzantine Empire. He had already in 1080 betrothed a young daughter, Helena, to the boy emperor Constantine Doukas, son of Michael VII; when in 1081 Alexios I Komnenos overthrew the Doukai and replaced Constantine with his own infant son as heir, Robert declared the change a violation of his daughter’s rights and prepared an invasion. He landed on Corfu in May 1081 with about fifteen thousand men and his elder son Bohemond in command of the vanguard; he besieged Dyrrhachium, the principal Byzantine port of the western Balkans, defeated Alexios in a great battle outside the city walls on 18 October 1081 in which Sichelgaita’s rally turned the day, and took the city by treachery in February 1082. Through 1082 and 1083 Bohemond pushed inland as far as Larissa in Thessaly while Robert was recalled to Italy by the imperial assault on Gregory VII; on returning in 1084 he relieved the imperial blockade of Corfu, broke a Venetian-Byzantine fleet, but in the camp on Cephalonia in July 1085 contracted a fever and died on 17 July, at about seventy. His body was carried back to Apulia for burial in the cathedral of Venosa, where the tomb of the Hauteville brothers still survives.

The duchy he left was rich but fragile. Roger Borsa, his nominated heir, inherited the title; Bohemond, denied any portion in Italy, would carry his father’s eastward ambition into the First Crusade and seize Antioch for himself; Calabria and Sicily remained with his brother Roger I as a separate county that would, two generations later, swallow up the duchy and become the kingdom of Sicily. Among contemporaries he was the most feared and the most successful man of his generation; a younger son with no inheritance who from sixty knights in Calabria built one of the great states of Europe in thirty years. Anna Komnene, who detested him as the great enemy of her father, devotes much of her Alexiad to him and admits without disguise his stature: “to his own people gentle, but to those who opposed him terrible; in stature so great that he overtopped the tallest, in glance a lion.”
