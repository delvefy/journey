Augustus

63 BC – AD 14 · first emperor of Rome, founder of the principate

Gaius Octavius, called Octavian after his adoption in his eighteenth year by his great-uncle Julius Caesar and proclaimed in his thirty-sixth year by the Roman Senate as Augustus, the venerable one, was the first emperor of Rome in fact and the first man in human history to have his title remembered in the way that the modern world has used it ever since. He was born on the twenty-third of September 63 BC in a house on the Palatine hill at Rome and died on the nineteenth of August AD 14 at Nola in Campania, at the age of seventy-five, after forty-five years as sole master of the Roman world.

His father, a praetorian senator of equestrian background, died when the boy was four; his mother Atia, the niece of Julius Caesar, raised him with care and ambition, and Caesar took notice. By the time of Caesar's assassination on the Ides of March 44 BC, the young Octavius was nineteen and on his way to join his great-uncle in the Balkans for the planned Parthian campaign. He learned the news in Apollonia and crossed back to Italy alone. The reading of Caesar's will, opened at Rome in his absence, disclosed that he was Caesar's adopted son and principal heir. He arrived at Rome to claim the legacy that more experienced Caesarians, Mark Antony chief among them, had already begun to dispose of without him, and within eighteen months had outmaneuvered or outlasted all of them.

The decade and a half that followed was an extended civil war fought, in succession, against the senatorial assassins of Caesar at Philippi in 42 BC, against Sextus Pompey at sea in the Sicilian campaign of 36, and finally, slowly and inevitably, against his fellow triumvir Mark Antony, who had taken the East and tied himself in marriage and policy to Cleopatra of Egypt. The propaganda war preceded the military war by several years; the final naval engagement at Actium on the second of September 31 BC, fought largely by Octavian's right hand Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, decided the question. Antony and Cleopatra committed suicide at Alexandria the next summer. Octavian was thirty-three, and was without a rival in the Roman world.

He held his triumph at Rome in August of 29 BC and immediately set about disguising the regime he had created. In January of 27 BC he formally returned all his powers to the Senate and the Roman people and was promptly voted them back in a carefully chosen package of magistracies that gave him the substance of monarchy without the name. He was given the new title Augustus, which had a faint religious overtone of veneration; he was granted the proconsular imperium over the great frontier provinces where most of the legions were stationed; he was made tribune for life, with the inviolability and veto power of that office. He was, in his own description, princeps, first man, a title that the Republic had honored without giving it constitutional weight. He held it for forty-one further years, refining it as he went.

The administrative achievements of those years redrew the Mediterranean. He completed the conquest of Hispania, of the Alpine peoples, of the Pannonians on the Danube, of Egypt as a personal possession, of much of the Balkans, and pushed the German frontier to the Elbe before the catastrophe of the loss of three legions under Varus in the Teutoburg Forest in AD 9 forced him to settle the line at the Rhine. He set up a professional standing army, paid and pensioned from a central military treasury he funded from his own resources; a permanent fleet at Misenum and Ravenna; a personal bodyguard of nine cohorts, called praetorian, stationed near Rome; a courier service along the trunk roads of the empire; a fire brigade and night watch in the capital; and a regular census of the provinces, the third such census, taken in AD 6, being the one in which the gospel of Luke places the journey of Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem.

His domestic life was a sequence of dynastic disappointments. He had no son by his first or his lasting marriage. His one acknowledged child, his daughter Julia by his second wife Scribonia, he married first to his nephew Marcellus, then to his right hand Agrippa, then to his stepson Tiberius. The two sons whom Agrippa gave him, Gaius and Lucius Caesar, both died young of illness in the first years of the new century; the daughter Julia he banished, in 2 BC, to a small island for promiscuity that he could neither tolerate nor explain. By the time of his own old age the heir-presumptive was Tiberius, his wife Livia's son by her first marriage — a competent, gloomy, and politically reluctant successor whom Augustus formally adopted in AD 4 and gradually associated with the powers of the principate.

He was a careful, frugal man in his private life — small in stature, light-haired, said to be plain in dress, fond of the same furniture and the same friends for decades, an unenthusiastic eater. His wife Livia outlived him; his sister Octavia, on whose moral standing the dynasty rested in many of the early years, died in 11 BC; his old friend Maecenas, who had patronized the poets of his reign — Virgil, Horace, Propertius — died in 8. His confidant Agrippa, who had stood beside him at Naulochus and Actium and built the Pantheon, the aqueducts, and the public baths of his capital, had died in 12 BC. He outlasted them all. He died at Nola, in a house where his father had died seventy-one years earlier, in the bed in which his father had died, of natural causes after a brief illness, and is reported to have asked his attendants whether he had played his part well in the comedy of life.

He left behind a Rome of marble, in his own famous claim, where he had found a city of brick — an exaggeration of the public works of his reign that was true only of the central areas his architects had rebuilt. He left a regime that bore the form of the old Republic and the substance of monarchy and that would last in that compromise for two and a half centuries before its forms began to fail. He left a successor in Tiberius, four further successors in the family of his wife Livia — Caligula, Claudius, Nero — and the precedent of one man's lifelong control of the Roman state. The principate he founded would, in the course of the next three centuries, evolve into the open imperial monarchy that gave Europe its longest political tradition; the month August, named for him by senatorial decree in his lifetime, has carried his name on every Western calendar since.

He had been adopted by Caesar at nineteen, taken over the world by thirty-three, and died at seventy-five as the only man at Rome who could remember the day Caesar fell.
