Guillaume de Nogaret

c. 1260 – 1313, Languedoc lawyer, Keeper of the Seal to Philip IV, the engineer of Anagni and the Templar trial

Guillaume de Nogaret was born about 1260 at Saint-Félix-de-Caraman, a small town in the diocese of Toulouse in the heart of the Languedoc — the country which had been the kingdom of the Cathars and which, two generations before his birth, had been broken by the Albigensian Crusade. His enemies, particularly Pope Boniface VIII when the duel between them began, made much of a story that his grandfather had been burned as a heretic at Saint-Félix in the early thirteenth century; modern historians regard the charge as a political fabrication, but it stuck. The Nogaret family was of the lesser legal nobility of the Toulousain. Guillaume studied Roman law at Montpellier, then the most progressive of the southern law schools, took the degree of doctor of laws by 1287, and held the chair of jurisprudence there before entering the king's service.

He came to the notice of Philip IV in the early 1290s as a royal judge in Languedoc and was raised in 1295 to the king's council. He was knighted in 1299. His earliest assignments concerned the absorption of the southern provinces — the seneschalsies of Beaucaire and Carcassonne — into the legal and fiscal order of the Capetian state, and from the beginning the temper of his work was that of a Roman lawyer of the school of the Glossators, severe and absolute on the question of royal sovereignty, contemptuous of the patchwork of feudal jurisdictions and ecclesiastical immunities he was paid to dismantle. Through the first conflict between Philip and Boniface VIII, in 1296–1297, he was a junior figure on the king's council; the leading lawyer of the first round was Pierre Flote, the chancellor.

Flote was killed at the Battle of the Golden Spurs at Courtrai on 11 July 1302 and Nogaret stepped up to lead the king's case in the second and definitive quarrel with the papacy. In March 1303, before the royal council at the Louvre, he delivered the great speech in which he formally accused Boniface VIII of heresy, simony, sodomy, blasphemy, and idolatry, and demanded that a general council of the church be summoned to depose him. The speech, with its forty articles of indictment, was the first time in the Latin Middle Ages that a sitting pope had been so accused before the council of a Christian king; it was distributed in dozens of copies through the kingdom and read out in chapter houses and town halls.

The king then sent him to Italy. Through the spring and summer of 1303 Nogaret, travelling under a false name and disguise, made his way through northern Italy, distributed French gold to the enemies of the Caetani — the Colonna in particular — recruited mercenaries in Tuscany, and reached the papal town of Anagni in the Roman Campagna in the first week of September. On 7 September 1303, with about three hundred soldiers and the assistance of Sciarra Colonna, he broke into the papal palace at Anagni, where the pope had retired to the family town for the heat of summer. Boniface, alone with a handful of attendants, refused to abdicate; Sciarra is said by one tradition to have struck him in the face with his mailed gauntlet (the so-called schiaffo di Anagni); Nogaret prevented his execution and held him prisoner for three days. The townspeople rose on 9 September and freed the pope. Boniface returned to Rome but died at the Lateran on 11 October, exhausted and ill, by general report broken by the captivity.

Benedict XI, the Dominican who succeeded Boniface, excommunicated Nogaret on 7 June 1304 and reserved his absolution to the pope alone; Benedict died at Perugia a month later. Nogaret thereafter laboured for nine years to obtain that absolution and to vindicate his name from the charge of having killed a pope — an obsession of his last decade which was not, as it might appear, a private matter of conscience, but a public requirement of his political position. He pressed Clement V relentlessly for a formal trial of the dead Boniface, in which his own actions could be exhibited not as crime but as instrument of legitimate accusation. The posthumous proceedings against Boniface dragged on from 1310 to 1311, with parades of witnesses procured and rehearsed by Nogaret himself, and were quietly extinguished at the Council of Vienne by a compromise: Nogaret was absolved on 27 April 1311, on light penances, of any guilt for Anagni; the heresy of Boniface was neither affirmed nor denied; the bulls of Boniface against the French crown were annulled.

His second great enterprise was the destruction of the Order of the Temple. The accusations had been brewing for some years; the political will and the inquisitorial machinery were Nogaret's. By August 1307 he had drafted the charges and the procedure, and was the only royal officer besides the king who knew the date and the targets of the simultaneous arrests planned for 13 October. He was appointed Keeper of the Seal — the highest legal office of the kingdom, in lieu of Chancellor — on 22 September 1307. He directed the interrogations at Paris in October and November, signed the orders for the question by torture, and was the architect of the legal theory that confessions extracted under interrogation by royal officers counted as ecclesiastical confessions for the purposes of papal jurisdiction — a theory which both shielded the king from charges of usurping spiritual jurisdiction and made Clement V's eventual concession of the parallel inquiry inevitable.

The other heavy work of his last decade was administrative: the expulsion of the Jews from France in July 1306, accompanied by the seizure of their property and the cancellation of debts owed to them; the expulsion of the Lombard bankers in 1311 on the same terms; the regulation of the coinage, in which Philip IV's repeated debasements were carried through by Nogaret's draftsmanship. He also drafted the great memoranda on royal sovereignty — on the king's right to tax the clergy, on the king's right to summon the Estates General, on the king's exemption from any human jurisdiction including the papal — that became the legal foundation of the early modern French monarchy. His writing has the cold, lucid violence of a man who has read all of the Digest and forgotten none of it.

He died, probably of an illness rather than the assassination later legend invented for him, in March or April 1313, before the Council of Vienne finished and before the burning of Jacques de Molay. He had been raised to the rank of councillor for life, granted lordships at Manduel, Bellegarde, and Calvisson in the Languedoc, and made the most powerful commoner-lawyer in the kingdom; he died, by the standards of the great Capetian ministers who followed him, rich but not extravagantly so. He was buried at the priory of Sussargues near Montpellier, of which he had been the patron.

The reputation of Guillaume de Nogaret has been ferociously hostile from his own time. Dante put him by name in Purgatorio XX, where Hugh Capet, the founder of the dynasty, speaks of him as the new Pilate who renewed the outrage of the Passion at Anagni — the most vivid single condemnation of any French royal officer in medieval Italian literature. To his admirers, mostly among nineteenth- and twentieth-century French legal historians, he is one of the great architects of the sovereign state; to his enemies, both contemporary and modern, he is the patron saint of the show trial, the man who taught the European monarchies how to dress up a confiscation as a heresy proceeding. Both views are right. He had no rival as a technician of royal power; he had no scruple about its uses.
