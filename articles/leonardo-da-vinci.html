Leonardo da Vinci

1452 – 1519, Florentine painter, engineer, anatomist, and natural philosopher, the type of the universal genius of the Renaissance

Leonardo was born at the village of Vinci in the Tuscan hills west of Florence on 15 April 1452, the illegitimate son of a young Florentine notary, Ser Piero da Vinci, and a peasant woman named Caterina. He grew up partly in his mother's household and partly in his father's; he was never legitimised and never inherited, which freed him from the legal training that would have been the family trade. Around 1466, when he was fourteen, his father moved the family to Florence and placed him in the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, then the most progressive painter-sculptor in the city, whose pupils also included Botticelli, Perugino, and Lorenzo di Credi. Leonardo was matriculated in the Florentine painters' guild, the Compagnia di San Luca, in 1472 but remained in Verrocchio's workshop for years afterward, painting the kneeling angel in the master's Baptism of Christ with a delicacy that, according to Vasari, made Verrocchio abandon painting altogether.

His first independent commissions in Florence were the Annunciation now at the Uffizi, a portrait of Ginevra de' Benci, and in 1481 the unfinished Adoration of the Magi for the monastery of San Donato a Scopeto, in which the experimental crowded composition and the use of underdrawing in transparent washes already mark the technique that the High Renaissance would inherit from him. He was at this date a strikingly handsome young man, fastidious in dress, vegetarian, ambidextrous, fond of music, in his own description "without letters" because he had not learned Latin in childhood and felt the inferiority for the rest of his life.

About 1482 he left Florence for Milan, sending the duke Ludovico Sforza, called il Moro, a letter in which he advertised himself principally as a military engineer who could also paint and sculpt as occasion required. He stayed at the Sforza court for almost seventeen years. The works of his Milanese period are the substrate of his fame: the two versions of the Virgin of the Rocks, the portraits of Cecilia Gallerani (The Lady with an Ermine) and of an unidentified woman called La Belle Ferronnière, the wall painting of The Last Supper in the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie, and the enormous model in clay of a horse for an equestrian monument to the duke's father, which stood in Milan for years and was destroyed by Gascon archers in 1499. He filled his notebooks in mirror-writing with engineering and anatomical drawings, designs for fortifications, cranes, canal locks, flying machines, the construction of an ideal city after the plague of 1485, and studies of the play of water in vortices. He produced for Sforza weddings, masques, theatrical machinery; he organised the pageant for the marriage of Gian Galeazzo Sforza in 1489 and the long fête in honour of the wedding of Bianca Maria Sforza to the Emperor Maximilian.

The Last Supper, painted between 1495 and 1498, made his European reputation. He worked on it slowly, sometimes for days without touching the wall, sometimes for hours without leaving it, and used an experimental oil-and-tempera technique on dry plaster that gave him fine detail and slow drying time but proved disastrous over the centuries; the paint began flaking before his death. The dramatic conception, the twelve apostles caught in the moment after Christ's announcement that one of them would betray him, replaced the static frieze of the older Last Supper tradition with the psychological narrative on which Western painting would model itself for three hundred years.

In October 1499 the French invasion of Italy by Louis XII overthrew Ludovico Sforza, and Leonardo, after a short period in Mantua and Venice, returned to Florence in 1500. The next decade was unsettled. In 1502 he served Cesare Borgia for some months as military engineer, surveying the fortifications of Romagna; the surviving maps from that summer are among the most beautiful cartographic documents of the Renaissance. In 1503 he was back in Florence; he received the commission for the great mural of the Battle of Anghiari in the council hall of the new republic, on the wall opposite Michelangelo's Battle of Cascina, the pair of cartoons that drew every painter in Italy to study them; both were left unfinished. He worked in these same years on the small portrait of Lisa del Giocondo, wife of a Florentine silk merchant, that was never delivered to its commissioner and that he carried with him for the rest of his life.

From 1508 he was again in Milan, now under French rule. He undertook anatomical dissections in collaboration with the young anatomist Marcantonio della Torre at Pavia — some thirty bodies altogether, men and women, foetuses, the elderly — producing drawings of bones, musculature, the foetus in the womb, the chambers of the heart, and the cerebral ventricles that were anatomically in advance of any printed work of his century. In 1513 he moved to Rome under the protection of Giuliano de' Medici, brother of the newly elected Pope Leo X, and lived three years at the Belvedere, working on optics, mathematics, and a long succession of unfinished projects.

The pope's brother died in 1516; Leonardo, now sixty-four, accepted the invitation of Francis I of France and crossed the Alps with his pupil Francesco Melzi and three paintings — the Mona Lisa, the Saint John the Baptist, and the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne. The king lodged him at the manor of Cloux, later Clos Lucé, near the royal château of Amboise, with a pension of seven hundred crowns and the title of premier peintre, ingénieur et architecte du Roi. He spent two and a half years there. The king visited him often; Vasari, writing a generation later, said that Leonardo died in Francis's arms on 2 May 1519, a story doubted by modern historians but accepted by everyone else. He left his manuscripts to Melzi, his vineyard outside Milan to his servant Salaì, and the residue to his half-brothers, with whom he had quarrelled for years.

The completed paintings he left behind, perhaps fifteen in number, were already in his lifetime recognised as a new standard of what painting could be. The notebooks, scattered, partly lost, partly bound by later collectors into the codices that bear their names — Atlanticus, Arundel, Forster, Hammer (now Bill Gates's) — only began to be properly published in the late nineteenth century, and continue to be edited. They contained, among much else, the principles of stratigraphy two centuries before geology, the observation of the moon's earthshine, designs for the parachute and the helical screw, and the first dynamic theory of muscle action. He had finished few of the projects he had begun, and many of those he finished he had ruined by experimental techniques; he had nevertheless changed what art and science could look like, and the Renaissance, which had several geniuses, has chosen to remember him as the type of all of them.
