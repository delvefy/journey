Anne Neville

1456 – 1485, Princess of Wales and Queen of England, daughter of the Kingmaker, wife of two enemies

Anne was born on 11 June 1456 at Warwick Castle, the younger daughter of Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick — the Kingmaker — and Anne Beauchamp, the great Beauchamp heiress through whom Warwick held his earldom. Her elder sister, Isabel, was four years older. The two girls grew up between Warwick, Middleham in Wensleydale, and the family's other northern strongholds, in a household that was, in the 1460s, the wealthiest and most powerful in England outside the king's. Among the boys raised in that household was their cousin Richard, Duke of Gloucester, sent north by his brother Edward IV in 1465 as part of the conventional schooling of a great prince. Anne was nine when he arrived; he was twelve.

Her father's quarrel with Edward IV, which built through the late 1460s and broke in open rebellion in 1469, drove him into French exile in the spring of 1470. There, under the brokerage of Louis XI, he reconciled with Margaret of Anjou, his enemy of the previous decade, and agreed to restore her husband Henry VI to the English throne. The price of the agreement was that Anne, then thirteen, should marry Margaret's seventeen-year-old son Edward of Lancaster, Prince of Wales. The wedding was solemnised at Angers Cathedral on 13 December 1470. Within months Warwick had crossed to England, driven Edward IV into exile, and restored Henry VI in what contemporaries called the readeption. Margaret and Anne waited in France for the situation to settle before crossing themselves; the delay was fatal.

They landed at Weymouth on 14 April 1471, the very day Warwick was killed at Barnet by the returning Edward IV. Margaret marched west to gather strength among the Lancastrian gentry of the south-west, with Anne in her train. At Tewkesbury on 4 May the Lancastrian army was destroyed. Edward of Lancaster, Anne's husband of less than five months, was killed in the battle or in its immediate aftermath. Margaret was taken prisoner. Anne, fourteen years old and now a widow, was brought back to London under Yorkist escort and lodged with her elder sister Isabel, who had married Edward IV's middle brother George, Duke of Clarence, in 1469.

What followed was one of the strangest episodes in the Yorkist family quarrel. Clarence, by virtue of Isabel's marriage, claimed the whole of the Neville inheritance — the great earldoms of Warwick and Salisbury — through his wife. The youngest brother, Richard of Gloucester, who had returned from Burgundian exile with Edward IV and fought at Barnet and Tewkesbury, proposed to marry Anne, which would have entitled him to a half-share. Clarence, determined to prevent the marriage, is said by the chronicler John Croyland to have hidden Anne in the City of London disguised as a kitchen maid, until Richard found her and removed her to sanctuary at St Martin-le-Grand. The legal dispute over the inheritance was thrashed out before the king in the spring of 1472; the brothers agreed an uneasy partition; Richard and Anne were married in the spring or early summer of 1472, almost certainly at Westminster.

For the eleven years that followed Anne was, in effect, lady of the north of England. She and Richard lived chiefly at Middleham, the castle in which she had been raised; their only known child, Edward of Middleham, was born there about 1473. Richard's career as warden of the West March and as Edward IV's lieutenant in the north was conducted from this household; Anne's role in the running of it can be inferred more easily than documented, since the Neville inheritance she carried was the financial basis of the whole enterprise. She accompanied Richard south for the great Yorkist court occasions and was, by the silent agreement of the surviving sources, one of the small, deliberately distant family circle in which Richard moved.

Edward IV's death in April 1483 brought her into the centre. Richard's coup against the Woodvilles and his elevation to the throne as Richard III in June 1483 made Anne queen consort. She was crowned with him at Westminster on 6 July, in a ceremony for which detailed accounts survive: she wore cloth of gold and white silk, her train was carried by Margaret Beaufort, and the two of them walked barefoot from Westminster Hall to the abbey. Their son Edward of Middleham, ten years old, was created Prince of Wales at York in early September during the royal progress north.

Within nine months both Edward of Middleham and Anne herself were dying. The prince of Wales died at Middleham in April 1484; the news reached his parents at Nottingham, where they were said by Croyland to have nearly gone mad with grief. Anne went into a decline through the autumn and winter that was almost certainly tuberculosis. By early 1485 she was bedridden at Westminster. She died on 16 March 1485 in the queen's apartments, on a day on which contemporary chroniclers noted a partial eclipse of the sun. She was twenty-eight. She was buried in Westminster Abbey, near the high altar, in a grave unmarked until a brass was placed there in 1960.

Her last weeks were complicated by political rumour. With both his son and his queen visibly fading, Richard's enemies whispered that he meant to marry his niece Elizabeth of York, the eldest daughter of Edward IV; some went further and claimed he had poisoned Anne. The rumour cost him in the country: he was driven to deny it in person, in an unprecedented public statement at the Hall of the Knights of St John at Clerkenwell, before the lord mayor and the leading citizens of London. He outlived her by five months and was killed at Bosworth on 22 August 1485.

Anne Neville's life is, in the surviving record, a sequence of dynastic placements: Warwick's daughter, Lancastrian princess, Yorkist duchess, English queen, in the space of five years. Of her person and inner life almost nothing direct is recorded. She passed through the central crisis of fifteenth-century English politics as the senior surviving Neville heiress; the inheritance she carried sustained her husband's career; she lived to see him crowned, and to bury her son, and to die before him. Of the Kingmaker's line, by the time the wars ended at Bosworth, nothing remained. Her elder sister Isabel had died of childbed fever in 1476; her father had been killed at Barnet in 1471; her uncles had been killed at Wakefield and at Towton; her son had died at ten. With Anne's death the Neville name vanished from the front of English politics, in which it had stood since the reign of Edward III.

The road continues to Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick.
