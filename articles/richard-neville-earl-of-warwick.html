Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick

1428 – 1471, the Kingmaker, who made and unmade Edward IV and died at Barnet

Richard Neville was born on 22 November 1428, the eldest surviving son of Richard Neville, Earl of Salisbury, and Alice Montagu, the heiress of the Montagu earls of Salisbury. He was a Neville of the great northern stock that descended from Ralph Neville, Earl of Westmorland, and Joan Beaufort, daughter of John of Gaunt; his uncle, by the same descent, was Cecily Neville, mother of Edward IV and Richard III, which made him the king's first cousin on the maternal side. His marriage in 1449 to Anne Beauchamp, the sole surviving heiress of Richard Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick, brought him in 1450 the Warwick title, the great Beauchamp estates in the Midlands and Welsh Marches, and a position in the upper aristocracy that, when combined with his father's Salisbury inheritance and the wider Neville connection in the north, gave him the largest concentration of landed wealth in England outside the royal demesne.

His political education was the collapse of Lancastrian government in the 1450s: the loss of Normandy in 1450; the loss of Guyenne in 1453; Henry VI's mental breakdown in the autumn of the same year; the rivalry between Richard, Duke of York — Cecily's husband — and Edmund Beaufort, Duke of Somerset, for control of the regency. Warwick and his father, who had quarrelled with the rival Neville-Percy faction in the north, attached themselves to York's party. At the first battle of St Albans on 22 May 1455, the opening engagement of what would later be called the Wars of the Roses, Warwick at twenty-six commanded the flanking attack that broke the king's army and killed Somerset, Northumberland, and Lord Clifford in the streets of the town. The victory put York into the regency a second time.

From 1456 to 1459 Warwick was Captain of Calais, the chief overseas command of the English crown, and made of the port and its garrison his personal political base. From Calais he conducted privateering raids in the Channel that built his reputation as a sea captain and brought him popularity in the south-eastern shires; he intervened repeatedly in English politics from across the water; he was, in effect, the operational arm of the Yorkist party. When the Lancastrian counter-attack of 1459 drove York out of England, Warwick crossed to Ireland to confer with him and returned to Kent in June 1460 at the head of a Yorkist invasion. He defeated the king's army at Northampton on 10 July, captured Henry VI, and rode him into London.

The Yorkist position collapsed at Wakefield on 30 December 1460, where the duke of York and Warwick's own father Salisbury were killed. Warwick himself was defeated at the second battle of St Albans on 17 February 1461 and lost custody of Henry VI to Queen Margaret. Within three weeks his cousin Edward, eighteen years old, had been acclaimed king as Edward IV in London, and the combined Yorkist army marched north to fight the climactic battle of Towton on Palm Sunday, 29 March 1461, in driving snow. Warwick fought in the centre of the line; the rout of the Lancastrians was unprecedented in its casualties. He emerged as the senior magnate of the new regime, with his brother John as Earl of Northumberland and his brother George as Bishop of Exeter and later Archbishop of York.

For the first phase of Edward IV's reign Warwick was, in everything but name, the government of England. He negotiated abroad on the king's behalf; he commanded armies in the north against the surviving Lancastrian risings; he stage-managed the royal marriage policy. The breach came in 1464, when Edward, without consulting him, married secretly Elizabeth Woodville, the widow of a Lancastrian knight and the daughter of a minor baron. Warwick had been at that very moment in France negotiating with Louis XI for a royal marriage with a French princess; the announcement humiliated him, and the subsequent flood of Woodville appointments at court — Elizabeth's father raised to an earldom, her many siblings married into the great houses — rebuilt the political nation against him.

By 1469 he was in open opposition, with the king's brother George, Duke of Clarence, as his ally. He married his elder daughter Isabel to Clarence in defiance of the king's wishes at Calais on 11 July 1469; on 26 July, at Edgcote Moor, a force loyal to him defeated the king's army and captured Edward IV. Warwick held the king at Middleham through the autumn before being forced by political necessity to release him. A second rising in the spring of 1470 collapsed at the battle of Losecoat Field, and Warwick and Clarence fled to France in April.

At Louis XI's court he made the boldest stroke of his career: he reconciled with Margaret of Anjou, the wife of the deposed Henry VI and his enemy of the previous decade. The peace was sealed by the marriage of his younger daughter Anne to Margaret's seventeen-year-old son Edward of Lancaster, Prince of Wales, at Angers Cathedral on 13 December 1470. Warwick crossed to England in September 1470, drove Edward IV into Burgundian exile, freed Henry VI from the Tower, and restored him to the throne in a brief reign that contemporaries called the readeption. For six months he governed England in Henry's name.

The recovery did not hold. Edward IV, supported by his brother-in-law Charles the Bold of Burgundy, landed at Ravenspur in Yorkshire in March 1471 with a small force, marched south, gathered men as he went, and was joined at Coventry by his brother Clarence, who deserted Warwick at the decisive moment. Warwick fell back on London and was outmanoeuvred. The two armies met at Barnet on Easter morning, 14 April 1471, in a heavy fog that confused the lines; in the chaos Warwick's right wing under the Earl of Oxford circled back and was mistaken by his own centre for Yorkists; the centre broke; Warwick, trying to reach his horses behind the line, was overrun and killed by Yorkist soldiers, perhaps deliberately, perhaps not knowing who he was. He was forty-two. His body was carried to London and exhibited at St Paul's so that the country could see he was dead, then released for burial at Bisham Priory beside his father.

His daughters inherited what remained: Isabel as Duchess of Clarence until her death in 1476, Anne as wife successively of Edward of Lancaster and Richard, Duke of Gloucester. Through Anne the Neville inheritance passed for a generation to the crown; through Isabel's son Edward, Earl of Warwick, executed by Henry VII in 1499, the line came to an end. The epithet "Kingmaker" was first applied to him in print only in the 1590s, but the substance was contemporary: in twelve years he had set one cousin on the throne, deposed him, restored his cousin's predecessor, and died trying to defend the restoration. No English subject before or since has held political power on the scale he held it between 1461 and 1470.

The road continues to Margaret of Anjou.
