Search engine optimization, universally abbreviated SEO, is the craft of arranging a website — its content, its markup, its links, and the machinery that serves it — so that a search engine judges it worthy of a prominent place among the unpaid results for a given query. It is at once a branch of web engineering and a branch of marketing, conducted in perpetual half-light, for the rules it seeks to satisfy are set by a handful of search companies who decline, on principle, to publish them in full. The optimiser therefore proceeds by documentation, experiment, and inference, forever reading the entrails of an algorithm that is itself revised thousands of times a year. In 2026 the discipline finds itself at its greatest inflection since its birth, for the search engine it was built to please is quietly becoming an answer engine, one that increasingly reads the web on the user’s behalf and returns a composed reply rather than a list of links to click.

Organic, not paid

The first distinction to grasp is the one between the two halves of a results page. A portion of it is sold: advertisers bid, in a continuous auction, to appear above or beside the natural listings, and pay when their advertisement is clicked. This is the province of search engine marketing in its narrow, paid sense, and of pay-per-click advertising. SEO concerns the other portion — the organic results, which cannot be bought and are awarded, at least in principle, on merit. The economic appeal is plain: organic visibility, once earned, draws visitors without a fee for each one, though the earning of it is neither quick nor certain nor, despite appearances, free.

How it works, in outline

A search engine performs three offices, and SEO addresses each in turn. First it crawls, sending automated programs across the web to fetch pages and follow the links between them. Then it indexes, parsing what it has fetched and filing the words, images and meaning of each page into a colossal data structure built for instant retrieval. Finally, when a query arrives, it ranks: from the millions of pages that might answer, it selects and orders a handful in the fraction of a second before the page is drawn. The optimiser must ensure, in order, that a page can be crawled, that it is indexed, and that once indexed it is judged relevant and trustworthy enough to rank.

The two halves of the craft

By long convention SEO is divided into work done on the site and work done off it. On-page and technical SEO covers everything within the practitioner’s control: clear and relevant content, sound HTML, descriptive titles, a logical architecture of internal links, fast and stable pages, structured data that spells out a page’s meaning to the machine, and the housekeeping of crawling and indexing. Off-page SEO covers the signals a site earns from the wider web, chief among them the links other sites choose to point at it — each, in the original conception, a small vote of confidence. The two are complementary: the most authoritative site in the world will not rank if its pages cannot be crawled, and the most flawlessly engineered site will not rank if no one in the world has reason to cite it.

The shifting ground

What the engines reward has changed beyond recognition across three decades. The earliest optimisation was a crude business of repeating keywords until a primitive algorithm was satisfied; a long sequence of named updates — Florida, Panda, Penguin, Hummingbird and their successors — has since taught the machine to prize genuine quality and to punish manipulation. The governing modern slogan is E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness, the qualities Google’s human quality raters are asked to look for and its algorithms to approximate. The optimiser’s task has accordingly migrated from tricking the machine into a far harder one: persuading it, by every honest signal, that a page genuinely deserves the place it seeks.

The 2026 turn

The development that defines the present moment is the arrival of generative search. Google’s AI Overviews — a synthesised answer, composed by a large language model and set above the traditional links — together with answer-first engines such as Perplexity and the search features of the chat assistants, increasingly satisfy the user without a click to any website at all. This both starves sites of the traffic they once relied upon and reframes the optimiser’s goal: no longer merely to rank, but to be cited — to be among the sources a model reads, trusts and names in its composed reply. A clutch of new coinages, answer engine optimization (AEO) and generative engine optimization (GEO) among them, has sprung up to describe the pursuit, even as the older technical foundations — crawlable, fast, well-structured, trustworthy pages — prove, if anything, more important than before. SEO is not dying, as it is annually pronounced to be; it is once again changing what it means to optimise, and for whom.

A deeper delve

The anatomy of a search engine

To optimise for a machine one must first understand what the machine does, and a modern web search engine does three large things in sequence: it discovers and fetches pages, it processes and stores them, and it retrieves and orders them on demand. The whole of SEO is an attempt to influence one or more of these stages, and the conventional vocabulary — crawling, indexing, ranking — names them directly.

Crawling

A crawler (or spider, or bot — Google’s is Googlebot) is a program that fetches a web page, extracts the hyperlinks it contains, and adds the destinations to a queue of pages yet to be fetched, repeating the process without end. Beginning from a set of known pages, it traverses the web much as a reader might follow footnote after footnote through a library, discovering new documents only insofar as something already known points to them. The first and most easily overlooked principle of SEO follows at once: a page to which nothing links, and which appears in no sitemap, is to the crawler simply invisible. Discovery is not automatic; it is the consequence of being pointed at.

Crawlers are not free to fetch without restraint. They are expected to honour robots.txt, a plain-text file at the root of a site that declares which paths a given crawler may or may not request; they ration their requests so as not to overwhelm a server (the so-called crawl rate); and to each site they allot, in effect, a finite crawl budget — a rough measure of how many of its pages they will trouble to fetch in a given period. For a small site the budget is irrelevant, for the crawler will reach everything; for a site of millions of pages it matters greatly, and wasting it on duplicate, broken or worthless URLs means that genuinely valuable pages may go unvisited and unrefreshed.

Indexing

Fetching a page is only the beginning. The engine must then render it — and here a great deal of modern grief resides. A page is no longer a static document but often a program: its visible content may be assembled in the browser by JavaScript after the initial HTML arrives. A crawler that reads only the raw HTML of such a page may find it nearly empty of content. Google addresses this with a two-stage process: it indexes the raw HTML at once, then returns later to render the page in a headless browser and index whatever the JavaScript produced. The delay and the cost mean that content present in the served HTML is indexed sooner and more reliably than content conjured by script — the practical case for server-side rendering and against building a content site that is blank without JavaScript.

Once rendered, the page is parsed into the index: an inverted index at its heart, a structure that maps each word to the list of documents containing it, so that the set of pages mentioning a term can be retrieved instantly rather than by scanning the web afresh at query time. To this are joined a great many derived signals — the page’s title and headings, its language, its structured data, its links inward and outward, measures of its quality and freshness, and far more. The engine also decides, at this stage, questions of canonicalisation: where several URLs serve substantially the same content, it elects one as canonical and folds the signals of the duplicates into it, lest a page’s strength be needlessly split across a dozen near-identical addresses.

Ranking and serving

When a query arrives, the engine consults the index for candidate documents and then ranks them, scoring each against the query and against one another by a combination of hundreds of signals, and returns the ordered best. This must happen in well under a second, across an index of hundreds of billions of pages, for any of the tens of thousands of queries arriving each second — an engineering feat as considerable as the ranking itself. The result is then composed into a search engine results page (SERP), which in the modern era is far more than a list of ten blue links: it interleaves advertisements, image and video panels, maps, “people also ask” accordions, featured snippets lifted from a single page, knowledge-graph panels, and — latterly — an AI-composed overview. Every one of these features is a place a site may win or lose visibility, and the study of them is a discipline in itself.

One such feature deserves particular notice as the herald of much that followed: the featured snippet, sometimes called “position zero,” in which the engine lifts a concise answer — a sentence, a list, a small table — from a single ranking page and sets it, attributed, above the rest. To win it, a page must answer the question plainly and early, in the clean, extractable form the engine can quote without editing. The featured snippet was, in hindsight, the engine’s first habitual act of answering rather than merely referring, and the craft of writing for it — the question restated, the crisp definition, the orderly list — has proved to be the same craft that now earns a citation in an AI-composed overview. The optimiser who learned, a decade ago, to write the one paragraph that deserved to be quoted had, without knowing it, been rehearsing for the generative age.

Crawling and indexing in practice

Because nothing can rank that is not first crawled and indexed, the technical housekeeping of these stages is the unglamorous foundation on which all else rests, and the practitioner has a small toolkit of standard instruments for managing it.

robots.txt governs what may be crawled, and is a blunt instrument easily misused. A common and costly error is to forbid the crawling of a page in the hope of keeping it out of the index — but a disallowed page may still be indexed (from its inbound links) while its content remains unread, the worst of both worlds. To keep a page out of the index one uses not robots.txt but a noindex directive in the page’s HTML or HTTP headers; to keep it out of the crawl, robots.txt; and the two must not be combined, for a page blocked from crawling can never be read to discover the noindex it carries.

XML sitemaps are files listing a site’s URLs, submitted to the engine to advertise pages the crawler might otherwise be slow to find, along with hints as to when each was last changed. They supplement discovery by links; they do not replace it, and inclusion in a sitemap is an invitation to crawl, never a guarantee of indexing. Canonical tags (rel="canonical") let a page nominate its own preferred URL, consolidating duplicates; redirects (the permanent 301 above all) move a page’s address and, with it, most of its accumulated signals; and the hreflang annotations tie together the several language or regional variants of a page so that the engine serves the right one to the right reader.

Two larger shifts have reshaped this terrain. The first is mobile-first indexing, completed across the web by the mid-2020s: Google now crawls, renders and indexes the mobile version of a page as the version of record, so that content or structured data present only in a desktop layout is, for ranking purposes, content that does not exist. The second is the steady tightening around HTTPS: encrypted transport, once optional, is now a baseline expectation, a (light) ranking signal, and the price of admission to many browser features. The modern default is a fast, secure, mobile-complete page whose meaningful content is present without waiting on script.

The page itself: on-page and technical SEO

With the page reachable and indexable, attention turns to the page as a document: how its content and markup declare, to both reader and machine, what it is about and why it should be believed. This is the most controllable region of the whole discipline, and its instruments are mostly elements of ordinary, well-written HTML.

The title element remains, after thirty years, among the most consequential lines on a page: it names the document to the engine and, often, supplies the clickable headline in the results. The meta description, though not itself a ranking factor, frequently furnishes the snippet beneath that headline and so governs how many searchers click. Headings — a single descriptive h1, then a logical hierarchy of h2 and h3 beneath it — give the page a skeleton the machine can read as a table of contents. Beyond these, the rise of semantic HTML — article, nav, main, figure, and their kin — lets the markup state the role of each region rather than leaving the engine to guess from a soup of anonymous divs.

Internal linking is the quiet workhorse of technical SEO. The way a site links among its own pages tells the engine which pages the site itself considers important, distributes ranking strength through the architecture, and gives the crawler its paths; a flat, well-linked structure in which no page sits many clicks from the home page is crawled and understood far better than a deep, orphaned tangle. Descriptive URLs, the judicious use of image alt text (which serves the blind reader and the image index alike), and the avoidance of gratuitous duplicate content all belong to the same housekeeping. None of it is arcane; it is simply the difference between a document built to be understood and one that merely happens to exist.

Structured data and the semantic web

Ordinary prose tells a human reader that a page concerns a recipe with a four-star rating, a forty-minute cook time and three hundred calories a serving; to a machine it is undifferentiated text. Structured data closes that gap. Using the shared vocabulary of schema.org — a collaboration founded in 2011 by the major search engines — a page may annotate its content with machine-readable statements of type and property: this is the author, this the price, this the event date, this the question and its answer. The favoured modern syntax is JSON-LD, a block of structured notation tucked into the page’s head, kept cleanly apart from the visible markup it describes.

The immediate reward is the rich result: the star ratings, recipe cards, event listings, frequently-asked-question accordions and breadcrumb trails that decorate the modern results page and draw the eye (and the click) more powerfully than a plain entry. The deeper reward is comprehension. A search engine, and now a language model, that can read a page’s own declaration of its facts need not infer them, and is the more likely to use the page with confidence — to feed the knowledge graph, to populate an answer, to name the source. As search becomes generative, structured data shifts from a decorative nicety toward a primary channel by which a site states plainly what it knows, in terms a machine cannot misread.

Speed and experience: the Core Web Vitals

For most of SEO’s history, how a page felt to use was beyond the engine’s reach; it could read the words but not the experience. That changed with Google’s programme of page experience signals, the measurable core of which are the three Core Web Vitals. Each captures one facet of a page’s behaviour in the hands of a real user, gathered in the field from actual visits rather than a laboratory simulation.

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) measures loading: the time until the largest visible element — typically the hero image or the headline — has been drawn. A good score is 2.5 seconds or less.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP) measures responsiveness: how promptly the page reacts, across the whole visit, to the user’s taps, clicks and key-presses. A good score is 200 milliseconds or less. INP became a Core Web Vital in March 2024, retiring the older First Input Delay, which had judged only the first interaction and so flattered pages that grew sluggish thereafter.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) measures visual stability: the degree to which content jumps about as the page loads — the maddening lurch that moves a button just as one moves to press it. A good score is 0.1 or less.

The Vitals are a genuine ranking input, though a modest and a thresholded one: clearing the “good” bar confers no special favour, but falling well short of it can hold a page back, particularly where competing pages are otherwise evenly matched. Their larger significance is as the first durable attempt to make the engine reward not merely what a page says but whether it is a tolerable thing to use — an alignment, for once, between what pleases the algorithm and what pleases the human being it ostensibly serves.

The link economy

If on-page work is how a site speaks for itself, links are how the rest of the web speaks for it, and the link was the founding insight of modern search. In 1998 Larry Page and Sergey Brin, then at Stanford, proposed PageRank: an algorithm that treated each hyperlink as a vote, weighted each vote by the importance of the page casting it, and so derived a recursive measure of a page’s standing from the structure of the entire web. A link from a widely-cited page counted for more than a link from an obscure one, exactly as a citation from a landmark paper carries more weight than one from a forgotten pamphlet. PageRank made Google’s results startlingly better than its keyword-counting rivals’, and it made the inbound link the single most coveted prize in SEO.

It also, inevitably, made links a currency to be forged, bought and farmed. Optimisers built link farms and private blog networks, traded and sold links, and stuffed keyword-laden anchor text — the visible, clickable words of a link, a strong signal of the target’s topic — into every corner they could reach. Google’s long counter-campaign produced the Penguin update of 2012, which demoted sites with manipulative link profiles, and a set of link attributes by which a site may disavow responsibility for a link’s endorsement: rel="nofollow" (1998, since softened to a hint), and the later rel="sponsored" and rel="ugc" for paid and user-generated links respectively. The modern doctrine holds that links should be earned by content worth citing, not built by schemes — a counsel of perfection that the persistence of the link-buying trade suggests is honoured as much in the breach.

From keywords to meaning

The deepest change in how engines rank is the long migration from matching strings to understanding meaning. The early engine sought the query’s words on the page and counted them; the modern engine seeks to grasp what the searcher wants and which pages satisfy that want, whatever words either happens to use. A succession of machine-learning systems, layered into the ranking stack over a decade, carried out this transformation.

RankBrain (2015) was the first, a learning system that helped interpret the great many never-before-seen queries by relating them to ones the engine understood, and the first machine-learning component Google acknowledged as a major ranking factor. Neural matching (2018) extended the same instinct to connect queries and documents by concept rather than by shared vocabulary. BERT (2019), built on the transformer architecture that now underlies the whole field of language models, let the engine read a query in context — to grasp that the little words, the prepositions and the order, can invert a sentence’s meaning, so that “travel to” and “travel from” are not the same request. MUM (2021), larger and multimodal, was trained across languages and media at once. The cumulative effect is that keyword density, the obsession of early SEO, is now nearly worthless as a lever: one writes for the human reader’s intent, and trusts an engine that has, to a striking degree, learned to read.

Search intent and the knowledge graph

To rank for a query, one must first understand what the query wants, and the modern optimiser thinks less about words than about search intent — the purpose behind the search. The conventional taxonomy, owed to a 2002 paper by Andrei Broder, sorts queries into a few kinds. A navigational query seeks a particular site already in mind (“BBC News”); an informational query seeks knowledge (“how does PageRank work”); a transactional query seeks to act, usually to buy (“buy running shoes”); and a commercial-investigation query weighs options before acting (“best running shoes 2026”). The intent dictates everything the engine should return, and a page that mistakes one for another — offering a sales catalogue to a searcher who wanted an explanation — will not rank however well it is built, for it answers a question no one asked. The surest reading of intent is empirical: examine what already ranks for a query, for the engine’s current results are its own published verdict on what the searcher wants.

Beneath this lies a deeper shift, from words to entities. Since the introduction of the Knowledge Graph in 2012 — the database of people, places, organisations and things, and the relations among them, that powers the information panels beside the results — Google has increasingly understood a page not as a bag of keywords but as a statement about identifiable things in the world. Its own slogan for the ambition was “things, not strings.” The practical consequence for the optimiser is the doctrine of topical authority: rather than chase a single keyword, one covers a subject comprehensively — the cluster of related questions, the defining entities, the connections a knowledgeable source would naturally make — so that the engine recognises the site as an authority on the topic, not merely a match for a phrase. This entity-centred understanding is also precisely the substrate on which generative answers are built, which is why it has only grown in importance as search has turned generative.

Not one search but many: the verticals

It is a convenient fiction to speak of “the” search results, for a modern results page is assembled from many specialised indexes, and a site may compete in several at once. This universal or blended search weaves into the main listing panels of images, videos, news, shopping and maps, each governed by its own conventions, and each a distinct opportunity for visibility.

Image and video search reward their own signals: descriptive filenames and alt text, captions and surrounding context, structured data, and — for video — transcripts, chapters and clear thumbnails, with YouTube functioning as the second-largest search engine in its own right. News has its own freshness-driven index and its own eligibility rules. Shopping draws on structured product feeds and merchant data rather than ordinary crawling. Each vertical is, in effect, a smaller search engine with its own optimisation, and a serious practitioner learns the ones that matter to their field.

The largest of these sub-disciplines is local SEO, the art of being found for searches with a place attached — “dentist near me,” “coffee in Halle” — and of winning a place in the local pack, the trio of map-pinned businesses Google sets above the ordinary results. Local ranking runs on a different engine of signals: the completeness and accuracy of the Google Business Profile (the free listing a business claims and curates), the consistency of its NAP — name, address and telephone number — across the web’s directories and citations, the volume and quality of its reviews, and the searcher’s own proximity to the premises. For the corner shop and the regional firm, local SEO is not a refinement of the discipline but very nearly the whole of it, and it answers a kind of query — the ready-to-act search for something nearby — that the rise of generative answers has so far disrupted least.

Quality, authority and the question of trust

As the engine learned to read, it grew preoccupied with a harder question than relevance: whether a page is any good, and whether its maker is to be believed. Two long campaigns express this preoccupation. The Panda update of 2011 took aim at thin, derivative and content-farm pages, demoting whole sites built to harvest traffic with low-value text; the Helpful Content system, introduced in 2022 and absorbed into the core ranking machinery by 2024, pursued the same quarry with a sharper criterion — whether content was made, as Google phrased it, “by people, for people,” rather than spun for the engine.

The framework that gathers these judgements is E-E-A-T: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trustworthiness. The first letter, Experience, was added only in December 2022, recognising that first-hand acquaintance with a subject — the reviewer who actually used the product, the traveller who actually walked the road — is itself a mark of value distinct from credentialed expertise. E-E-A-T is not a single number the algorithm computes; it is the constellation of qualities Google’s Search Quality Rater Guidelines — a public document running to many score of pages — instruct the company’s thousands of human raters to assess. Those raters do not directly move any page’s rank; their verdicts are used to evaluate and train the algorithms, which then attempt to reproduce the human judgement at scale. The doctrine is strictest in the domain Google calls YMYL — “Your Money or Your Life,” the medical, financial and legal pages where bad information does real harm — where the bar for demonstrated expertise and trust is set highest of all.

A short history of optimisation

SEO is as old as the searchable web, and its history is essentially a duel: optimisers discover what an engine rewards and exploit it to exhaustion; the engine, its results degraded, changes the rules; the optimisers adapt; and the cycle turns again.

In the 1990s, before Google, ranking leaned heavily on the page’s own declarations — above all the meta keywords tag, an invisible list of terms the page claimed to be about. It was trivially abused: one simply listed every term one wished to rank for, relevant or not, and added white-on-white keyword text where the reader could not see it. Google’s arrival (1998) and PageRank shifted authority from what a page claimed to what others said of it, a far harder thing to fake — though the link-spam industry rose to try.

The 2000s brought the first great named updates. “Florida” (2003) wiped out swathes of keyword-stuffed sites and announced that the rules could change overnight. Then came the modern era of animal-named algorithms: Panda (2011) against thin content, Penguin (2012) against manipulative links, and Hummingbird (2013), a wholesale rewrite of the engine to handle conversational, intent-laden queries. The 2010s added the mobile turn — “Mobilegeddon” (2015) rewarded mobile-friendly pages, and mobile-first indexing followed — alongside the HTTPS push and the machine-learning systems (RankBrain, BERT) that taught the engine to read. By the 2020s the named, discrete update had largely given way to frequent, sweeping core updates and the ever-present Helpful Content and spam systems, the engine tuned continuously rather than in occasional convulsions — until the generative turn, treated below, changed the terrain again.

White hat, black hat and the spam wars

The trade divides itself, half in earnest and half in jest, into white hat and black hat — those who optimise within the engines’ published guidelines and those who flout them for faster gain, with a broad grey hat between. The black-hat repertoire is a museum of ingenuity: cloaking, serving the crawler one page and the human another; keyword stuffing and hidden text; doorway pages built only to funnel traffic; link schemes of every kind; and, in the present day, the industrial generation of plausible-seeming AI-written content at a scale no human team could match.

Against these the engine wields both algorithm and human hand. SpamBrain, Google’s machine-learning spam-detection system, hunts manipulation at scale; the spam policies codify what is forbidden, including, since 2024, explicit strictures against scaled content abuse (mass-produced low-value pages, however generated), site reputation abuse (the “parasite SEO” of renting a reputable domain’s authority to third-party junk), and expired-domain abuse. Where the algorithm is insufficient, a human reviewer may impose a manual action — a penalty, notified through Search Console, that can demote or wholly remove a site until the offence is corrected and reconsideration granted. The notable point is that the engine’s stated objection is not to automation as such but to low value: content made to manipulate rankings rather than to help a reader, by whatever means it was produced.

Measuring the invisible

SEO would be mere superstition without measurement, yet much of what the optimiser most wishes to know is deliberately withheld. The indispensable free instrument is Google Search Console, through which the engine reports back to a site’s owner: which queries showed the site and how often, its average position, its click-through rate, which pages are indexed and which excluded and why, its Core Web Vitals in the field, and any manual actions outstanding. It is the one channel through which the engine speaks directly about a particular site.

Two limits frame the whole enterprise. The first is the “(not provided)” curtain: when Google moved search to HTTPS in the early 2010s it stopped passing the searched keyword to the destination site, so that analytics can show that a visitor came from Google but rarely what they searched to arrive — the keyword, once the optimiser’s daily bread, now largely hidden. The second is the irreducible opacity of the algorithm itself, which obliges the field to lean on third-party tools that estimate rankings, search volumes and competitors’ visibility, and on controlled experiment. Two windows have lately opened on the black box despite Google’s wishes: the antitrust trial of United States v. Google, in which the company was ruled in August 2024 to hold an unlawful monopoly in search and during which internal testimony confirmed the heavy use of click and engagement signals (a system named Navboost among them); and the Content Warehouse leak of May 2024, a vast trove of internal API documentation that, while it did not reveal how signals are weighted, named thousands of them and quietly contradicted several of the company’s public denials.

The generative turn: SEO in 2026

The defining condition of SEO in 2026 is that the search engine is no longer only a finder of pages but a reader and synthesiser of them. The change arrived under several banners and from several quarters at once, and it has unsettled assumptions the field had treated as permanent.

AI Overviews. What began as Google’s experimental Search Generative Experience in 2023 became, from 2024, the broadly deployed AI Overviews: a language-model answer, assembled from multiple sources and placed at the very top of the results, above the organic links it draws upon. For a large class of informational queries the user now reads a composed answer and never scrolls to a website at all. This deepens a pre-existing trend, the rise of the zero-click search — already, well before generative answers, a large share of searches ended without any click, satisfied by featured snippets, knowledge panels and the like — and turns it from a nuisance into a structural fact. Sites report that they may now be read far more than they are visited, their substance feeding answers that bear their name, perhaps, but not their traffic.

The answer engines. Alongside Google sit a new class of answer-first services — Perplexity, the web-search modes of the major chat assistants, and others — which dispense with the list of links almost entirely and return a cited prose reply. The optimiser’s goal before such an engine is no longer to rank a page but to be among the handful of sources it chooses to cite. Two new coinages have grown up to name the pursuit: answer engine optimization (AEO) and generative engine optimization (GEO). Their early findings are, sensibly enough, that models favour sources that state facts plainly and early, that carry clear authorship and citations of their own, that are quoted and corroborated elsewhere, and that are structured so a machine can extract a clean, attributable claim — which is to say that good GEO looks remarkably like good SEO and good editing, with the cosmetic tricks stripped away.

The crawler question. The generative turn has also broken the old, settled bargain of the crawl. For thirty years a site tolerated Googlebot because crawling led to ranking led to traffic. The new AI crawlers — OpenAI’s GPTBot, Anthropic’s ClaudeBot, Google’s training-specific Google-Extended, PerplexityBot and their kin — offer no such reciprocity: they consume a site’s content to train or to answer, and may return little or nothing in exchange. Publishers have responded by blocking these agents in robots.txt; infrastructure providers, Cloudflare most prominently, moved in 2025 to block unconsented AI crawling by default and to broker pay-per-crawl arrangements; and a proposed convention, llms.txt (2024), offers a site a way to lay out clean, model-friendly summaries of its content for the machines it does wish to feed. Whether the web settles into a paid licensing regime, an arms race of blocks and evasions, or some new equilibrium is among the genuinely open questions of the moment.

The road ahead

It has become an annual ritual to declare SEO dead — slain in turn by social media, by voice search, by app stores, and now by generative AI — and the discipline has buried each obituary by changing rather than expiring. The change now underway is real and large: if the engine increasingly answers in place of referring, then the value a site captures from being understood by the engine, as opposed to merely visited through it, must be rethought from the ground up, and with it the business model of the open, ad-supported, traffic-fed web that SEO grew up to serve.

Yet the fundamentals prove stubbornly durable, and indeed are sharpened by the new regime. A page that cannot be crawled cannot be cited; a page that loads slowly or buries its meaning in script is as opaque to a language model as to a spider; a source without demonstrable experience, expertise and trust is no likelier to be quoted by a model than ranked by an algorithm; and clear structure, plain statement of fact, and genuine authority remain the surest path to being chosen, whoever or whatever is doing the choosing. The instruments and the vocabulary will go on shifting — from rankings toward citations, from clicks toward mentions, from optimising for a results page toward optimising for an answer — but the underlying task is the one it has always been: to make a genuinely worthy thing legible, and to persuade an inscrutable arbiter that it deserves to be found.
