Andronikos I Komnenos

c. 1118 – 1185, Byzantine emperor, last Komnenian ruler of Constantinople

Andronikos was born about 1118, the son of the sebastokrator Isaac Komnenos, younger brother of the emperor John II. He was therefore a first cousin of his exact contemporary, Manuel I, and the two were raised together in the imperial schoolroom and as young men in the same army camps. The chroniclers of his life — above all Niketas Choniates, who in old age devoted a long, ambivalent, and partially affectionate book to him — describe a man whose physical and personal endowments were extraordinary even in a court that took such things for granted: he was nearly seven feet tall, of athletic strength into his sixties, dark-haired and dark-eyed, fluent and amusing in conversation, brave in a way that bordered on indifference to consequences, and almost without restraint in his pursuit of women. Of these qualities his cousin the emperor Manuel was at first a willing patron and increasingly a wary one.

His first imprisonment was in 1153, for an obscure plot against Manuel that the latter chose to treat as a private misadventure rather than a treasonable conspiracy. He escaped after nine years in the prisons of the Great Palace by a famous stratagem — through tunnels he had dug with a small knife — and was retaken, and confined in a deeper cell, and escaped again, and was forgiven again by an emperor whose policy seems to have been to keep his charismatic cousin contained rather than dead. Restored to a command on the Cilician frontier in 1166, he seduced his sister-in-law Philippa of Antioch — the younger sister of Manuel’s second wife Maria — then fled the empire altogether for the Crusader principalities. In Jerusalem he conducted, with even greater public scandal, an affair with the widowed queen Theodora Komnene of Jerusalem, herself his second cousin and the niece of his Latin sister-in-law; the two of them, threatened with extradition, fled to Damascus, then to Baghdad, then to the court of the Danishmend emirs in eastern Anatolia, then to the Seljuk court at Konya. He spent the better part of fifteen years — through the 1160s and 1170s — circulating between the Muslim and Latin courts of the eastern Mediterranean, with a small Byzantine following loyal to him, an income from the estates the Seljuk sultan settled on him, and an unbroken correspondence with the cousin he had betrayed.

Manuel forgave him a third and final time about 1180, recalling him to Constantinople and entrusting him with the governorship of Pontic Heraclea. The recall was almost certainly an attempt to neutralise him at the moment when the imperial succession was about to fall into the hands of a child. Manuel died in September 1180 leaving the throne to his eleven-year-old son Alexios II under the regency of the empress Maria of Antioch and her lover the protosebastos Alexios Komnenos — a regime that within months had made itself detested in Constantinople by the Latinising character of the court, the favouritism shown to Italian merchants, and the rumour of an actual love affair between the regent and the empress. The Greek population of the capital, the Orthodox clergy, the provincial aristocracy, and the army all looked for an alternative. Andronikos in Pontus heard their preference, raised troops, marched on Constantinople in April 1182, and on his arrival was carried into the capital on the shoulders of a crowd whose first act of welcome was the massacre of the Latin merchant quarter — the great pogrom in which perhaps several thousand Italians, including the papal legate, were butchered in the streets while their houses and churches burned. Andronikos governed the capital as regent through the summer, executed the protosebastos, executed Maria of Antioch on a confession of treason extracted by torture, and in September 1183 had himself crowned co-emperor in Hagia Sophia. Two months later the boy emperor Alexios II was strangled at his cousin’s order with a bowstring; Andronikos married his twelve-year-old widow, Agnes-Anna of France — daughter of Louis VII — and ruled alone.

The two years of his sole reign were the strangest reform attempt in Byzantine history. Andronikos, in his sixties, ran a Constantinople in which his guards stood with drawn swords at the entrance of every public office and which he visited in unannounced inspections, sometimes in disguise. He attempted to abolish the abuses of the tax-farming system that had crystallised under his cousin: he forbade the sale of offices, fixed the official rate of state collections, reorganised the customs at the Bosphorus, executed a number of magnates for embezzlement, restored to peasant farmers lands seized by court favourites in the previous reign. The countryside, the chroniclers concede unanimously, prospered. The capital and the aristocracy — on whom he turned an active and unrelenting hostility — lived in terror. Suspicion of conspiracy was followed by torture and execution; the prisons filled with members of the Doukas, Kantakouzenos, and Angelos clans; the imperial guard was reorganised on personal lines around his bastard son John and a small Latin and Caucasian retinue.

The end came from outside and inside at once. William II of Sicily, with the largest Norman expeditionary force ever sent against the empire, took Dyrrhachium in June 1185 and Thessalonica in August: the second city of the empire was sacked for a week with a brutality that scandalised the Latin chroniclers who described it. The army Andronikos sent to relieve the city was broken; in Constantinople the news produced general panic. On 11 September 1185, the imperial police arrested Isaac Angelos, a Komnenian cousin of moderate standing in the army, in his house in the city; the soldier seized a sword, killed his pursuer, fled to Hagia Sophia, claimed sanctuary, and was acclaimed by the crowd of the cathedral as emperor. Andronikos, returning to the palace by sea, found the gates closed against him. He attempted to escape across the Bosphorus with his wife and a young concubine, was captured at a small port on the Black Sea coast, brought back to Constantinople, and on 12 September 1185 delivered to the people of the capital. The chronicler Niketas Choniates — whose account of the death is one of the most extraordinary set pieces in Byzantine literature — describes the old emperor riding on a mangy camel through the Mese in chains, pelted with refuse, lashed with whips, his right hand cut off in front of a butcher’s stall, one eye gouged out, his teeth pulled, finally suspended by the ankles between two columns of the Hippodrome and torn apart by the spectators with knives and clubs over the course of an entire afternoon. He was sixty-seven.

What he left was a poisoned political world. The Komnenian dynasty, in its main line, was ended; the Angeloi who replaced him would prove unable to hold the empire together for a generation; the Latin pogrom of 1182 was remembered in the West and contributed to the climate of grievance that the Fourth Crusade would in 1204 turn against the city itself. His reforms, harsh and brilliant and personal, did not survive him. The judgment of Choniates — who detested him and could not help admiring him — was that Andronikos “had the genius of a great emperor and the heart of a tyrant, and in the brief time he held power both were on display at once.”
