Martin Luther

1483 – 1546, German theologian and instigator of the Protestant Reformation

Martin Luther was born on 10 November 1483 at Eisleben in the County of Mansfeld, a small mining region in Thuringia where his father Hans Luder had clawed his way up from peasant to small-mine operator and would, by the end of his life, employ a workforce of several dozen. The family was rising, demanding, and stern; the household's expectation that Martin would become a lawyer and complete the family's social ascent was strong, and the path he took at first conformed to it. He was sent to school at Mansfeld, Magdeburg, and Eisenach, matriculated at the University of Erfurt in 1501, took his bachelor's and master's degrees there in the standard arts curriculum, and began legal studies in the spring of 1505.

On 2 July of that year, returning to Erfurt after a visit home, he was caught in a thunderstorm near the village of Stotternheim and threw himself to the ground in a vow to Saint Anne, swearing that if he survived he would become a monk. He survived; two weeks later, against his father's furious objection, he entered the strict Augustinian Eremite friary at Erfurt. He was ordained priest in 1507 and, on the recommendation of his vicar general Johann von Staupitz, transferred in 1508 to the new University of Wittenberg in Saxony to teach moral theology. He took his doctorate in theology in 1512 and was given the chair of biblical exegesis, which he would hold for the rest of his life.

Between 1513 and 1517, in lectures on the Psalms, on Romans, on Galatians, and on Hebrews, his theology shifted. He came to a reading of Paul's letter to the Romans — particularly the phrase “the just shall live by faith” — that placed justification at the centre and located it in a passive trust in God's mercy rather than in any work or merit of the believer. He later described the moment of clarity, perhaps consolidated rather than instantaneous, as having occurred in the tower of the Wittenberg friary; the “tower experience” became a fixed point in Lutheran biographical tradition. From this insight the whole edifice unfolded.

The immediate occasion of his public emergence was an indulgence campaign. Pope Leo X had granted Albert of Brandenburg, archbishop of Mainz, the right to sell indulgences in Germany to fund the rebuilding of St Peter's in Rome — with half of the proceeds going to repay the loans Albert had taken to purchase his archbishopric. The Dominican Johann Tetzel preached the campaign in territories near Wittenberg with an aggressive, transactional theology that scandalised Luther. On 31 October 1517 Luther sent to Albert of Mainz, and at the same time apparently posted on the door of the castle church at Wittenberg, ninety-five theses for academic debate on the doctrine and abuse of indulgences. Whether the door-posting is historical or legendary remains in dispute. The letter is not in dispute, and from the letter the theses spread through Germany within months by means of the printing press, which would be the instrument of the entire Reformation.

For three years the curia in Rome and Luther in Wittenberg manoeuvred. He was examined by Cardinal Cajetan at Augsburg in October 1518, debated Johann Eck at Leipzig in the summer of 1519, and in 1520 published the three central programmatic treatises of the Reformation: To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church, and On the Freedom of a Christian. The first appealed to the secular rulers to reform the church when its hierarchy would not; the second reduced the sacraments from seven to two or three on biblical grounds; the third set out the paradoxical Lutheran theology of the believer as wholly free and wholly bound. The papal bull Exsurge Domine of June 1520 condemned forty-one of his propositions and threatened excommunication; Luther burned the bull in the Wittenberg town square in December.

He was excommunicated in January 1521 and summoned to the Diet of Worms in April. Before the assembled estates of the Empire and the young emperor Charles V he refused to recant: unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason, he said, his conscience was captive to the Word of God, and he could not and would not retract. The closing words attributed to him — “Here I stand, I can do no other” — are not in the official records of the diet but appear in the printed accounts that circulated within weeks. He was placed under the imperial ban by the Edict of Worms and would have been seized had not his territorial lord Frederick the Wise of Saxony intercepted him on the road home in a staged abduction and concealed him in the Wartburg Castle for almost a year. There, working from the Greek of Erasmus's New Testament, he translated the New Testament into German in eleven weeks. It would shape the German language for the next four centuries.

When he returned to Wittenberg in 1522 he found his own movement contested by radicals who had pushed reform farther than he had wanted. He took back the centre by force of preaching. In the catastrophic peasants' war of 1524–1525, when peasants across south Germany rose in arms and appealed to evangelical doctrine as their justification, Luther wrote his most violent political tract, Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants, urging the princes to “smite, slay, and stab” the rebels. The princes did. Tens of thousands died. His credit with the peasants never recovered, and his identification of the Reformation with the established secular power was confirmed for centuries.

In 1525 he married Katharina von Bora, a former Cistercian nun who had fled her convent. The marriage of the priest-monk and the runaway nun was a doctrinal statement and, by all accounts, a happy union; they had six children together. In the same year he published On the Bondage of the Will in answer to Erasmus's On Free Will, breaking publicly with the great humanist over the metaphysics of grace.

The remaining twenty years of his life consolidated and complicated his work. The Augsburg Confession of 1530, written by his close colleague Philip Melanchthon with his input, became the doctrinal foundation of the Lutheran churches. His complete German Bible appeared in 1534. He preached, wrote, polemicised, advised territorial princes on church order, and quarrelled with reformers to his right and his left. His last years were embittered. His writings against the papacy grew coarser; his attacks on the radicals grew more bitter; and in 1543, with no plausible defence, he wrote the long and savage tract On the Jews and Their Lies, recommending the burning of synagogues and the expulsion of Jewish communities. The text was used four hundred years later by the Nazi regime and stands as a permanent moral stain on his work and reputation.

He died on 18 February 1546 at Eisleben, the town of his birth, during a journey to mediate a dispute between the counts of Mansfeld. He was buried at the castle church in Wittenberg. The German Reformation he had set in motion was, by then, the political and confessional reality of a third of the Empire and a growing fact across northern Europe.
