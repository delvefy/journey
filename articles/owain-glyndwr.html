Owain Glyndŵr

c. 1359 – c. 1415, last native Prince of Wales, leader of the great Welsh revolt against Henry IV

Owain ap Gruffydd Fychan, called of Glyndyfrdwy from his estate on the upper Dee — whence the anglicised Glyndŵr — was born about 1359 into one of the senior Welsh princely lineages that had survived the Edwardian conquest of Wales eighty years earlier. He was descended on his father's side from the princes of Powys Fadog and on his mother's, more importantly, from the princes of Deheubarth in the south-west, through whom he was the only living adult who could plausibly claim descent from all three of the ancient royal houses of Wales: Gwynedd, Powys, and Deheubarth. His patrimonial lands consisted of the lordships of Glyndyfrdwy in the Dee Valley and Cynllaith Owain in the Berwyn hills, together with smaller properties in Cardiganshire from his mother's inheritance; he was, in English terms, a substantial Marcher gentleman rather than a great lord.

His early life was the unremarkable career of a gentleman of his class on the Welsh side of the March. He was sent to one of the Inns of Court — the Inns of Chancery or, by some accounts, Lincoln's Inn — in his late teens, where he gained the legal training that survives in the careful documentation of his later proclamations. He served in the army of Richard II in Scotland in 1385, and at sea under Sir Henry Percy — the elder of that name — in 1387, both of which campaigns are documented by his name in the contemporary muster rolls. He married Margaret Hanmer, daughter of an English judge from a settled Marcher family, by whom he had a numerous family: at least four sons and four daughters who survived to adulthood.

The trigger of his revolt was a local property dispute with his neighbour Reginald Grey of Ruthin, a man with the ear of the new Lancastrian regime. Grey, by Welsh tradition supported by later evidence, had withheld a royal summons for service against the Scots so that Owain might be charged with default; the king's council, when Owain appealed, dismissed his suit with the remark that they cared nothing for "barefoot Welsh dogs". On 16 September 1400, with about three hundred kinsmen and tenants gathered at Glyndyfrdwy, Owain was proclaimed Prince of Wales. Within a week the revolt had spread across the north-east; English settlements at Ruthin, Denbigh, Rhuddlan, Flint, and Hawarden were burnt; Henry IV, alarmed, marched a small army into the country in October and returned without engaging.

The rising survived its first winter because Henry IV's regime was at the same time fighting the Scots, the Percys' creditors, and the Despenser-Holland conspiracy of January 1400. Owain's force grew during 1401 by recruiting Welsh students out of Oxford and Cambridge and Welsh labourers off the English manors. He defeated an English force at the battle of Mynydd Hyddgen in Plynlimon in the early summer, the first set-piece victory of the rebellion; he extended his control across the country with a sequence of carefully co-ordinated raids that suggested, to his enemies and his allies, that he had studied campaigns more carefully than the local rising of a Marcher gentleman would have prepared him to.

The first great expansion came in 1402. On 1 April, Easter Sunday, he ambushed Reginald Grey at Bryn Saith Marchog and captured him, holding him for ransom of ten thousand marks — an extraordinary sum which Grey was permitted to raise on the security of his lands. In June at Pilleth in Radnorshire he defeated and captured Edmund Mortimer, the brother of the late Earl of March and uncle of the boy who, by Yorkist reckoning, was Richard II's heir. Mortimer married Owain's daughter Catrin in November and joined the revolt. By the autumn of 1402 Owain controlled most of Wales west of the Severn and was negotiating with the Percys.

The Tripartite Indenture of February 1405, signed at Aberdaron, was the climax of his political vision: it divided England and Wales between three signatories — himself as Prince of Wales with everything west of a line from Worcester to Chester to the Severn; Mortimer, as King of England with the south and east; Northumberland, as ruler of the north — and represented a serious attempt at a new constitutional settlement of the British Isles. Three months earlier, at Machynlleth in June 1404, he had held the first Welsh parliament, with two representatives from each commote; he had concluded a formal alliance with Charles VI of France, who in August 1405 landed a French expeditionary force of two thousand five hundred men at Milford Haven that marched with him as far as the Worcestershire border before withdrawing. He proposed to the antipope Benedict XIII at Avignon — in the so-called "Pennal Letter" of March 1406 — an independent Welsh church under metropolitan authority at St David's, in return for Welsh adherence to the Avignon obedience; he proposed two universities, one in the north and one in the south of Wales. The intellectual scope of the programme was unique among medieval insurgencies of the British Isles.

The high point did not hold. Hotspur was killed at Shrewsbury in 1403; Northumberland was destroyed at Bramham Moor in 1408. The Mortimer claim, by the failure of the Percys, collapsed politically; Edmund Mortimer himself died at the siege of Harlech in early 1409. Henry IV's son, the future Henry V, had been campaigning steadily in Wales since 1403 out of Chester and Hereford; he reduced one castle a year through tedious blockade, broke the rebel grip on Carmarthen in 1407, took Aberystwyth in 1408 and Harlech, the last great rebel stronghold, in early 1409. Owain's wife Margaret, two of his daughters including Catrin, and her three young grandchildren by Mortimer were captured in the fall of Harlech and taken to the Tower of London, where they died in obscure circumstances within a few years.

Owain himself was never captured. From 1409 he disappeared from the documented record. The Welsh tradition preserved later by the chronicler Adam of Usk — a Welshman near to the events — reports that he lived for some years afterwards as a guest of his married daughter Alys Scudamore at Monnington in Herefordshire, just over the English border, refusing the pardon that Henry V eventually offered him in 1415 through his son Maredudd. He is conventionally given a death-date of about 1415; he has no known grave, and Welsh tradition preserved well into the nineteenth century the belief that he, like Arthur, had not died but slept.

His revolt was, in measurable consequence, almost a clean failure: Wales remained an English principality; the Welsh penal laws imposed in its aftermath were harsher than anything before; the cultural devastation of the last decade of the revolt was severe. As a figure in the Welsh imagination, however, Owain became the central national hero of the country. He was the only Welsh ruler after the Edwardian conquest formally crowned and acknowledged as Prince of Wales by his own people. The Welsh national assembly opened in Cardiff in 1999 took as one of its symbols the seal of his 1404 Machynlleth parliament.
