Henry VII of England

1457 – 1509, founder of the Tudor dynasty, victor of Bosworth, reorganiser of the English crown

Henry Tudor was born at Pembroke Castle in Wales on 28 January 1457, in a kingdom already at war with itself. His father, Edmund Tudor, Earl of Richmond, eldest son of Owen Tudor and Catherine of Valois and half-brother of King Henry VI, had been captured by Yorkist troops the previous autumn and had died in their custody of plague on 3 November 1456, three months before the boy's birth. His mother, Margaret Beaufort, was thirteen, a coheiress of the legitimised Beaufort line that descended from John of Gaunt and Katherine Swynford. He was raised first by his uncle Jasper Tudor, Earl of Pembroke, in the Welsh Marches; after the Yorkist victory at Mortimer's Cross in February 1461, when his grandfather Owen Tudor was beheaded at Hereford, he and Jasper were dispossessed and the boy passed into the wardship of the Yorkist Lord Herbert at Raglan Castle, where he lived for ten years among books and the company of Lord Herbert's own son.

The brief Lancastrian restoration of 1470–1471, the readeption of Henry VI, brought him to the king's London court at fourteen, where Henry VI is said to have looked at him and prophesied that to the boy "we and our adversaries must yield." Within five months the king was dead, his son the prince of Wales killed at Tewkesbury, the Lancastrian cause was effectively over, and Henry Tudor was the senior surviving male representative of the line. Jasper Tudor smuggled him out of Wales by sea; they were blown off course and landed in Brittany, where Duke Francis II gave them protection. They spent fourteen years in Breton exile.

From 1471 to 1485 he was Edward IV's diplomatic problem, then Richard III's. The death of Edward IV in April 1483 and the seizure of the throne by his brother as Richard III, the disappearance of the young Edward V and his brother in the Tower, and the unease they caused among Yorkist gentry of the south, gave Henry's claim, hitherto remote, a sudden practical relevance. His mother Margaret Beaufort, working from London while her latest husband Lord Stanley navigated the new regime, opened negotiations with the dowager queen Elizabeth Woodville for the marriage of her son to Edward IV's eldest daughter Elizabeth of York, the principal Yorkist heir. On Christmas Day 1483 at Rennes Cathedral, before his small exile court, Henry swore to marry Elizabeth on his accession.

The Buckingham revolt of October 1483, which was meant to bring him in, failed; he sailed for England with a Breton-supplied fleet that was scattered by storms and turned back. In the summer of 1485, with French support negotiated by his uncle Jasper, he sailed again. He landed at Mill Bay near Milford Haven on 7 August 1485 with about two thousand men, marched through Wales gathering reinforcements, and on 22 August met Richard III's larger army at Bosworth Field in Leicestershire. The battle was decided when his stepfather Lord Stanley's forces, which had stood aside until then, joined his side at the critical moment. Richard III was killed; his crown, the chronicler tells us, was found in a thornbush and placed on Henry's head by Lord Stanley. He was twenty-eight years old.

He was crowned at Westminster on 30 October 1485 and on 18 January 1486 married Elizabeth of York, uniting the rival claims of Lancaster and York in his children and giving the new dynasty the Tudor rose, white within red. The reign that followed was the work of a politically careful man who knew that his title was contestable and that his survival depended on solvency, on suppressing rival claimants, and on demonstrating effective government to the political nation that had elected him by force of arms.

Two Yorkist pretenders tested him. In 1487 Lambert Simnel, a baker's son coached to impersonate Edward, Earl of Warwick (then an actual prisoner in the Tower), was crowned king of England as Edward VI in Dublin and led an Anglo-Irish-Burgundian army to invade England. Henry defeated him at the battle of Stoke Field in Nottinghamshire on 16 June 1487, the last and least-remembered battle of the Wars of the Roses; Simnel was captured, found to be a boy, and sent to work in the royal kitchens. From 1491 the more politically dangerous Perkin Warbeck, claiming to be Richard, the younger of the princes in the Tower, was variously sheltered by Burgundy, France, Scotland, and Ireland; Henry pursued him for eight years through diplomacy, invasion, and capture, and finally hanged him at Tyburn in 1499 with the real Earl of Warwick, who had been brought from the Tower and accused of complicity.

His administration was a quiet revolution. He extended the system of bonds and recognisances binding the great magnates by financial penalty to good behaviour, restored the royal demesne by inquisitions into the alienations of his predecessors, expanded the chamber finance system under his treasurers Edgcombe and Heron, and concentrated the prosecution of overmighty subjects in the Council in the Star Chamber. The cost of war was minimised by treaty. The Treaty of Étaples with France in 1492 secured an annual pension and ended his only French campaign without a battle. The Treaty of Medina del Campo with Ferdinand and Isabella in 1489 established the alliance with Spain through the marriage of his eldest son Arthur to Catherine of Aragon, which took place in 1501. The Treaty of Perpetual Peace with Scotland in 1502, sealed by the marriage of his elder daughter Margaret to James IV, was less perpetual than its title indicated; through her grandson, James VI of Scotland in 1603 inherited the English throne, fulfilling the long-term Tudor settlement that Henry's diplomatic agents had not consciously planned.

The personal losses of his last decade were heavy. Prince Arthur died at Ludlow in April 1502, six months after his marriage; Queen Elizabeth died in childbirth in February 1503; his mother Margaret Beaufort, who had outlived him by months, was at the end his principal confidante. He grew suspicious, parsimonious in ways the political class began to find oppressive, and reliant on the lawyers Empson and Dudley, whose fiscal investigations into the gentry were the subject of one of the first acts of his son's reign — their attainder and execution. He had a long, slow decline through 1508 and died at Richmond Palace on 21 April 1509 in his fifty-second year. His mother died on 29 June, two months later.

He left to his second son the wealthiest crown in the history of medieval England, a working bureaucracy of councillors, treasurers, and judges, a marriage alliance with Spain that was the central diplomatic asset of his reign, and a dynasty that for the next ninety-four years would rule England, break with Rome, conquer Wales fully, and reach, by Drake and Raleigh, the Atlantic. The settlement looked, in his lifetime, fragile; the institutions outlasted the dynasty. Modern historians have judged him by his administrative achievement, which is hard to overstate, by his caution, which sometimes shaded into pettiness, and by the marriages he arranged, which proved over the long term to have been chosen with extraordinary foresight.
