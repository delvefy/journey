Richard I of England

1157 – 1199, the Lionheart, duke of Aquitaine, king of England, leader of the Third Crusade

Richard was born on 8 September 1157 at Beaumont Palace, just outside the walls of Oxford, the third surviving son of Henry II of England and Eleanor of Aquitaine and the favoured child of his mother. The Angevin empire of his father, by the year of his birth, stretched without break from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees: England, Normandy, Maine, Anjou, Touraine, and, through Eleanor, the immense duchy of Aquitaine that ran from Poitou to Gascony. With elder brothers in the field for the English and Norman succession, Richard was raised as Eleanor's heir to the southern half of this empire. He was taken to Poitiers as a small child and brought up speaking the langue d'oc; his Latin was good, his French his second tongue, his English rudimentary at best. In 1169, in the dynastic conferences at Montmirail, Henry II formally divided the future inheritance among his sons: Aquitaine to Richard. In 1170, at age twelve, he was solemnly installed as duke at the abbey of Saint-Hilaire in Poitiers, ring on his finger and lance in his hand, and was placed under the regency of his mother and her tutors.

The next decade in Aquitaine was a continuous schooling in war. The great lords of the duchy — the Lusignans, the counts of Angoulême, the viscounts of Limoges and Ventadour — had never accepted close ducal government, and the young duke, riding from castle to castle and forcing surrenders by sieges that became famous, broke them down systematically. He acquired the engineering interest in fortification and siegecraft that would mark his whole career. By his early twenties he was a soldier whose reputation reached as far as the German courts. The trouble with his father grew with the trouble of his elder brother Henry the Young King: the great revolt of 1173–1174, in which Richard, his elder brother, his younger brother Geoffrey, his mother, and the king of France Louis VII rose together against Henry II, ended in defeat, the imprisonment of Eleanor for what proved to be sixteen years, and the formal reconciliation of the sons with their father. The Young King died in 1183 of dysentery during a fresh rebellion; Geoffrey of Brittany died in 1186 at a Paris tournament; by the late 1180s only Richard and his much younger brother John were left, and the succession to the English throne had become Richard's at last.

The relationship with his father turned bitter in the final years. Henry II, ageing and ill, refused to confirm Richard's status as heir while pressing him to surrender Aquitaine to John; Richard refused. In November 1188, at the conference of Bonsmoulins, he did public homage to the new king of France, Philip Augustus, with whom he had formed a close personal alliance, and demanded recognition of his rights from his father. The alliance was the most consequential of his life. Philip and Richard rode together that winter and into the next summer, slept in the same chamber as the chroniclers ostentatiously recorded, and made war on Henry II through the spring of 1189. The campaign collapsed Henry's defences in the Loire valley; on 4 July at Ballan he submitted; he was carried to Chinon and died there on 6 July. Richard was crowned king at Westminster on 3 September 1189; he was thirty-one. The coronation was followed by riots in London against the Jewish community, who had been forbidden to attend the ceremony; Richard punished the rioters and ordered protection of the Jews of the realm in a writ that was only partially enforced.

From his accession he had a single overriding plan: to recover Jerusalem, which had fallen to Saladin two years before. He spent the winter of 1189–1190 raising money on a scale that the English administrative records register with disbelief, selling sheriffdoms, charters, and earldoms ("I would have sold London if I could find a buyer", he is reported to have said); he reconciled with his mother and released her from Sarum; he met Philip at Vézelay in July 1190 and the two armies marched south together. They wintered at Messina, where Richard intervened against the Sicilian king Tancred to recover the dowry of his widowed sister Joan, sacked the town of Messina to enforce the settlement, and was joined by his mother Eleanor bringing his bride Berengaria of Navarre, whom he was to marry in place of Philip's sister Alys to whom he had been betrothed since boyhood.

He sailed from Sicily in April 1191. A storm scattered his fleet; the ship with Joan and Berengaria was driven to Cyprus, where the self-styled emperor Isaac Komnenos seized the cargo and imprisoned the passengers. Richard followed in May, conquered the island in three weeks, married Berengaria at Limassol on 12 May, and sold Cyprus to the Templars and shortly afterwards to Guy de Lusignan, the displaced king of Jerusalem; for the next four centuries it was the Lusignan kingdom. He reached Acre in June 1191, where the crusader siege had been in progress for two years. The city surrendered on 12 July. After the Saracen garrison's ransom was not paid by Saladin within the appointed term, Richard ordered the execution of some two thousand seven hundred prisoners on the plain before Acre on 20 August. The act was within the conventions of the age and the agreed terms; the chroniclers of Saladin's camp recorded it with horror and answered it with reprisal massacres of their own.

The campaign that followed has been the model military narrative of the Latin Crusades. He marched the army south down the coast in disciplined ranks, with the fleet flanking him offshore; he defeated Saladin's harassing forces at the open battle of Arsuf on 7 September 1191; he refortified Jaffa as his base. Twice in the next year, in December 1191 and again in June 1192, he advanced within sight of Jerusalem, and twice the war council of the crusader princes — barons of the resident kingdom who knew that a captured Jerusalem held by a departing army could not be defended — decided against an assault. The principal local figure of the campaign, Conrad of Montferrat, was murdered at Tyre by Assassins in April 1192 in circumstances that were rumoured to involve Richard. After a final flurry of fighting at Jaffa in July, the king negotiated through Saladin's brother al-Adil the Treaty of Jaffa of 2 September 1192: a truce of three years, three months, three days; the coastal strip from Tyre to Jaffa retained by the Christians; the interior, with Jerusalem itself, kept by Saladin; and Christian pilgrims given safe passage to the Holy Sepulchre. Richard left Acre on 9 October 1192, having renewed in his last conferences with al-Adil the personal mutual respect that the Saracen chroniclers as well as the Latin ones record.

The return journey was the disaster of his life. The Mediterranean route was closed by Saracen and Italian enemies; he took the Adriatic and sailed up the eastern coast; storms drove him ashore near Aquileia in November; disguised as a Templar he tried to cross Austria overland and was recognised and arrested near Vienna on 21 December 1192 by men of Duke Leopold V, whom he had publicly insulted at Acre. Leopold sold him on to the Emperor Henry VI, the son of Frederick Barbarossa and the father of Frederick II, who held him in a series of imperial castles for fourteen months. The ransom set at Mainz in February 1193 was one hundred and fifty thousand marks of silver, the equivalent of more than two years of the English crown's revenue. His mother Eleanor, then over seventy, raised the sum from the English and Norman exchequer and the Church plate of both kingdoms by an extraordinary effort, brought it to Mainz herself, and Richard was released on 4 February 1194. Philip Augustus is reported to have written to John in England, "Look to yourself; the devil is loosed."

The five years that followed were a war of skirmishes and sieges with Philip Augustus across the Norman, Vexin, and Loire marches. Richard's military skill was undiminished by his captivity; he won most of the engagements; he raised on the lower Seine, between 1196 and 1198, the great concentric castle of Château Gaillard, the most sophisticated fortification of the European twelfth century. His finances were ruinous and his methods of raising money increasingly resented. In March 1199, at a minor siege of the small castle of Châlus-Chabrol in the Limousin, undertaken to enforce his rights to a hoard of Roman coins discovered by a local lord, he was struck in the shoulder by a crossbow bolt fired from the walls. The wound was bandaged carelessly and gangrene set in. He died on 6 April 1199, aged forty-one, in the arms of his mother who had ridden from Fontevraud to attend him. He had no legitimate children; the throne passed to his brother John, and the Angevin empire, which Richard had held intact, began at once to fall apart.

His body was buried at Fontevraud Abbey beside his father; his heart at Rouen Cathedral; his entrails at the abbey of Châlus where he died. The chroniclers of all parties — Saracen, French, German, English — agreed in placing him among the great soldiers of his century; the legend that took shape almost at once, of the lion-hearted king of the troubadour songs who had spoken with Saladin in the desert and lain in chains in a German tower, became one of the foundational stories of European chivalric literature.
