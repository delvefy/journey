Livia Drusilla

58 BC – AD 29 · wife of Augustus, mother of Tiberius, matriarch of the Julio-Claudians

Livia Drusilla, the third wife and lifelong consort of the emperor Augustus, was born in 58 BC into one of the great senatorial families of late-republican Rome and died in AD 29 at the age of eighty-six, having outlived her husband by fifteen years and dominated the courts of two reigns. She was the mother of one emperor, the grandmother of another, the great-grandmother of a third, and the great-great-grandmother of a fourth; the four emperors who succeeded Augustus — Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, and Nero — were all descended from her, and only from her, of the founding couple of the principate.

Her father, Marcus Livius Drusus Claudianus, was a Claudian by blood who had been adopted into the Livian family, an old plebeian-noble name; he killed himself at Philippi in 42 BC after the defeat of the senatorial cause by the young Octavian and Mark Antony. Livia by that point was sixteen and already married to her cousin Tiberius Claudius Nero, a senior senator and praetor twice her age. She had borne him one son, the future emperor Tiberius, by 42 BC; she was carrying the second, the future Drusus the Elder, in 39 BC, when she met Octavian at a dinner party and the two of them fell, by Suetonius's report, suddenly and entirely in love.

He was twenty-four, already married to Scribonia, who had just borne him his only daughter Julia, and the senior partner in a tense triumvirate that ruled the empire. He divorced Scribonia on the day of Julia's birth. Livia's husband, persuaded by what mixture of imperial pressure and private resignation can only be guessed, divorced her and gave her away in marriage to Octavian on the seventeenth of January 38 BC, three days after the birth of her younger son. The wedding was attended, in the conventions of the day, by both the bride's former husband and the new one. The old Roman aristocracy ground its teeth and accepted what it could not help.

The marriage lasted fifty-one years and was, by every contemporary account, a working partnership. She had no children by Augustus; a single live birth ended in the loss of the infant, and after that no more pregnancies came. She was therefore the only mother of an imperial heir to the principate Augustus would ever have, and through that fact, when his blood heirs died in succession — his nephew Marcellus, his right-hand Agrippa, the young grandsons Gaius and Lucius Caesar — the succession devolved inexorably on her son Tiberius. Her elder son she had given up at her remarriage to be raised in his father's house; she recovered him in adolescence, when Tiberius Claudius Nero died, and brought him into the imperial circle.

She lived in the city of Rome at the heart of the new regime for the fifty-one years of her marriage, in a series of houses on the Palatine of an austerity that Augustus himself made part of his public image. She wore wool spun in her own household, dressed her hair in a simple style that became the norm for senatorial women, and is associated with the patronage of a great deal of building in the city: the portico of Livia near the Subura, public granaries, the renovation of the temple of Concord. She managed properties and finances on a vast scale — Augustus had given her authority to act in her own right, an unusual concession for a Roman woman — and corresponded with provincial governors as an informal channel of the regime.

The hostile tradition, derived chiefly from Tacitus and from later imperial gossip retailed by Suetonius and Dio, made her a serial poisoner: of Augustus's blood heirs Marcellus, Gaius, and Lucius, in order to clear the way for her own son; of Augustus himself, in his last illness, by figs from a tree she had quietly painted with poison; of her grandson Germanicus, who threatened her favored grandson Drusus; and of various lesser obstacles in between. None of this is supported by anything contemporary, and modern historians treat it as the conventional libel of the long-lived, powerful woman who survived an emperor and acted with apparent freedom. What is historical is that the deaths of the more obvious heirs took place; that her son Tiberius eventually received the principate; and that Livia, on Augustus's death in AD 14, was formally written into his will as his adopted daughter and given the new name Julia Augusta.

Her relations with Tiberius, by then in his fifties, deteriorated as soon as he was emperor. She had been his mother and his political backer for half a century; she expected to be the public co-regent of his reign, and he found her insistent presence in his life intolerable. The historian Tacitus traces the long slow alienation: she pressed; he withdrew. By the time of his retirement to Capri in AD 26 he had absented himself from her almost entirely. When she died in AD 29, in her great age, he did not come to Rome for the funeral. He vetoed the senatorial decree of divine honors for her, and her will lay unexecuted for thirteen years until her grandson Claudius, becoming emperor, completed her elevation and her deification by the Senate in AD 42.

She is preserved in art chiefly in the form of a calm, matronly portrait type with a simple hairstyle and the heavy folds of a woolen palla, replicated in stone across the cities of the empire, and in the famous frescoes of her villa at Prima Porta outside Rome, with their painted garden full of birds and pomegranates. The villa was excavated in the nineteenth century; the frescoes are now in the Palazzo Massimo. Of her writing there is nothing. Of her household management, scattered fragments survive in legal references in the Digest. Of her political role, the most reliable witness is the Senate itself, which voted her, before her marriage to Augustus had reached its tenth year, the right of three children, an honor reserved for matrons of fertility and dignity.

She lived through the dictatorship of Caesar, the assassination of Caesar, the rise of Antony and Octavian, the proscriptions, the wars, the defeat of Antony, the founding of the principate, the deaths of Augustus's daughter and grandsons, the German disaster, the succession of her own son, and Tiberius's retreat to Capri. She is the only person who had been at the center of Roman political life from before the battle of Philippi to the Praetorian regency of Sejanus, sixty-seven years later. The four emperors of her line came after her.
