Albertus Magnus

c. 1200 – 1280, the Universal Doctor, German Dominican, master of Thomas Aquinas and the first Latin to assimilate the whole of Aristotle

Albert was born about 1200 at Lauingen on the Danube, in the Swabian county of Dillingen, the eldest son of a family of the lesser nobility serving the lords of Bollstädt. His birth year is uncertain — the chroniclers give dates between 1193 and 1206 — but the place and the social rank are secure, and explain something of his career: he grew up bilingual in the German and the academic Latin of the cathedral schools, with the means to travel for study and the obligation to leave the management of family estates to younger brothers. In adolescence he was sent south to study the liberal arts at Padua, then the most attractive of the Italian studia after Bologna, and it was there in 1223 that he encountered the Dominicans. The order had been founded only seven years earlier by Saint Dominic, and its master general Jordan of Saxony was making a circuit of the universities recruiting young scholars. Albert, against his uncle's furious opposition, took the habit at the Dominican convent of Padua and was sent for further training to Cologne.

The Dominican system of higher studies moved its most promising lectors through a sequence of houses, and over the next two decades Albert taught in turn at Cologne, Hildesheim, Freiburg, Regensburg, and Strasbourg. The pattern of his learning in these years is recoverable mainly from his own later citations: the standard theological textbook of the four books of Peter Lombard's Sentences, the Aristotelian logic in the Latin versions of Boethius, the early translations of Aristotle's natural philosophy that had begun to reach northern Europe from Toledo and Sicily, the works of the Arab philosophers Avicenna and Algazel, and the Jewish commentator Maimonides. About 1241 the order sent him to Paris, the highest stage of its formation, to incept as master of theology. He held one of the two Dominican chairs at the university from 1245 to 1248, completing the doctorate that the faculty had until lately been reluctant to grant the friars. Among his students in those years was the young Italian Thomas of Aquino, freshly arrived from Naples.

In 1248 the order's general chapter decided to elevate the studium of the German province at Cologne into a studium generale, a house of advanced theology of the rank of Paris and Oxford, and Albert was sent home to organise and lead it. He took Thomas with him; for the next four years Thomas was his closest pupil and his amanuensis, and the lecture courses Albert delivered at Cologne — on the works of the pseudo-Dionysius, on the Nicomachean Ethics, on the Lombard's Sentences — survive in the reportationes that Thomas took down. The Cologne school under Albert became the seedbed of German Dominican scholasticism for the next three generations, and the city itself a centre of the wider intellectual revival of the Rhineland that would, two generations later, produce Meister Eckhart, Heinrich Suso, and Johannes Tauler.

From 1254 to 1257 Albert served as provincial of the German Dominican province, an immense administrative charge that ran from Holland to Livonia and from Holstein to the Alps, requiring him to visit every house annually on foot in accordance with the rule. He continued at the same time to write, and to take on diplomatic tasks for the papacy. In 1256 he was summoned to Anagni to defend the mendicants against the secular masters of Paris, led by William of Saint-Amour, who had attacked the right of the friars to hold university chairs; Albert's De Unitate Intellectus on the Averroist question of the agent intellect dates from this stay at the curia. He resigned the provincialate in 1257 to return to teaching at Cologne, but in 1260 Pope Alexander IV named him bishop of Regensburg, a difficult Bavarian see whose finances and discipline were in disorder. Albert accepted reluctantly, set the diocese in order in two years, and resigned the mitre in 1262 to return to the order — an act unusual enough that the chroniclers commented on it. The remaining eighteen years of his life were spent mainly at Cologne and Strasbourg, with one further public commission: in 1263 Urban IV charged him with preaching the Crusade throughout the German lands.

The intellectual project that occupied Albert across all this activity was the production of a complete Latin paraphrase of Aristotle. By his own account, his aim was to make the whole corpus — logic, natural philosophy, psychology, metaphysics, ethics, politics — "intelligible to the Latins", and to do for Aristotle what Avicenna had done in Arabic: write a series of commentaries that would expound the master's doctrine, defend it against misreading, and supplement it where the experience of nature had advanced beyond what Aristotle himself had known. The series, when complete, ran to some forty treatises. The natural philosophical books, written largely in the 1250s and 1260s, include the De Animalibus, the De Vegetabilibus, and the De Mineralibus, in which Albert combined Aristotle with his own and his correspondents' first-hand observations on the plants, birds, fish, beasts, and minerals of Germany. He described the falcons of the Rhineland, the climate of the high Alps, the action of magnets, the growth of crystals in the silver mines of Goslar, and the breeding behaviour of bees, in passages that the modern history of biology recognises as the first serious empirical natural history written in medieval Latin. His judgments on alchemy were cautious but not dismissive; his works on minerals and on metallurgy were copied for three centuries by alchemical practitioners, who attached his name to a great deal that he did not write.

His theology was less original than Thomas Aquinas's but earlier and broader in range. The Summa de Creaturis of his Paris years and the unfinished Summa Theologiae of his old age were comprehensive surveys rather than systematic constructions; his commentaries on the pseudo-Dionysius transmitted to the Latin world a Neoplatonic strand — emanation, the hierarchy of being, the ascent of the mind through created things to God — that ran alongside the Aristotelianism and reappeared in Eckhart and the German mystical school. The doctrine for which Thomas became famous, that the soul is the substantial form of the body, Albert had taught before him; the project of harmonising Aristotle's philosophy of nature with Christian doctrine he laid down before Thomas took it up.

Thomas died in March 1274 on the road to the Council of Lyon. Albert, then almost the only survivor of the great Paris generation, made the journey from Cologne to Paris in 1277 — on foot, in his late seventies — when he learned that Bishop Stephen Tempier had included some of Thomas's positions in a list of condemned propositions. He defended his pupil before the masters, with what effect is disputed; the condemnations stood until they were quietly set aside after Thomas's canonisation. Albert returned to Cologne and lived three more years. He died there on 15 November 1280, in his convent, and was buried beneath the floor of the Dominican church, where his relics remain. He was beatified by Gregory XV in 1622, canonised by Pius XI in 1931, declared Doctor of the Church in the same act, and named in 1941 the patron of natural scientists. Of the medieval doctors only Albert is called both Saint and Great.
