Roger II of Sicily

1095 – 1154, the first king of Sicily, founder of the Norman kingdom of the south, builder of the Palatine Chapel

Roger was born at Mileto in Calabria on 22 December 1095, the youngest of the surviving sons of Roger I, the great count of Sicily and Calabria, and of his third wife Adelaide del Vasto, of the Aleramic family of Piedmont. His father was the youngest of the twelve sons of the petty Norman knight Tancred of Hauteville; the family, sailing south from Normandy in the 1030s in search of opportunities the older inheritance no longer offered, had taken the Lombard duchies of Apulia and Capua from the Lombards and Byzantines in the next decades, and from 1061 to 1091 had wrested Sicily from the divided Arab emirates of the island. Roger I died at Mileto on 22 June 1101 when his youngest son was five years old; the eldest son Simon succeeded as count but died at twelve in 1105; Roger II, the second remaining boy, inherited what was still the County of Sicily and Calabria at the age of nine, under the regency of his Lombard-Piedmontese mother.

Adelaide's regency lasted until 1112 and was an effective period of consolidation. She kept the local Norman magnates, who were already in 1101 the chief threat to the count's authority, in check by a careful policy of marriages and confirmations; she moved the seat of comital government from Mileto to Palermo, the old Arab capital, retained the Arabic chancery officials and tax-collectors who had served the previous regime, and added a Greek office to deal with the Sicilian Greek minority and the bilingual administration that the count's father had left in place. In 1113, her own political position uncertain as Roger grew into his teens, she married Baldwin I, king of Jerusalem, in a match that took her east at the head of an enormous Sicilian dowry; Baldwin's existing wife Arda was set aside without ecclesiastical sanction, the marriage was eventually annulled in 1117 as bigamous, and Adelaide returned to Sicily in poverty and disgrace. She died in 1118. Roger never forgave the kings of Jerusalem and the Latin states of the East for what he reckoned the humiliation of his mother, and refused all participation in the affairs of the Holy Land for the rest of his reign.

From 1112 to 1127 he ruled the county of Sicily and Calabria in his own person, accumulated the treasure that the conditions of insular agriculture, mineral wealth, and Mediterranean transit produced for a competent ruler, and watched the inheritance of his cousins on the mainland fall into disorder. In 1127 his first cousin William II, duke of Apulia and Calabria and grandson of the great Robert Guiscard, died without children and bequeathed the duchy to Roger. The legacy was contested by Pope Honorius II, who maintained the papal overlordship of Apulia and threatened excommunication; the Apulian baronage opposed the unification of their fragmented duchy with the Sicilian county; the cities — Bari, Salerno, Troia — preferred their own independence. Roger crossed the Strait of Messina in late 1127, defeated the assembled opposition at Brindisi the next year, and at the small town of Benevento in August 1128 received from Honorius the ducal investiture for which he had bargained. The duchy of Apulia and Calabria, which his uncle Robert Guiscard had built in the previous century, was now united with his Sicilian county.

The opportunity to convert this collection of titles into a kingdom came two years later. Honorius II died in February 1130. The cardinals split: the majority, led by Aimeric of the chancery, elected Cardinal Gregorio Papareschi as Innocent II; a substantial minority elected Cardinal Pietro Pierleoni, of the converted Jewish-Roman banking family, as Anacletus II. The schism that followed lasted eight years. Innocent had the support of Bernard of Clairvaux and most of the trans-Alpine kingdoms; Anacletus held Rome itself but needed an Italian protector against the imperial armies that were sure to come from the north on Innocent's side. Anacletus turned to Roger. By the bull Cunctis populis of 27 September 1130, the antipope conferred on him and his successors the dignity of king over Sicily, Calabria, Apulia, the principality of Capua, the honor of Naples, and the city of Salerno; on Christmas Day 1130, in the cathedral of Palermo, Roger was anointed and crowned king by the archbishop of Palermo in the presence of the Greek, Latin, and Arab notables of the kingdom. The kingdom of Sicily, the political unit of the south of Italy under a single crown that survived in one form or another into the nineteenth century, dates from that ceremony.

The eight years that followed were a long defensive war for the new title. The mainland barons rose almost annually; the German emperor Lothair III, allied with Innocent II and prompted by Bernard of Clairvaux, invaded southern Italy with an army in 1137 and overran most of the mainland kingdom; the Genoese and Pisan fleets, partisans of Innocent, raided the Sicilian and Apulian coasts; Naples was besieged by sea. Roger held Sicily itself and the coastal castles by patient defence, retook Salerno and the mainland one town at a time as Lothair retired across the Alps in late 1137, and was rescued from the schism by the death of Anacletus II in January 1138 and the consequent failure of the schismatic election. The decisive engagement of the war for papal recognition came on the Garigliano on 22 July 1139, where the troops of Roger's son Roger III of Apulia caught Innocent II's army in disorder near the bridge of Galluccio and captured the pope. The Treaty of Mignano three days later confirmed Roger's royal title, the kingdom in its present extent, and the apostolic legateship of the Sicilian Church that Urban II had granted to his father in 1098: in return Roger acknowledged Innocent as legitimate pope and held the kingdom of him as papal vassal.

With his kingdom secured, the last fifteen years of the reign were administrative and intellectual. At the Assizes of Ariano in 1140 he promulgated a written code of laws that combined Roman, Lombard, Norman, Greek, and Islamic provisions and was the first systematic legislation issued by a western king since the Carolingian capitularies. He built the Palatine Chapel within the Norman palace at Palermo, consecrated about 1140, with its conjunction of a Latin basilical plan, a Greek mosaic programme of the saints and the Pantocrator, and an Arab muqarnas ceiling on which Kufic Arabic religious inscriptions are interwoven with the Latin liturgy of the Sicilian court; the chapel is the surviving monument of his idea of the multicultural kingdom. At Cefalù on the northern coast he began in 1131 the cathedral that he intended for his own burial, with a Christ Pantocrator in the apse vault that is the masterpiece of Sicilian-Greek mosaic. At Palermo his admiral George of Antioch, the Syrian Greek Christian who governed the navy, founded the Martorana with its Greek-cross plan and its own programme of mosaics. The chancery functioned in Latin, Greek, and Arabic at the same time; the silver and gold coinage carried inscriptions in all three scripts.

To the same court he drew the leading scholars of the Latin and Greek and Arab Mediterranean. The Moroccan geographer Muhammad al-Idrisi, sent to Palermo from his patron in the Maghreb, spent fifteen years at Roger's court producing the planispheric world map and the prose geographical compendium that the Latin tradition called the Tabula Rogeriana: dedicated to the king and finished in the months before his death, it remained the most accurate atlas of the world in any language for three centuries. Henry Aristippus, archdeacon of Catania, translated Plato's Phaedo and Meno directly from Greek into Latin in this circle, the first translations of these dialogues in the medieval West. Eugenius of Palermo translated mathematical and astronomical works from Greek and Arabic. The Sicilian silk industry was founded in the 1140s with the Greek weavers brought back by George of Antioch from his raid on Thebes and Corinth during the Second Crusade; the Sicilian gold tarì coin circulated from Constantinople to Lübeck.

The kingdom he was leaving had cost him heavily in the family. His son Roger III, duke of Apulia and the captor of Innocent II, died of fever at Salerno in May 1148. His son Tancred died young. His son Alfonso, prince of Capua, died in 1144. His son Henry of Andria died in childhood. Of the five sons of his first wife Elvira of Castile, who had died in 1135 and to whom he had been entirely attached, only the fourth, William, survived him. He had married twice more for the sake of further sons — Sibylla of Burgundy in 1149, who died in childbirth the next year; Beatrix of Rethel in 1151, who was eight months pregnant when he himself died — and the posthumous daughter Constance of that last marriage would, forty years after his death, deliver his kingdom to the Hohenstaufen empire of Henry VI. Roger died at the palace of Palermo on 26 February 1154 at the age of fifty-eight. He was buried, by his own direction, in the cathedral of Palermo, in the porphyry sarcophagus that his masons had been preparing for the last twenty years. The line of kings from his hand reached its end with his great-grandson Frederick II, who in the next century closed the cathedral roof above the tomb that his great-grandfather had begun.
