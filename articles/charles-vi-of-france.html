Charles VI of France

1368 – 1422, King of France, called first the Beloved and then the Mad, in whose reign France divided itself and lost the war

Charles was born at the Hôtel Saint-Pol in Paris on 3 December 1368, the eldest surviving son of Charles V the Wise and Joanna of Bourbon. His father, the politically ablest of the Valois kings, had recovered France from the catastrophe of his own father John II's capture at Poitiers in 1356 and had won back, by patience, alliance, and the careful campaigns of his constable Bertrand du Guesclin, most of the territory England had taken in the first phase of the Hundred Years War. Charles VI inherited from his father, when he became king at the age of eleven on Charles V's death in September 1380, a kingdom larger and richer than it had been in fifty years, with a working treasury, a professional army, and a council of trained administrators.

The minority government was held by his four uncles — Philip the Bold of Burgundy, John of Berry, Louis of Anjou, and Louis of Bourbon — under whom the careful structures of his father's reign were dismantled. The treasury was emptied within five years to finance the family adventures: Louis of Anjou's expedition to claim the throne of Naples (where he died in 1384), Philip the Bold's accumulation of Flemish inheritances. The Parisian Maillotin revolt of 1382, the Flemish Cabochian rising, and the heavy taxation of those years all date from the uncles' tenure. In November 1388, at a council at Reims, Charles, just turned twenty, formally took the government into his own hands and recalled his father's old ministers, the so-called Marmousets. The intervening years were treated by contemporaries as a return to good government.

They lasted four years. On 5 August 1392, on the road through the forest of Le Mans, Charles, riding in chain mail in summer heat with a small armed party in pursuit of a man suspected of an attempt on the Constable Olivier de Clisson, was startled by a leper or peasant emerging from the trees who cried out a warning; a page dropped his lance against a steel helmet behind the king; the metallic clatter set Charles off. He drew his sword, attacked his own attendants, killed four of his own knights, and only stopped when his squire wrestled him to the ground. He fell unconscious. When he recovered, he did not recognise his wife or his uncles.

The attack of August 1392 was the first of what some modern psychiatrists have identified as a recurrent schizoaffective disorder and others as the porphyria of his Plantagenet descent; whichever it was, it returned for the rest of his life, with intervals of complete lucidity sometimes lasting months and intervals of severe disturbance lasting weeks. At his worst he believed himself made of glass and refused to be touched, sometimes refused to acknowledge that he was the king, sometimes did not recognise his wife or his children, sometimes wandered the corridors of the Hôtel Saint-Pol in soiled clothes, sometimes howled. During his lucid intervals he ruled normally and was, according to the writers who knew him in those periods, intelligent, courteous, and conscientious. The two states of the king ran in parallel for thirty years.

In January 1393, a few months after the first attack, a court masquerade at the Hôtel de Saint-Pol turned into the Bal des Ardents, the Ball of the Burning Men. Six men, including the king and his brother Louis of Orléans, danced disguised as wild men, the costumes of pitch and tow flax. A torch, perhaps carried by Louis, set fire to one of the dancers, and the fire spread through the linked costumes. Four of the dancers burned to death; the Duchess of Berry threw her thick skirts over the king and saved him; the queen Isabeau fainted; the city, hearing of the disaster, rose in mutters of judgement that almost overturned the government. The episode marked Isabeau and Louis's enemies, who saw in it confirmation that the king was being mishandled.

The political vacuum created by his illness was the central fact of the next quarter-century. His uncle Philip the Bold of Burgundy and his brother Louis of Orléans contested the regency openly through the 1390s. After Philip's death in 1404, the contest passed to Philip's son John the Fearless and continued. The murder of Louis of Orléans in November 1407 in a Paris street, on John's orders, opened the civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians that ran through the rest of the reign. Throughout these years, depending on which faction held Paris and the royal seal, the king was used by either side to issue ordinances against the other; documents issued in his name during his most disturbed periods committed France to opposite policies in successive months.

The English invasion of 1415 came on a kingdom that could not concentrate to meet it. At Agincourt on 25 October 1415 Henry V destroyed a largely Armagnac French army. Burgundian troops did not fight; Burgundian propagandists were not displeased. Henry returned in 1417 and methodically conquered Normandy: Caen fell in 1417, Rouen after a famine-siege in January 1419. By then John the Fearless held Paris and the king; the Dauphin Charles, expelled by the Burgundians, ruled south of the Loire. The murder of John the Fearless on the bridge of Montereau in September 1419 by men of the Dauphin's escort threw the Burgundians decisively into the English alliance.

The result was the Treaty of Troyes of 21 May 1420. The king, in a clearer interval, presided over its sealing; the queen Isabeau signed; the Burgundians endorsed. Henry V was married to Charles's daughter Catherine of Valois, made regent of France, and named heir of the French crown to the exclusion of the Dauphin, who was declared (under the polite legal cover of "great enormities") illegitimate. The Dauphin refused to recognise the treaty and continued to call himself king. Henry V then administered the parts of France he held, in the king's name, until his sudden death of dysentery on 31 August 1422 at Vincennes, two months before Charles VI's. Charles died at the Hôtel Saint-Pol on 21 October 1422 of natural causes at the age of fifty-three. He was buried at Saint-Denis with state ceremony attended, on the English side, by the regent Bedford, who alone of any of the company stood to receive the sword borne before the coffin. The infant Henry VI of England was proclaimed king of France in Paris that month; the Dauphin, in Bourges, was crowned by no one but proclaimed himself Charles VII.

Charles VI was a tragic figure rather than a wicked one. He had inherited a state his father had repaired and he had not the powers to keep. The illness from which he suffered was not understood, was certainly not cured, and was treated by his contemporaries as a partly supernatural visitation; even the most hostile of the Parisian and Burgundian sources retain an undercurrent of pity. He was buried by both sides of his sundered kingdom as their common king. The settlement that emerged from his reign — the Dauphin, eventually crowned, the English at last expelled by the campaigns of his son's middle age — was not what he had ordered, and the kingdom Joan of Arc led his son to Reims to inherit was not the one he had wanted to leave.
