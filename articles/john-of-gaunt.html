John of Gaunt

1340 – 1399, Duke of Lancaster, titular King of Castile, the wealthiest English subject of the fourteenth century, father of Henry IV and ancestor of every English monarch since

John was born at the abbey of St Bavo in Ghent in March 1340, the third surviving son of Edward III of England and Philippa of Hainault, during one of his father's long Flemish campaigns at the opening of the Hundred Years' War. The name by which English history has known him, "John of Gaunt", is no more than an anglicised version of his birthplace. He was created Earl of Richmond in his cradle; his elder brother Edward, the Black Prince, was already Prince of Wales; his second brother, Lionel of Antwerp, was Earl of Ulster by marriage; the boy John was, until he was sixteen, the youngest of the Plantagenet princes raised in his father's enormous English court.

His position was transformed in May 1359 by his marriage to his second cousin Blanche of Lancaster, the younger daughter and ultimately the sole heiress of Henry of Grosmont, the great soldier-Duke of Lancaster. On his father-in-law's death in 1361 and the death of Blanche's elder sister Matilda the following year, the whole Lancastrian inheritance — the largest single concentration of landed property in England outside the royal demesne — descended upon the twenty-one-year-old prince. He was created Duke of Lancaster in his own right by his father in 1362 and from that moment was the wealthiest subject in England, with an income from his estates that contemporaries reckoned at ten or twelve thousand pounds a year, several times that of the next greatest English peer.

He campaigned with his brother the Black Prince in France in the 1360s and in Spain in 1367, where the brothers won the battle of Nájera on the dauphin's behalf for Pedro the Cruel of Castile against his half-brother Henry of Trastámara. The campaign won the battle but lost its purpose; Pedro was murdered by Henry of Trastámara in March 1369, and Gaunt's second marriage in 1371 to Pedro's daughter Constance of Castile, made after Blanche's death of plague in 1368, gave him a paper claim to the Castilian crown that he pressed, with varying intensity, for the next sixteen years.

From the early 1370s, with the Black Prince in failing health from a wasting disease contracted in Spain and Edward III declining into old age, Gaunt became the effective head of the English government. The "Good Parliament" of 1376, with its impeachments of the king's mistress Alice Perrers and a clutch of court financiers, was substantially directed against him; he reversed its measures the following year and, in the brief interval between his father's death in June 1377 and the maturity of his ten-year-old nephew Richard II, was in everything but name the regent. He carried the orb at Richard's coronation; through the early years of the new reign he was the dominant figure in the council.

It was the period of his closest political connection to the Oxford theologian John Wycliffe, whom he had defended in 1377 against a heresy trial at St Paul's and continued to shelter as long as the Lollard programme of disendowment of the English church appeared to him politically useful; the connection lapsed, on his side, when Wycliffe's movement passed beyond what conservative magnates could countenance. The Peasants' Revolt of June 1381, when the men of Kent and Essex marched on London demanding the abolition of serfdom and the heads of the king's councillors, was directed in particular against him: the Savoy, his great palace on the Strand, was burnt to the ground in his absence, and the rebels at Smithfield demanded his execution by name. He was at the time on the Scottish border negotiating a truce; the king's councillors he had left behind were murdered, the Savoy was lost with all its furnishings, and Gaunt did not return to London until the revolt had been broken.

From 1386 to 1389 he was absent in Spain, pursuing his Castilian claim with a small army landed at Corunna. The campaign achieved no military objective; what it achieved was a negotiated settlement in 1387 by which his daughter Catherine of Lancaster married the heir to the Castilian throne, the future Henry III of Castile, and Gaunt and Constance renounced their own claims in return for an indemnity and a perpetual pension. The marriage made Gaunt grandfather of the Castilian royal line that ran through the fifteenth century to Isabella I; through it he is ancestor of, among others, Catherine of Aragon.

He returned to England in 1389 and resumed his role at his nephew's court as the senior royal counsellor, by then aged forty-nine and visibly elder statesman. After Constance's death in 1394 he married in January 1396 his long-time mistress Katherine Swynford, the daughter of a Hainault knight in his first wife's household and the governess of his children by Blanche; their four children, all adults already, were legitimated the following year by act of parliament and bull of Pope Boniface IX under the surname Beaufort. They became the ancestors of Cardinal Beaufort, of the Tudor royal line through Margaret Beaufort, and through the female lines of the Beaufort earls and dukes of Somerset of every later English monarch.

His relations with Richard II declined as the king's autocratic phase deepened in the 1390s. He held himself, with practised restraint, out of the climactic quarrel; he was at home when Richard exiled his eldest son Henry Bolingbroke in September 1398. Gaunt died at Leicester Castle on 3 February 1399, aged fifty-eight, leaving Bolingbroke as his heir. Richard II, with what proved to be a fatal misjudgement, declared Bolingbroke's exile permanent and confiscated the Lancastrian inheritance to the crown. The confiscation was the trigger of the revolution that followed within six months: Bolingbroke returned to Yorkshire in July 1399, gathered the northern lords, took the king prisoner, and was crowned in October as Henry IV. The Lancastrian dynasty was thus, in legal substance, founded on John of Gaunt's estates, which his son had returned to claim.

Through his three marriages Gaunt is ancestor to almost every European royal line that mattered after 1400: the English Lancastrians and, through Beaufort and Tudor, every monarch of England, Scotland, and the United Kingdom since 1399; the Castilian and Aragonese royal houses through his daughter Catherine; the Portuguese house through his daughter Philippa, who married João I of Portugal. He was buried with Blanche of Lancaster in old St Paul's; the tomb was destroyed in the Great Fire of 1666. His enormous wealth, his political dominance for thirty years, and his genetic legacy together make him the central English figure of the second half of the fourteenth century, even as he himself was rarely either king or commander in chief.
