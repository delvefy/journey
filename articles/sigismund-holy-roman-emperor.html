Sigismund, Holy Roman Emperor

1368 – 1437, King of Hungary, Bohemia, of the Romans, last emperor of the House of Luxembourg, convener of the Council of Constance

Sigismund was born on 15 February 1368 at Nuremberg, the second son of the Emperor Charles IV of Luxembourg and his fourth wife Elizabeth of Pomerania. The Luxembourg dynasty had taken the imperial crown three generations earlier with Henry VII and held it almost without interruption from 1346 with Charles IV. The boy Sigismund was the spare to his elder brother Wenceslaus, who succeeded his father as King of the Romans and King of Bohemia in 1378. The younger brother was provided for by his father by betrothal at six to Mary, the elder daughter of Louis I of Hungary and Poland, whose succession to Hungary the betrothal was meant to secure. Sigismund was raised partly in Buda, partly in Prague, partly at the imperial court at Aachen and Nuremberg; he spoke German, Czech, Latin, and Hungarian, with French and some Italian acquired later in life.

Mary became Queen of Hungary on her father's death in September 1382, ten years old, and Queen of Poland in name only, since the Polish nobles rejected Sigismund and Mary in favour of Mary's younger sister Hedwig, called Jadwiga, who married the Lithuanian Grand Duke Władysław Jagiełło and gave Poland its long Jagiellonian dynasty. Sigismund married Mary in October 1385 and was acknowledged co-ruler of Hungary in 1387. He was nineteen and ruled in his own right after Mary's death in 1395 from a riding accident in late pregnancy. He never remarried for love; his second wife Barbara of Cilli, whom he took in 1408 for the dowry, gave him only one child, the daughter Elizabeth who carried the Luxembourg claim to the Habsburgs by her marriage to the future Albert II.

His first great campaign was the Crusade of Nicopolis in 1396, the largest international Christian army assembled against the Ottomans before Lepanto. Bayezid I had advanced into the Balkans through the 1390s; Sigismund led an army of Hungarians and French volunteers under the duke of Burgundy's heir John, the future John the Fearless, to the Danube at Nicopolis in modern Bulgaria. The army was destroyed by Bayezid on 25 September 1396 in a defeat that contemporaries took as one of the worst of the century. Sigismund escaped down the Danube by boat. The Ottoman advance through the Balkans was checked, against all expectation, not by him but by Tamerlane, who broke Bayezid at Ankara in 1402 and gave the Ottoman expansion a generation's respite.

From 1402 to 1410 he was preoccupied by long civil wars in Hungary, in which his magnates revolted twice and he was briefly imprisoned by his own subjects. His brother Wenceslaus's reign in the Empire and in Bohemia had collapsed by 1400 into deposition by the imperial electors, and the imperial succession ran through Rupert of the Palatinate to a contested election in 1410 in which Sigismund was eventually elected King of the Romans. He was crowned king at Aachen in 1414 and, after long delay, emperor at Rome by Pope Eugenius IV in 1433, the last reigning emperor crowned at Rome.

The dominant work of his reign was the resolution of the Western Schism. He convened the Council of Constance in November 1414 on the authority of the schismatic pope John XXIII, and presided over its work for four and a half years. The council deposed John XXIII for simony and immorality, secured the resignation of the Roman pope Gregory XII, declared the Avignon line schismatic and deposed Benedict XIII, and elected Martin V at Constance in November 1417, ending the thirty-nine-year Schism that had begun in 1378. The council also condemned Wycliffe in two hundred and sixty articles posthumously and burned Jan Hus, whom Sigismund had brought to Constance under a safe-conduct that the council declared did not protect a convicted heretic. The breach of the safe-conduct — whether by Sigismund's connivance or by his having been outmanoeuvred — was the political opening of the Hussite Wars.

Wenceslaus IV died at Prague in August 1419 and Sigismund succeeded him as King of Bohemia. The Bohemian crown was, in effect, what he was unable to hold. The Hussite revolt — armed by the Bethlehem Chapel's preachers, organised by the Tábor encampments under Jan Žižka, equipped with the war-wagon tactics that defeated five papal crusades between 1420 and 1431 — kept him out of his kingdom for fourteen years. He was formally crowned at Prague Cathedral in 1420 in the brief Royalist occupation of the city; he was driven out within months and did not return until the Compactata of Basel in 1436, the year before his death, when the moderate Utraquist party accepted him on the basis of communion in both kinds. He died at Znojmo in southern Moravia on 9 December 1437 on his way to summer at Buda. He was buried at Várad in Hungary (modern Oradea), where his tomb was destroyed in the Ottoman wars of the sixteenth century.

His other long campaign, less visibly successful but politically more durable, was the resistance against the Ottomans on the southern frontier of his kingdoms. He recruited from Italy and Bohemia and Burgundy a series of soldiers of fortune, of whom John Hunyadi, the Hungarian voivode of Transylvania, would be the most lasting; the Hunyadi family carried the Ottoman war forward under Sigismund's grandchildren and produced, in Hunyadi's son Matthias Corvinus, the greatest of the late medieval kings of Hungary.

The dynasty he carried did not outlast him in the male line. His only legitimate daughter Elizabeth, by Barbara of Cilli, married in 1421 Albert V of Austria, of the Habsburg line, and made Albert his heir. Albert succeeded as King of Hungary, King of Bohemia, and King of the Romans in 1438 in close succession on his father-in-law's death; he himself died of dysentery on the Hungarian frontier the following year, leaving a posthumous son Ladislaus. The Habsburg inheritance of the Luxembourg crowns was the beginning of the long association of the Hungarian and Bohemian thrones with the Austrian house, which lasted with intermittent gaps until 1918.

Sigismund himself was a tall, ginger-bearded, polyglot prince of unusual reach and intermittent capacity, who held more crowns simultaneously than any other Holy Roman Emperor of the late Middle Ages and lost most of them to a movement his own safe-conduct had helped to ignite. The Order of the Dragon, which he founded at Buda in 1408 to consolidate his Hungarian and Slavonian magnates against Ottoman raiders, took in Vlad II Dracul of Wallachia, father of Vlad the Impaler, and gave the order's name to the second figure's family. The Council of Constance, in any case, was his lasting work: the reunified papacy of the fifteenth century, on the basis on which it later quarrelled with Luther, was his.
