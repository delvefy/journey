Petrarch

1304 – 1374, Francesco Petrarca, the first humanist, poet of Laura, author of the Canzoniere

Francesco Petrarca was born at Arezzo on 20 July 1304, the son of Pietro di Parenzo, called Petracco, a notary, and his wife Eletta Canigiani. The family were Florentine exiles: Petracco had been driven from Florence in 1302 as a White Guelf in the same banishment as Dante Alighieri, and the boy grew up first at Incisa in the Val d'Arno where his father had retreated, then from 1311 at Avignon, where the papal court had moved from Rome in 1309 and where his father had found employment as a notary in the curia. Francesco was educated at Carpentras under the grammarian Convenevole da Prato, then in law at Montpellier (1316 to 1320) and Bologna (1320 to 1326), the law training imposed by his father and detested, in his own later account, almost to the point of breakdown.

His father's death in 1326 freed him to abandon the law. He returned to Avignon, took minor orders to qualify for ecclesiastical benefices, and entered the household of Cardinal Giovanni Colonna, a position which provided him with a moderate income, the leisure to read, and access through the cardinal's library to a wider corpus of classical texts than was then available to almost any of his contemporaries. On the morning of 6 April 1327, Good Friday, in the church of Saint Claire at Avignon, he saw for the first time the woman he called Laura, of whom no other record survives but his poetry: he was twenty-two, she was nineteen and married, and the unrequited and largely literary love for her became the central theme of his vernacular verse for the next forty-one years.

His search for the manuscript remains of classical Latin literature, which would shape European humanism for two centuries, began in his twenties. At Liège in 1333 he discovered the lost Cicero oration Pro Archia, a defence of the value of poetry that he carried back to Italy and circulated; at Verona in 1345 he found in the cathedral library the complete text of Cicero's letters Ad Atticum, Ad Quintum fratrem, and Ad Brutum, manuscripts unknown to medieval scholarship and constituting the most important single textual discovery of the century. He began the practice of writing letters in self-conscious classical Latin and arranging them for eventual publication — the practice of the humanist epistolary collection — with which he was, in effect, the inventor of an entire genre.

He was crowned poet laureate at Rome on 8 April 1341, the first laureation since antiquity, on the Capitoline Hill in a ceremony staged by King Robert I of Naples who had examined him in person for three days in February. The laureation, more than any other public event of his career, defined his social position for the rest of his life: he was, from that day, the leading poet of his age, employed and welcomed at every court in Italy. He moved repeatedly through the next thirty years: between Avignon, where he resigned from Cardinal Colonna's service in 1347; the small Provençal village of Vaucluse, where he kept a house in retreat and wrote large portions of his Latin work; Milan, where he served the Visconti from 1353 to 1361; Venice, where the republic gave him a house on the Riva degli Schiavoni from 1362 to 1368 in return for the bequest of his library; and finally Arquà in the Euganean hills near Padua, where he settled in 1369 and lived the last five years of his life.

His vernacular work consists almost entirely of one book: the Canzoniere, also called the Rerum Vulgarium Fragmenta, the collection of 366 lyric poems in Italian (mostly sonnets, with a smaller number of canzoni, sestinas, ballads, and madrigals) that he composed, polished, and rearranged across forty years. The sonnets, written in the strict fourteen-line form perfected before him by Guittone of Arezzo and Dante but transformed under his hand into a vehicle of inwardness, gave the European vernacular tradition its dominant lyric form for the next three centuries. The Petrarchan sonnet sequence, in particular as a vehicle for unrequited love analysed across a structured progression of emotional states, was imitated in every European language: by Pierre de Ronsard, Joachim du Bellay, Lope de Vega, Garcilaso de la Vega, Camões, Wyatt, Surrey, Sidney, Spenser, Shakespeare. The sequence form he invented, with its scrupulous emotional inventory, its commemoration of the dead beloved, its meditation on the writer's own progress toward conversion, was the literary template for the early modern European interior life.

His Latin work, on which he spent the larger share of his time and his ambition, has lasted less well. The epic Africa, on Scipio Africanus, was the work for which he chiefly expected to be remembered; it was unfinished at his death and was not the success in his lifetime that he had hoped. The collected Latin letters, in the polished sequence he prepared as the Epistolae Familiares and Epistolae Seniles, were widely read by humanists into the seventeenth century. The autobiographical Latin prose Secretum, a dialogue between the poet and St Augustine on the conflict between worldly ambition and salvation, is one of the most-read documents of fourteenth-century interior life.

His friendship with Boccaccio, formed at Florence in 1350 and sustained by correspondence until Petrarch's death twenty-four years later, was the formative relationship of early Italian humanism. He sheltered the Greek scholar Leontius Pilatus, with Boccaccio, in the long project that gave western Europe the first complete Latin translation of Homer. He encouraged Boccaccio, against the latter's reluctance, to learn Greek and to redirect his work from the vernacular to Latin; he persuaded the Florentine government in 1373 to establish the first university lectureship in Dante.

Laura is said by Petrarch's own annotation, in a Virgil manuscript still preserved at the Ambrosiana in Milan, to have died of plague at Avignon on 6 April 1348 — twenty-one years to the day after he first saw her. He marked her death with a long and structurally distinct second part of the Canzoniere, the In Morte di Madonna Laura, and never claimed to have recovered. The plague that killed her also killed his patron and friend Cardinal Colonna, and the next four years of his correspondence are dominated by the experience of mortality and the search for a public good in a depopulated Europe.

He died at Arquà during the night of 18 to 19 July 1374, the day before his seventieth birthday, found in the morning by his servant in his study, his head fallen forward on the open page of a Virgil. He was buried in the parish church of Arquà; his tomb of red marble, raised by his son-in-law Francescuolo da Brossano, stands in the square outside the church and is the place of his pilgrimage cult. The library he had bequeathed to Venice, partly dispersed by the time his executors made the inventory, included Greek and Latin manuscripts that became the foundation of the Marciana.

Petrarch's claim to be "the first modern man" was made by Jacob Burckhardt in the nineteenth century and has been argued over since. The historical Petrarch was an early-fourteenth-century Tuscan in minor orders whose principal contributions were three: the methodical recovery of classical Latin literature from cathedral and monastic libraries, the invention of the humanist letter and the humanist autobiography, and the reshaping of vernacular love poetry from the troubadour mode into the introspective sequence that became the standard European lyric form. The three together amounted to the founding act of European humanism.
