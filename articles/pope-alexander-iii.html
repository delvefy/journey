Pope Alexander III

c. 1105 – 1181, Rolando Bandinelli, master of the Third Lateran Council, the pope who broke Frederick Barbarossa

Rolando Bandinelli was born about 1105 at Siena, of a family of the urban patriciate of that city; the name Bandinelli is given by the surviving documents only from the fifteenth century, but the Sienese origin is secure. He was educated, by the report of his contemporaries, in the schools of Bologna, where the new science of canon law was being assembled out of Justinian's Roman code and the conciliar tradition. A scholarly tradition that long identified him with the "Magister Rolandus" who wrote a Summa and a book of Sententiae in the 1150s has been largely abandoned by twentieth-century specialists, who now distinguish two Rolands of similar background; what is certain is that Bandinelli's later legislative work shows a canonist's training at a high level. In 1150 Pope Eugenius III named him cardinal-deacon of Saints Cosmas and Damian; in 1151 he was promoted cardinal-priest of San Marco; in 1153 Pope Anastasius IV made him chancellor of the Roman Church, an office which under his successor Adrian IV he made the executive heart of the curia.

Adrian IV — Nicholas Breakspear, the only English pope — reigned from 1154 to 1159, and the political horizon of his pontificate, and of Rolando's chancellorship, was the imperial coronation in 1155 of the new German king Frederick of Hohenstaufen, called Barbarossa, and the rapidly hardening conflict between the resurgent imperial party and the papacy and the northern Italian communes. The famous incident of the Diet of Besançon in October 1157 belongs to this period and to Rolando personally. Adrian had written to Frederick a letter complaining of an attack on a returning legate, in which the pope used the word beneficia to describe the favours he had conferred on the emperor; Rolando, deputed to read the letter aloud to the diet, used a translation that Frederick and his German princes understood as suggesting that the imperial dignity itself was a beneficium, a fief held of the pope. The protest was furious; the chancellor was nearly killed on the spot by the count palatine Otto of Wittelsbach and only saved by Frederick's personal intervention; the German embassy left in indignation. Whether the word had been so intended remains disputed; the episode was the public marker of the breach.

Adrian died at Anagni on 1 September 1159. The cardinals went into conclave in St Peter's a week later; the majority elected Rolando, who took the name Alexander III. A minority of perhaps a quarter of the cardinals, including the imperial party in the Sacred College, elected Cardinal Octaviano of Monticelli, who took the name Victor IV. The double election produced eighteen years of schism. Frederick Barbarossa took up Victor's cause and at the council of Pavia in February 1160, convoked at the imperial summons against the pope's prohibition, recognised him as legitimate. Alexander excommunicated emperor and antipope from the cathedral of Anagni in the same month, fled to France in 1162 when the imperial pressure on central Italy became insupportable, and made his court for three years first at Montpellier, then at Sens, where he was sheltered by Louis VII and where he kept his exile court alongside that of the other great exile of the decade, the archbishop of Canterbury Thomas Becket, whom he protected and supported. Victor IV died at Lucca in 1164; Frederick at once procured the election of a successor, Paschal III, and on Paschal's death in 1168 a third, Calixtus III, prolonging the schism for as long as imperial arms could maintain it.

The struggle was decided in the Italian plain. Alexander, returning from France in 1165, made his alliance with the rising league of the Lombard communes — Cremona, Mantua, Bergamo, Brescia, Verona, Padua, Vicenza, Treviso, and the towns of the Marca, joined on 1 March 1167 in the formal Lombard League at the abbey of Pontida. In 1168 the league founded a new fortified city on the lower Tanaro at the strategic angle of the routes from Genoa and Pavia, and named it Alessandria, in the pope's honour: a stone monument to the alliance and a check on the imperial supply line. Frederick's fourth Italian expedition of 1166–1168 occupied Rome and forced Alexander to flee disguised as a pilgrim to Benevento; the imperial army was destroyed not by arms but by plague, and Frederick himself escaped only by riding north through hostile country. The decisive engagement came on his fifth expedition. On 29 May 1176, at the bridge of Legnano on the Olona, the foot soldiers of the Lombard League around the carroccio of Milan held the imperial cavalry until a counter-attack of the Milanese knights, the so-called Company of Death, broke the German line; Frederick was unhorsed, lost his standard, and his army scattered. The political consequence was the Treaty of Anagni in November 1176 and, the next summer, the public reconciliation at Venice.

The Treaty of Venice of 24 July 1177 was conducted by Doge Sebastiano Ziani in the basilica of San Marco. Alexander sat on a throne in the porch of the basilica; Frederick approached on foot, knelt before him, and kissed his foot. The pope raised him with the kiss of peace. The schism was over; the antipope Calixtus III submitted the next year and was made a minor curial official. Alexander returned to Rome. In the basilica of the Lateran, in March 1179, he convoked and presided over the Third Lateran Council, attended by some three hundred bishops — the largest western council since Nicaea II four centuries before. The canons of the council reorganised the practice of the church on a series of long-pending questions. The first canon required a two-thirds majority of the cardinals for a valid papal election, a rule which removed the basis for any future schism of the kind just survived and which, modified at later councils, governs papal elections to this day. The other canons regulated simony, episcopal residence, the obligation of schools, the rights of universities, the limits of pluralities and prebends, the position of lepers, the trade with the Saracens, the Cathar heretics of Languedoc (the first general anathema against them, with the call for armed action that the next century would convert into the Albigensian Crusade), and the relations of Christians, Jews, and Muslims in the Latin kingdoms. With Gratian's Decretum of forty years before, and the great body of papal decretals Alexander himself had issued, the council established the framework of the western canon law that the school of Bologna would teach for the next four centuries.

Other affairs of the pontificate, less central but politically heavy, ran through these years. In the Becket dispute, Alexander supported the archbishop of Canterbury through the six years of exile, gave him refuge in his own court at Sens, threatened Henry II of England with interdict, and on 21 February 1173, twenty-six months after the murder, canonised Thomas of Canterbury at Segni, the swiftest canonisation of a recent martyr in the medieval Church. With the kings of France, England, Norway, Hungary, Bohemia, Castile, Aragon, and Sicily he maintained the relations of arbitration and confirmation that the Becket case made central; the surviving registers of his decretals, more than seven hundred of which were later collected into the Liber Extra by his successor Gregory IX, are the principal monument of twelfth-century canon law in action. With the Greek emperor Manuel I Komnenos he negotiated, fruitlessly in the end, on a possible reunion of the churches against the common pressure of the Hohenstaufen and the Seljuks. With the Waldensian preachers of Lyon he was initially conciliatory, granting them permission to preach in 1179; the move toward formal condemnation came only under his successors.

The Roman commune, never reconciled to his rule, drove him out of the city a final time in 1179. He retired to Civita Castellana, where he died on 30 August 1181, aged about seventy-six, having reigned twenty-two years. The Roman populace stoned the cortege that brought his body back to be buried at the Lateran; the burial was performed at night. The pontificate that had outlasted three antipopes, the Lombard wars, and the great quarrels of the western kings, had been the longest of the twelfth century and the one in which the medieval papacy assumed something close to its mature form.
