Henry V of England

1386 – 1422, King of England, victor of Agincourt, conqueror of Normandy, heir of France by the Treaty of Troyes

Henry was born at the gatehouse of Monmouth Castle on the Welsh Marches, probably on 16 September 1386, the eldest son of Henry Bolingbroke, Earl of Derby, and Mary de Bohun. His father, son of John of Gaunt and grandson of Edward III, stood at the head of the Lancastrian branch of the Plantagenet house; his mother, who died when Henry was eight, was a coheiress of the great Bohun earldoms of Hereford and Essex. He grew up partly in his mother's English manors, partly in his uncle Henry Beaufort's care, and partly — from the age of ten — in the household of King Richard II, who took the boy with him to Ireland in the summer of 1399 and there knighted him, partly perhaps as a courtesy and partly as a hostage against the conduct of his father, then in exile in France.

The hostage was unnecessary, but the situation was not. While Richard was in Ireland, Bolingbroke landed at Ravenspur in Yorkshire, raised the Lancastrian retainers, deposed Richard, and on 30 September 1399 was acclaimed king as Henry IV. The twelve-year-old Henry was returned from Ireland and created Prince of Wales, Duke of Cornwall, Earl of Chester, and (a year later) Duke of Aquitaine and of Lancaster. The legitimacy of the new dynasty was disputed; Richard II died, possibly murdered, at Pontefract in February 1400; and the prince's early career was a series of campaigns against the consequences. From 1400 he was titular commander of the operations against Owain Glyndŵr's Welsh rebellion, which lasted until about 1409 and which he had to learn to fight at fourteen. At the battle of Shrewsbury on 21 July 1403 against the Percy rebels he was struck in the cheek by an arrow that lodged in the bone; the royal surgeon John Bradmore designed a special pair of pincers to extract it and treated the wound with mead and probably honey. He recovered to fight in the rear-guard later in the day and bore the scar for the rest of his life.

The relationship between father and son in Henry's last princely years became politically strained. The prince and his close circle, including his Beaufort uncles, pressed for vigorous war in France while the king, increasingly ill, preferred a defensive policy. There was talk in 1410 of forcing the king's abdication; the king dismissed the prince from the royal council in late 1411. The reconciliation, dramatised in Shakespeare's account, was real and was finally complete when Henry IV died at the Jerusalem Chamber at Westminster on 20 March 1413. Henry V was crowned by Archbishop Arundel at Westminster Abbey on 9 April in a sudden snowstorm.

His domestic policy combined religious orthodoxy with conscious distancing from his father's faction politics. He suppressed the Lollard rising of Sir John Oldcastle in 1414 with severity, hanging and burning the captured Lollards. He had Richard II's body removed from King's Langley and re-interred with state honours at Westminster Abbey, an act of dynastic piety intended to draw a line under his father's usurpation. He cultivated friends across the political class with deliberate evenhandedness. He brought into his administration the able clerks of the chancery who would manage his French war — Henry Beaufort, John, Duke of Bedford, the brilliant chancery secretary Bishop Thomas Beaufort.

The French war was the project of his reign. The civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians in France had been running since the murder of Louis of Orléans in 1407; King Charles VI was intermittently mad; the French royal house was divided. Henry V revived the claim to the French throne that Edward III had asserted in 1340 and that had been largely dormant since the Treaty of Brétigny of 1360. He demanded, in the negotiations of 1414, the cession of all Plantagenet possessions in France according to Brétigny and the marriage of Charles VI's daughter Catherine of Valois; he was offered a smaller settlement and refused it.

The campaign began in August 1415 when he landed at the mouth of the Seine with about ten thousand men. He took Harfleur after a five-week siege but lost perhaps a third of his army to dysentery. With the remaining force he marched north toward Calais, pursued and outpaced and outflanked by a much larger French army that finally blocked his crossing of the Somme. On 25 October 1415, Saint Crispin's Day, at the village of Agincourt in Artois, on a narrow muddy field between woods, the French chivalry attacked his exhausted army. The English archers, protected by sharpened stakes, broke the French cavalry charge; the dismounted French men-at-arms struggling forward in heavy armour through mud were funnelled together, killed in their thousands, or taken prisoner. The English losses were a few hundred; the French lost upward of six thousand including the constable, two dukes, five counts, and ninety greater barons. Some of the French prisoners were killed on Henry's order during the battle, when it seemed that the rearmost French line might rally; the killing of prisoners has divided his biographers ever since, and was scarcely an act unique to him in the warfare of the time.

Agincourt was a single battle, won; the conquest of Normandy was the work of the campaigns of 1417 to 1419. Henry returned with a larger army in August 1417, took Caen by storm, Falaise by negotiation, Cherbourg by siege; Rouen, the capital of the duchy, fell after a six-month siege that ended in January 1419 with the city's population dying of famine in the moat. Pontoise fell in 1419, putting English troops within sight of Paris. The murder of John the Fearless at Montereau in September 1419 threw the Burgundians into open English alliance, and the Treaty of Troyes followed on 21 May 1420. Henry was married to Catherine of Valois at Troyes on 2 June, was made regent of France during the life of the king he had defeated and heir to the throne thereafter, and rode in triumph into Paris that December beside the king he had displaced.

The fortune of war turned against him in the months that followed. The Dauphin Charles, repudiating Troyes, raised a fresh army south of the Loire. At Baugé in Anjou, on Easter eve 1421, a Scottish-French force defeated and killed the Duke of Clarence, Henry's eldest surviving brother. Henry returned to France with reinforcements in June 1421 and resumed the slow reduction of the Loire towns. At the siege of Meaux in the winter of 1421–1422 he contracted dysentery. He was carried by litter to the royal château of Vincennes outside Paris and died there on 31 August 1422, two months short of his thirty-sixth birthday. His son Henry VI was nine months old.

Charles VI died seven weeks later. By the terms of Troyes the infant Henry VI was now king of France as well as of England. The English regency in France under his brother John, Duke of Bedford, held northern France together for thirteen years and lost the rest after Joan of Arc's relief of Orléans in 1429 and Burgundy's defection at Arras in 1435. The settlement Henry V had imposed could not survive him without him.

His reputation in his lifetime, fixed by the chronicler Tito Livio Frulovisi who wrote his life for Bedford in the 1430s, was of a king who united knightly piety, personal courage, and meticulous administrative attention in a way that medieval England remembered for a long time. Shakespeare's two plays at the end of the sixteenth century made him a national icon; modern historians have refined the icon without dismantling it. The campaign that took six years had been planned with a financial and logistical care England would not match again until the seventeenth century. He had been an unusually focused king; he had also been killed by the campaign he had pursued so single-mindedly, in his thirty-sixth year, leaving his country and his French inheritance to a child.
